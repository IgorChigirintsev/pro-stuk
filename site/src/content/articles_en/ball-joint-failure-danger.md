A ball joint is a small pivot that connects the suspension arm to the
hub carrier. The weight of the car passes through it into the arm, and at
the same time it lets the wheel steer. Of all the wear items in a
suspension, this is one of the few whose failure turns not into an
inconvenience but into an emergency. Here is what happens physically and
which noises warn you in advance.

## What happens at the moment it lets go

The stud pulls out of its housing, or out of its seat in the hub carrier.
At that moment the wheel is no longer held from below by the arm and
folds into the arch — the top leans out, the bottom tucks in. The car
pulls sharply towards that wheel, the steering has no effect on it, and
the body drops onto the arch liner, the brake disc or the arm itself.

At parking speed it usually ends with a bent wing and a tow truck. On a
main road the consequences are heavier: the car darts sideways in a
fraction of a second, you brake on three wheels, and a suspension
component lying on the tarmac acts like an anchor. On top of that the
brake hose almost always tears or the driveshaft is damaged — so the bill
is never for one part.

## The signals that arrive first

| What you notice | What it means |
|---|---|
| A torn boot on the joint | The grease has gone, water and grit are inside |
| A dull click over a kerb | A gap has opened between the stud and the housing |
| A creak when steering while parked | Dry friction in a joint with no grease |
| Play in the wheel, top to bottom | Wear has reached a mechanically obvious stage |
| Pulling to one side under braking | The wheel no longer holds its set angle |

A sudden failure with no warning does happen, but it is rare and usually
follows a heavy impact — a deep pothole at speed, a kerb, a collision. In
the overwhelming majority of cases the joint knocks over bumps for weeks
first. The problem is not that the part hides its condition; it is the
habit of blaming the knock on the state of the roads.

## How to check it

The simplest check is to lift the wheel off the ground and rock it in the
vertical plane, holding it at the top and the bottom. Play in that
direction points to the ball joint or the wheel bearing; the character of
the noise, and whether the wheel hums while driving, tells them apart.

The second check is visual: on a lift you look at the boot and at any
grease thrown around it. The third uses a pry bar under load, and that is
a mechanic's job.

A sensible interval for an inspection is every 15,000–20,000 kilometres,
or at every tyre change, when the wheels are off anyway. It costs nothing
if you ask for it while the car is already up.

## How urgent it is, and how the repair is decided

Noises in a car differ enormously in how long they can wait. A whining
release bearing, for example, tolerates months. A ball joint is not on
that list. With obvious play, the right tactic is not to drive long
distances under your own power and not to leave it longer than a few
days. To compare the character of the impact with other suspension
noises, see the symptom page
[knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/).

Two things decide the shape of the repair. First, whether the joint is
separate or built into the arm: on many modern cars it is not sold on its
own, and the whole arm is replaced — a more expensive part, but a faster
job. Second, what the failure has already touched: a joint that has been
knocking for months puts extra load on the wheel bearing and the tie rod
end, so a decent workshop checks both while it is in there rather than
selling you a second visit later.

One step is not optional afterwards: wheel alignment. Replacing a ball
joint changes the geometry, and skipping the alignment shows up as
uneven, rapid tyre wear within a few thousand kilometres.
