You switch the engine off, get out — and an even hiss comes from under the
bonnet, as though air were escaping somewhere. A sound from a stopped
engine is always more unsettling than usual: the familiar hum is gone, and
against that silence you hear what would simply drown while driving. Yet
some of those hisses are entirely normal, and some are the first and only
symptom of a leak that will become an overheat within a month. Here is how
to separate them.

## What hisses on a healthy car

After shutdown the engine stays hot while active circulation stops. Local
temperatures in the cylinder head even rise for a minute or two — that is
called heat soak. The cooling system answers with rising pressure, and the
valve in the expansion tank cap releases the excess vapour. The result is
a short hiss in one or two bursts, most often in hot weather or after a
climb.

The second normal source is the cooling exhaust. The red-hot manifold and
downpipe contract, the metal ticks and clicks, and condensate on hot
sections evaporates with a hiss. Both sounds share one thing: they fade
within two to five minutes and leave neither puddles nor a smell. Gurgling
after shutdown is a related story, covered in
[gurgling in the engine after shutdown](/en/articles/gurgling-in-engine-after-shutdown/).

## When hissing means a leak

Be alert if the sound is even, lasts longer than five to seven minutes and
does not weaken. That kind of hissing usually comes from a jet of liquid
or gas escaping under pressure through a small opening.

The three most common variants. A micro-crack in a cooling hose: coolant
is forced onto the hot block and evaporates instantly, hence the sweetish
smell and whitish deposits around the clamp. A failing gasket or seal —
the fluid seeps slowly, but a trace is visible. And an air leak through a
vacuum hose or the intake manifold gasket — there the hissing is audible
with the engine running too, and the idle wanders, as covered in
[signs of an intake air leak](/en/articles/signs-of-an-intake-air-leak/).

## Localising the source

| What you hear and see | Likely source | How urgent |
|---|---|---|
| A short release, once or twice, then silence | Expansion tank cap valve | Normal |
| Ticking and clicking low down | The cooling exhaust | Normal |
| An even hiss for more than 5 minutes, sweet smell | A coolant leak | Days |
| White deposits and a stain at a hose clamp | Hose, clamp | Days |
| Hissing with the engine running too, wandering idle | An air leak | Weeks |
| Steam from under the bonnet, the gauge rising | The system has failed | Immediately |

The practical order of checks is simple. An hour after the drive, with the
engine cold, open the bonnet and inspect the hoses at the clamps and
bends — that is where the first cracks appear. Check the level in the
expansion tank and remember it; if it is lower a week later there is a
leak, even without puddles under the car. Look at the cap itself while you
are there: a cracked rubber seal is a cheap consumable.

## What to do about it

If the hissing is short, the level holds and there is no smell, nothing
needs doing — just check the tank every couple of weeks. If the level is
falling, do not put the diagnosis off: the cooling system is pressure
tested, that is, pumped up with a hand pump so you can see where fluid
appears. It is a quick procedure and it shows the leak point at once.

Two things are worth asking about at that point. Whether the cap was
tested along with the system — a cap whose valve no longer holds pressure
produces exactly this hissing and a slowly falling level while every hose
is perfect, and it is the cheapest part in the whole system. And whether
the water pump was inspected while the system was under pressure, since a
weeping pump seal shows itself best that way; its signs are in
[signs of a failing water pump](/en/articles/signs-of-a-failing-water-pump/).

The general catalogue of sounds that appear in the first seconds of engine
running is on the symptom page
[squeal on startup](/en/symptoms/squeal-on-startup/).
