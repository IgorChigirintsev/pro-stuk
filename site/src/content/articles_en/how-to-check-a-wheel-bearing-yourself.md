The wheel bearing is the part the wheel turns on, and one of the few in a
car you can diagnose fairly confidently yourself. You need an empty road, a
jack and fifteen minutes. Below are five checks in the order that is
convenient to do them: first on the move, then parked. Together they
produce a diagnosis you can take to a workshop with confidence.

## Three tests on the move

1. **The speed test.** Find a level stretch and accelerate smoothly,
   noting the speed where the hum appears. A wheel bearing usually speaks
   up at 40–60 km/h and grows steadily with speed, monotonously and
   without pauses.
2. **The lane-change test.** At 60–80 km/h, ease left, then right. In a
   curve the body leans outwards and the loaded outer wheel hums louder:
   growth when you go left points at the right bearing, going right at the
   left.
3. **The coasting test.** Accelerate to the speed where the hum is clear
   and coast in neutral. If the hum is unchanged, the source rotates with
   the wheels and the engine and gearbox are not involved.

A fourth check for good measure is the surface test. Drive on different
tarmac: tyre noise changes with the surface, a bearing hums the same
everywhere. That step eliminates the most common false alarm. The full
sound portrait is on the symptom page
[wheel bearing hum](/en/symptoms/wheel-bearing-hum/).

## Two checks while parked

The first is raising the wheel. Jack it up, put the car securely on a
support and spin the wheel by hand. A healthy bearing turns silently and
smoothly; a worn one gives a rustle, crunch or hum that is clearly audible
if you put a hand on the spring. Then take the wheel at 12 and 6 o'clock
and rock it: noticeable movement means play. The detailed procedure is in
[checking hub play with the wheel raised](/en/articles/checking-wheel-bearing-play-with-the-wheel-raised/).

The second is temperature. Right after a calm drive with little braking,
carefully bring a palm near each hub. A difference between one wheel and
the others is a warning sign: the bearing is heating itself through
friction.

Two caveats for both checks. The wheel must be raised safely — on a stand
or with the spare under the sill, not on a jack alone. And always compare
with the neighbouring wheels on the same axle: there are no absolute
values here, only the difference between the suspect wheel and the other
three.

## What gets confused with a wheel bearing

| Source | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|
| Tyre noise | Changes with the surface, the same in both curves |
| Outer CV joint | Not a hum but crunching in a tight turn under power |
| Transmission | The sound follows drive: different under power and coasting |
| Power steering pump | Hums when turning the wheel even while parked |
| A sticking caliper | Hum with heat, but the wheel turns against resistance |

It is worth checking the neighbours too: play found when rocking a wheel
does not always belong to the bearing — the steering and the arms produce
similar sensations. How to separate them is described in
[checking track rod ends for play](/en/articles/checking-track-rod-ends-for-play/).

## What to do with the result

If the speed test, the lane-change test and a rustle from the raised wheel
all agree, a wheel bearing is highly likely and you can book with
confidence.

Two things decide the job. Whether your car uses a bearing pressed into
the existing hub or a complete hub assembly replaced as a unit — that
governs the price and the time, and on many modern cars the ABS sensor
lives inside that assembly. And the hub nut torque: over- or
under-tightening destroys a new bearing within thousands of kilometres, so
a torque wrench rather than an impact gun is what you want to see.

There is no need to replace both bearings “just in case”. But asking for
the other side to be checked makes sense: if it is also near the end, you
can plan the expense rather than meet it in a month.

If the checks contradict each other — which happens often — record the hum
in the Stuk app during the lane-change test. The app matches the recording
with your answers and shows the likely causes with percentages, including
which side to start the inspection from.
