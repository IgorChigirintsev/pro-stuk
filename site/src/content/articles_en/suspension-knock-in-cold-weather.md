With the first real frosts cars start to sound different: a suspension
that was quiet all summer suddenly knocks on every bump and then settles
down once warm. Where winters run to −30 °C and summers to +40 °C, this is
a classic of the genre. The good news is that in most cases a "frost"
knock is not a breakdown but an early signal. Here is what knocks in the
cold, where the line between normal and faulty runs, and what to do about
it.

## Why frost brings knocks out

A suspension is half made of rubber: bushes, anti-roll bar bushes, strut
top mounts, mountings. Below freezing the rubber stiffens — it loses
elasticity and stops gripping the metal as tightly as it does in summer. A
small amount of play that warm rubber damped invisibly turns into an
audible knock in frost.

The second factor is the oil in the dampers. In hard frost it thickens,
the damper valves work against resistance, and for the first few minutes
the suspension feels stiff and wooden. The third is plastic: wheel arch
liners, the bumper and covers ring and rattle in the cold where they were
silent in summer.

## What knocks most often in the cold

| Sound | Likely source | How urgent |
|---|---|---|
| Dull knock for the first minutes, then quieter | Thick damper oil, stiffened rubber | Watch it |
| Knock on fine ripples for the whole drive | Anti-roll bar links and bushes | Diagnosis within 1–2 weeks |
| Knock with a creak | Dry anti-roll bar bushes | Cheap to fix |
| Hard impacts, body float | Worn damper | Do not postpone |
| Plastic rattle | Arch liners, undertray, bumper | Tighten the fasteners |

A separate winter story is a knock that appeared after recent work: in
frost the first thing to answer is any under-torqued fastener. If the
suspension has recently been apart, start with
[a knock after suspension repair](/en/articles/knock-after-suspension-repair/),
and if the struts were changed, with
[a knock after replacing shock absorbers](/en/articles/knock-after-replacing-shock-absorbers/).

## Normal or faulty: how to tell

The time guide first. If the knock disappears completely within 10–15
minutes of driving and does not return until the next cold start, it is
most likely the physics of cold rubber and oil — within normal, or the
very earliest stage of wear. If the knock merely gets quieter but is
audible for the whole drive, the part is worn and the frost has simply
made it louder; the problem will not go away in summer.

The second guide is the character of the sound. An even, soft knock on
ripples is the anti-roll bar's handwriting and is not a safety matter. But
hard single impacts felt through the body, a knock with the car pulling to
one side, or play in the steering do not depend on temperature and need
checking without seasonal discounts. The general breakdown is on the
symptom page
[knocking in the suspension](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-suspension/), and
the fine-bump case in
[front suspension knock over small bumps](/en/articles/front-suspension-knock-over-small-bumps/).

## How the repair is decided

The criterion is which of the two pictures above you are in, and one
practical point decides it: come to the diagnosis on a car that has been
driven, and describe the temperature dependence to the mechanic straight
away. Play that is only audible when cold may not show itself in a warm
workshop bay — and then an honest "no play found" from the diagnostician
disproves nothing. If you can, book the first appointment of the morning.

Two things mechanics do not always say. Dry anti-roll bar bushes are worth
trying before replacement: cleaning and greasing them removes a
creak-and-knock combination for a season and costs almost nothing. And
where dampers are replaced, the bump stops and boots go with them — in
winter a crumbled bump stop is exactly what turns a hard impact into a
knock through the body,
[signs of worn bump stops](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-bump-stops/).

## Is it worth swapping rubber parts for polyurethane

Polyurethane bushes last longer than rubber and are less afraid of road
salt, but in hard frost they stiffen more noticeably: the car becomes
harsher and more prone to creaking. For cold winters the sensible
compromise is quality rubber from a known manufacturer: it is quieter and
behaves more predictably across temperature swings. Polyurethane earns its
place where the factory parts do not survive a single season.
