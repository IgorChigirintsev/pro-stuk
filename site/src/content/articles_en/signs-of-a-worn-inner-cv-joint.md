Everyone knows the outer CV joint: it starts crunching in turns, so it
gets replaced. The inner joint on the same driveshaft is far less known,
though it fails just as often. The problem is that it sounds completely
different and so regularly collects somebody else's diagnosis: its
symptoms are blamed on the suspension, the engine mounts or unbalanced
wheels. Here is how it actually behaves.

## Where it sits and why it sounds different

A front driveshaft has two joints. The outer one, at the hub, works at
large steering angles: its job is to turn a wheel that follows the
steering. The inner one, at the gearbox, barely handles angles at all but
compensates for suspension travel and for the change in shaft length as
the suspension compresses and extends — in design it is usually a tripod,
three rollers on trunnions sliding in the housing's tracks.

Hence the difference in symptoms. The outer joint is loaded by steering,
so it rattles in a manoeuvre. The inner one is loaded by drive and
suspension travel, so it speaks up under acceleration and over bumps,
while the steering barely affects it.

## What the wear sounds like

| Sign | When it appears |
|---|---|
| A dull knock when pulling away | The first throttle after a stop |
| A knock accelerating over bumps | Potholes and undulations under power |
| Vibration in the body and floor | Accelerating and climbing, grows under load |
| A click on selecting reverse | The load direction changes in the joint |
| Crunching in a turn | That is the outer joint, not the inner one |
| A knock over bumps with no throttle | More likely the suspension than the driveshaft |

The last two rows are the typical false trails. If the knock is heard over
bumps while coasting with no drive, the driveshaft is probably not
involved. The general catalogue of sounds tied specifically to drive is on
the symptom page
[knocking when accelerating](/en/symptoms/knocking-when-accelerating/), and
play in the shaft's splines has its own breakdown in
[signs of worn driveshaft splines](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-driveshaft-splines/).

## Checking it yourself

The inspection takes a few minutes and needs no lift, though one helps:

1. **The boot.** Find the inner joint at the gearbox and inspect the
   rubber cover. A tear, cracks, grease over the subframe and splatter on
   the body nearby are practically a verdict.
2. **The acceleration test.** On a level stretch accelerate from 20 to 60
   km/h with normal throttle, then lift off. A knock and vibration only
   under drive point at the driveshaft.
3. **The reverse test.** Move off slowly backwards, then forwards. A
   single click as the direction changes means play in the joint or the
   splines.
4. **Play by hand.** With the wheel raised, rock the shaft at the gearbox
   along and across its axis: noticeable free movement confirms wear.

How fast the problem develops is almost entirely decided by the boot:
while it is intact the joint covers hundreds of thousands of kilometres;
after it tears the count is in weeks. That scenario is set out in
[a torn CV boot: how far can you drive](/en/articles/torn-cv-boot-how-far-can-you-drive/),
and the outer joint's own signs in
[signs of a worn outer CV joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-outer-cv-joint/).

## How the repair is decided and how urgent it is

The first question is the joint or the complete shaft. On a high-mileage
car with worn splines the whole shaft avoids a repeat visit; on a sound
shaft the joint alone is cheaper. The decision is made with the shaft off.

The second is the grease and the boot clamps — CV joints need their
specific molybdenum grease and properly crimped clamps, and both are
small details that decide whether the repair lasts.

The third is worth asking outright: whether the inner joint's housing was
inspected for scoring in its tracks, not just the rollers. A tripod
replaced into a scored housing starts vibrating again within months.

On urgency the inner joint behaves more gently than the outer one: it
rarely fails suddenly, more often knocking and vibrating for a long time.
But driveshaft vibration wears its neighbours — engine mounts, the gearbox
bearing — and a drawn-out wait turns one line on the bill into three. Once
it starts clicking, the guidance in
[can you drive with a clicking CV joint](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-a-clicking-cv-joint/)
applies.
