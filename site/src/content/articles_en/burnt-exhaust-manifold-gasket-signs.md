The exhaust manifold collects gases from the cylinders and joins the
cylinder head through a gasket working in hard conditions: temperature
swings of hundreds of degrees, vibration, constant cycles of expansion and
contraction. When the gasket burns through, a gap appears at the joint and
exhaust gases escape straight into the engine bay. The sound is so
recognisable that the diagnosis is often made in the first minute — if you
know what to listen for.

## What it sounds like

The classic picture is **rapid dry ticking or chattering** from under the
bonnet, synchronous with engine revs and loudest in the first minutes
after a cold start. Many describe it as “the valves have started
knocking” — the resemblance is real, and that is the main cause of
mistakes.

The markers that give the manifold away:

- **A sharp reduction after warm-up.** The metal has expanded, the gap has
  nearly closed, the sound has faded or disappeared.
- **Growth under load.** Blip the throttle and the ticking becomes
  clearer, with hissing added.
- **Location.** The sound comes from the exhaust side rather than from the
  rocker cover; it is heard better from outside, by the wheel arch, than
  from above.
- **Accompanying signs.** A smell of exhaust in the bay and sometimes in
  the cabin, and a perceptible flow of hot air at the joint.

## What is looked at during an inspection

The theory can be confirmed almost without tools, on a cold engine.

1. **Black soot deposits** around the manifold-to-head joint or on the
   heat shield — the most reliable visible sign.
2. **Soot in a fan shape** spreading from one particular port: it shows
   which passage is leaking.
3. **The state of the studs and nuts**: a snapped or loose stud almost
   always accompanies a burnt gasket.
4. **A check with the engine running**: a gloved hand near the joint feels
   a pulsing jet of hot gas.
5. **Fuel trim data**: air drawn in through the exhaust distorts the
   oxygen sensor signal and the mixture can go rich.

## What it gets confused with

| Source | Character | What separates it |
|---|---|---|
| Manifold gasket | Ticking with hissing | Louder when cold, soot at the joint |
| Hydraulic lifters | Ticking from above | From under the rocker cover, no soot |
| Heat shield | Rattling, ringing | Reacts to bumps and vibration |
| A cracked manifold | The same ticking | Visible split, the sound does not go after warm-up |
| Catalytic converter | Rattles, chokes power | The sound comes from the canister under the floor |

The last row is a frequent neighbour: exhaust faults often come in pairs.
A pouring rustle from the converter is covered in
[a broken catalytic converter: the sound](/en/articles/broken-catalytic-converter-rattle/),
and rhythmic popping from the engine itself in
[signs of a burnt valve by ear](/en/articles/burnt-valve-signs-by-ear/).

## How the repair is decided

The repair is comparatively cheap if it is taken on in time. The main
difficulty is seized and snapped studs: drilling them out and restoring
the threads sometimes costs more than the gasket itself. That is the
question worth asking before the job — what happens if a stud snaps, and
whether the workshop can repair the thread in place or the head has to
come off.

Two more things. Whether the manifold's mating face will be checked for
flatness: a manifold that has warped will burn a new gasket within months,
and a workshop that skips that step is selling the job twice. And whether
the nuts will be torqued in the correct sequence — manifold fasteners are
tightened from the centre outwards in stages, and a gasket clamped
unevenly leaks from the first cold start.

There is also an indirect cost. Part of the exhaust escapes before the
oxygen sensor, and during valve overlap outside air is drawn into the
system. The sensor sees extra oxygen, the ECU decides the mixture is lean
and adds fuel — consumption rises and the converter works outside its
design range. So a jump of a litre or two in consumption alongside
cold-start ticking is not a coincidence but the same cause.

Do not put it off, for two reasons. A jet of red-hot gas works like a
blowtorch on everything nearby: hoses, wiring, plastic covers. And the gap
grows while the studs burn further, so a simple gasket change becomes a
drilling saga.

If the ticking appears only when cold and you are not sure it is not the
valves, record the first thirty seconds after a cold start in the Stuk
app, then the same fragment on a warm engine. The app compares the
recordings and shows the likely causes with percentages — this fault is
recognised especially well by the cold-versus-hot difference.
