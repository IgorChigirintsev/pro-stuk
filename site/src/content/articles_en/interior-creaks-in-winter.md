The first drive at minus twenty turns a familiar car into an unfamiliar
one: the interior answers every joint in the tarmac with a creak, the door
clicks as it closes, and the dashboard produces sounds it never made in
summer. In hard winters that is a seasonal norm for most cars. Here is
what actually changes in the cold, which sounds will clear by themselves,
and where the frost has merely revealed a real fault.

## What frost does to materials

The plastic the interior panels are made of contracts as it cools and
noticeably loses elasticity. Parts that sat tightly in their clips in
summer gain a few tenths of a millimetre of freedom in winter — and start
moving relative to each other over bumps. Where in summer there was a soft
springy contact, in winter there is dry hard contact, and that is a creak.

The same happens to rubber, only more so. Door and window seals stiffen,
lose their ability to absorb micro-impacts and begin creaking as the body
flexes. Suspension bushes — the rubber sleeves that damp vibration — are
stiffer in the cold too, and the arm passes more high-frequency sound into
the body.

A separate factor is moisture. Condensation that got into a panel joint in
autumn freezes in winter, and ice in a gap acts as a wedge: the parts move
apart, the fixing loosens, and after the first thaw a creak appears that
was not there before.

## Which sounds are seasonal and which are real

| Sound | Winter normal | Needs checking |
|---|---|---|
| Dashboard creak for the first 10 minutes | Yes, clears after warm-up | If it stays all day |
| Door seal creak | Yes, cured by treating the rubber | If the door has become loose |
| Crunching in the suspension pulling off a parking spot | Often frozen dirt | If it crunches when warm too |
| A knock from the engine at a cold start | Seconds after starting | If it lasts more than a minute |
| Brake grinding after a night outside | The first two or three stops | If it never clears |

A simple criterion: a winter sound disappears as things warm and does not
appear on a warm car. Anything that lives after warm-up has nothing to do
with the season. That applies especially to the engine bay: a cold knock
that does not clear within a minute is covered in
[can you drive with engine knock](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-engine-knock/),
with the sound examples on the symptom page
[engine knock when cold](/en/symptoms/engine-knock-when-cold/).

## What to do before the cold

The preparation takes half an hour and removes most winter creaks.

1. **Treat the rubber.** Wipe the door and boot seals dry and apply
   silicone grease. That saves you from creaking and from frozen doors.
2. **Grease the door mechanicals.** Locks, hinges and check straps are
   exactly what click and creak when opened in frost.
3. **Dry the cabin.** Wet mats in winter become a source of condensation
   that settles in the panel joints.
4. **Remove the loose items.** Coins in cubbies and unsecured luggage
   rattle louder in the cold: the suspension is stiffer and sound is
   sharper in cold air.
5. **Check the fixings.** Loose trim clips are silent in summer and not in
   winter; the dashboard is worth a look at the same time —
   [a rattling dashboard](/en/articles/rattling-dashboard/).

## What a winter check covers

Before the season, the sensible list is a suspension inspection on a lift,
attention to the anti-roll bar bushes — a frequent source of a winter
creak from below — and a look at the underbody fasteners and shields. If a
damper has started knocking in the cold, that is its own job.

Two things worth knowing. Rubber components that creak only in frost are
often serviceable rather than dead — greasing anti-roll bar bushes with
silicone before winter is a legitimate fix, not a bodge. And a damper that
knocks only in the cold is a different matter: cold oil is thicker, and a
damper whose valving is worn shows it first at low temperatures, so a
winter knock from a strut is worth taking seriously rather than waiting
for spring.

Telling a seasonal creak from a real one is easiest by comparison. Record
the sound in the Pro-Stuk app in the first minute after setting off, and
repeat the same recording half an hour later on a warm car. If the second
recording is clean, it is the frost; if the sound is still there, the app
suggests which components to check first. The over-bumps version of the
