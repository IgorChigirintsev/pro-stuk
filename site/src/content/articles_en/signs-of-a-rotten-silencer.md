A rotten silencer is the fault your neighbours notice first. The car gets
louder, takes on a low rumble under acceleration and starts popping on
lift-off. It is not an emergency in itself, but the sound is a sure sign
that a hole has appeared in the exhaust system, and the question is worth
closing before it turns into a smell of exhaust in the cabin.

## Why a silencer rots

The exhaust system lives in hard conditions: outside there is water, road
salt and stones; inside, hot gases and condensate. The condensate is the
most dangerous of them. On short town trips the system does not warm up
enough, moisture collects in the bottom of the box and does not evaporate,
and together with combustion products it forms an acidic environment that
eats the metal from inside. So silencers almost always rot from the bottom
and from the inside out — from outside the casing can look decent right up
to the end.

Winter use with salt on the roads speeds it up, as do a poorly chosen
cheap silencer made of thin metal and running a rich mixture that puts
unburnt fuel into the system. Mechanical damage is its own story: a kerb
or a speed hump can start a crack along a seam.

## What it sounds like and what else you notice

The set of signs is nearly always the same:

- **Growing loudness.** The car sounds deeper, especially when cold and
  under load. The sound comes not from the tailpipe but as if from under
  the floor.
- **Popping on lift-off.** Air is drawn in through the hole and the
  remaining fuel burns in the system, producing characteristic pops and
  bangs. That picture is covered on the symptom page
  [exhaust backfire](/en/symptoms/exhaust-backfire/).
- **Rattling at certain revs.** Detached internal baffles resonate.
- **A smell of exhaust in the cabin** — the most unpleasant sign,
  requiring an immediate response.
- **Soot marks** on the box or the pipe underneath: a dry black patch
  around the hole is visible even without a lift.

You can check by ear and by hand. With the engine running, have a helper
block the tailpipe firmly through thick cloth: the pressure in the system
rises and hissing becomes clearly audible at the hole, sometimes with a
wisp of smoke. Do this on a cold or barely warm engine and only briefly.

## Not to be confused with its neighbours

| Sign | Rotten silencer | Another source |
|---|---|---|
| Loudness grew gradually over months | Yes, typical | — |
| A roar appeared suddenly, with vibration at idle | Less often | [A torn flex pipe](/en/articles/signs-of-a-torn-exhaust-flex-pipe/) |
| Metallic rattle at 1,500–2,500 rpm | Possible | [A heat shield](/en/articles/finding-a-rattling-heat-shield/) |
| Power dropped along with the noise | No | A blocked catalytic converter |
| A pouring rustle of debris | No | [Broken converter ceramic](/en/articles/broken-catalytic-converter-rattle/) |

The key divider is power. A hole in the exhaust does not take it away —
the gases simply escape more easily. If the car has gone both loud and
slow, it probably has two separate problems, or one that is not in the
silencer. The catalogue of noises from below is on
[rattling under the car](/en/symptoms/rattling-under-car/).

## How the repair is decided

The diagnosis is simple: an inspection on a lift, usually free when you
book the repair. What follows depends on the state of the metal. A local
hole in a sound box is welded with a patch; a pipe is replaced in a
section. If the casing has rotted through and gives under a finger, a new
box is the sensible answer — otherwise a fresh hole appears next to the
weld within a couple of months.

Two things worth asking. Whether the rest of the system was tapped along
its length rather than only at the visible hole: a box that rotted from
condensate rarely did so in one spot, and welding one patch on a system
that is thin everywhere buys weeks. And whether the exhaust hangers are
sound — a sagging system rubs the body and loads the seams, so a new box
on old rubber rots ahead of its time.
