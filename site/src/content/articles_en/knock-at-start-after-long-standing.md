The car stood for a week at an airport car park or a month outside — and
at the first start the engine greets its owner with a knock that was not
there before. A few seconds later the sound goes and the engine runs as
though nothing happened. A familiar scenario and almost always
explicable: while the car stood, the oil left the top of the engine, and
for the first revolutions some parts run without lubrication. The only
question is whether your case falls within normal — and there are clear
markers for that.

## What happens while the car stands

While the engine runs, the oil pump drives oil through the galleries to
every friction pair. After it stops, the pump stops and the oil slowly
drains into the sump under gravity — out of the lifters, the chain
tensioner, the cam phasers, off the camshafts. Overnight not much goes; in
a week or two almost all of it does.

The first start after standing is a few revolutions run dry: the pump has
to fill the galleries and build pressure again. That is when the knock is
born. The longer the car stood, the colder it is outside and the worse the
oil and filter, the longer the pause before silence.

## Who knocks in the first seconds

| Source | Sound | Duration | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydraulic lifters | Ticking from the top | 2–10 seconds | Normal after standing |
| Chain tensioner and chain | Metallic rustle, clatter | 1–3 seconds | Borderline, watch it |
| Cam phasers | A dry rattle | 1–2 seconds | Borderline, watch it |
| Crankshaft bearings | Dull heavy knock from below | Persistent, growing | Alarm |
| Starter and its drive | Grinding, squeal before firing | Until the engine catches | A separate system |

Hydraulic lifters without oil tick, and they are the most common and most
harmless participant —
[hydraulic lifters ticking when cold](/en/articles/hydraulic-lifters-ticking-when-cold/).
A chain tensioner without pressure does not hold the chain taut and it
slaps the guides —
[signs of a failing timing chain tensioner](/en/articles/timing-chain-tensioner-failure-signs/).
Cam phasers rattle until they fill —
[cam phaser rattle when cold](/en/articles/cam-phaser-rattle-when-cold/).
All three go quiet as soon as the pressure rises.

A heavy dull knock from the bottom of the block that does not clear within
seconds is a different league: that is how crankshaft bearings knock, and
dry starts are the most destructive thing for them —
[signs of main bearing knock](/en/articles/signs-of-main-bearing-knock/).

## Normal or fault: three questions

The first question is **after how long a stand**. A knock after two weeks
parked is explicable; the same knock every morning is not, and that is a
reason to check the oil, the filter and the lifters.

The second is **how long it lasts**. Two or three seconds is acceptable;
ten or more, or a sound that only clears with warm-up, means looking
further.

The third is **its character**. Ticking and rustling from above are the
standard participants in a dry start; dull thumps from below are not. If
you are unsure of the terms, compare your sound with the recordings on the
symptom pages
[engine knock when cold](/en/symptoms/engine-knock-when-cold/) and
[squeal on startup](/en/symptoms/squeal-on-startup/) — whistling sounds
after standing usually come from the belt and its idlers, which is a
separate story.

## Reducing dry starts

The practical measures are simple and cheap. The main one is a quality oil
filter: its anti-drainback valve keeps oil in the galleries while the car
stands. On cheap filters that valve stiffens within months and the engine
starts dry every morning. An oil change with a good filter often removes
the morning ticking entirely — and if a knock appeared right after a
service, the related breakdown is in
[engine knock after an oil change](/en/articles/knock-after-an-oil-change/).

The second measure is seasonal viscosity: oil that is too thick in winter
takes painfully long to reach the top of the engine. The third is to let
the starter turn the engine for a couple of seconds without firing before
the first start after a long stand, so the pump begins building pressure
before combustion starts.

If the knock at start-up survives all of that, the next steps are
replacing the lifters or the timing kit — and both are decided by
measurement rather than by ear, so ask what was measured before agreeing
to either.
