A confusing situation: with the windows up the car seems perfectly
healthy, and the moment you drop the glass a clear knock, tick or grinding
appears in time with the speed. The first thought is that you imagined it.
In fact this is ordinary physics, and it is convenient to use as a tool:
the fact that a sound is only audible from outside already tells you
something about its source.

## Why glass swallows this particular sound

The body passes different frequencies into the cabin differently. Low
rumble and vibration travel through metal, the subframe and the mountings
— you hear them even in a sealed car. High-frequency sounds — clicks,
ringing, grinding, ticking — travel mainly through the air and are
absorbed beautifully by glass, seals and door sound deadening. An open
window removes that barrier and the sound arrives directly.

Hence the practical conclusion: if a fault is only audible through an open
window, it is almost certainly high-frequency and located outside — in a
wheel arch, in a brake mechanism, in the exhaust, under the floor.
Problems that sit on the body through mountings — the engine, a loaded
suspension, the gearbox — are usually audible with the window closed too.

## What most often sounds like this

| Source | Character of the sound |
|---|---|
| Pad wear indicator | Thin metallic squeal, stops when you press the brake |
| A stone in the brake mechanism | Grinding once per wheel revolution, often clears in a day or two |
| Outer CV joint | Dry clicking in a tight turn, exactly in time with the wheel |
| Brake disc shield | Bright ticking, bent metal touching the disc |
| Wheel bearing at a late stage | Rumbling and growling, louder in a bend |
| A loose exhaust component | Rattle from below at certain engine speeds |

Everything in that table has one thing in common: the source is in a wheel
arch or under the floor — the zone from which the sound goes straight into
an open window and barely reaches the cabin through the body. So the list
of suspects is immediately shorter than with the usual "something knocks
somewhere".

Some of those sounds are harmless, some are a direct warning. A metallic
squeal that stops when you press the brake pedal is the mechanical pad
wear indicator doing its job; it is covered in detail in
[telling brake pad wear by ear](/en/articles/brake-pad-wear-by-ear/). Dry
clicking in a turn is the outer CV joint —
[signs of a worn outer CV joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-outer-cv-joint/).

## How to use the open window for diagnosis

The trick is worth turning into a small protocol. Find a narrow street
with a wall or high fence along one side: the reflected sound comes back
into the open window and the source is twice as audible. Drive the stretch
at 20–40 km/h first with the left window down, then the right — the
difference between the runs points at the side.

Then add variations: the same stretch coasting, under light throttle, and
with a single touch of the brake pedal. A sound that disappears under
braking is the brake mechanism. A sound in time with wheel rotation and
independent of engine speed is the running gear. An even rumble that grows
with speed is usually a bearing; how it develops stage by stage is in
[what a failing wheel bearing sounds like](/en/articles/what-a-failing-wheel-bearing-sounds-like/).
If the sound comes from below and resembles rattling tin, it is worth
looking at the symptom page
[rattling under the car](/en/symptoms/rattling-under-car/) and at
[noise underneath: exhaust or undertray](/en/articles/noise-underneath-exhaust-or-undertray/).

## How urgent it is and how the repair is decided

The rule is simple: a sound audible only from outside is judged by type,
not by loudness. A pad indicator squeal means there is not much friction
material left before metal, and it should not be put off. CV joint
clicking will wait weeks, but not months. A rattling heat shield can
calmly wait for a scheduled visit.

What decides the work is what the sound turns out to be, and the checks
are cheap. A stone in the brake mechanism and a bent disc shield are
sorted with the wheel off in minutes — no parts at all. Exhaust and shield
fasteners are tightened or a rubber hanger is replaced. Only pads, a CV
joint or a bearing are genuinely a parts job.

Two things mechanics do not always say. First, if the pads are being
changed because of the indicator, ask about the state of the discs at the
same time — the indicator only warns about the friction material, and a
worn disc gives the same squeal back on new pads. Second, ask for the
caliper slide pins to be stripped and greased: a caliper that does not
release fully wears the pads to the indicator far sooner than the mileage
suggests, as covered in
[signs of seized caliper slide pins](/en/articles/seized-caliper-slide-pins-signs/).

Such a sound is almost impossible to convey to a mechanic in words: in the
workshop bay the car is standing, the windows are open, and the very
