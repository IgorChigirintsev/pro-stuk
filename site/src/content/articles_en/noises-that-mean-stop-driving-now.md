The overwhelming majority of car noises are an invitation to book a
workshop visit in a convenient week. Hums, squeaks and ticking build up
over months and leave you time. But there is a short list of signals with
no slack at all: here every further kilometre turns a repair of one part
into a repair of a whole system, and sometimes the question is no longer
money but safety. Here is how to tell those noises from the rest and what
to do when one appears.

## The common markers: suddenness and a low tone

Dangerous noises share two traits. The first is that they arrive abruptly,
with no warning: yesterday the car was quiet, today there is a new sound.
Gradual growth almost always means slow wear; a sudden arrival means a
part has already broken or come loose.

The second is timbre. High sounds — whistles, squeals, rustles — come from
light parts rubbing and are rarely critical. Low, dull ones — knocks,
thuds, grinding — mean massive loaded parts are contacting each other. If
both markers coincide, sudden and low, the sensible move is to stop and
work it out on the spot.

## Five signals where you switch the engine off

| Sound | What is happening | Why you must not drive on |
|---|---|---|
| Dull knock in time with the revs, growing under load | Worn crankshaft bearings | A spun bearing ruins the crank and the block |
| Sharp knock together with the oil pressure light | Oil starvation | Scoring within a few kilometres |
| Metal-on-metal grinding when braking | Pads worn down to the backing | The disc is destroyed, stopping distance grows |
| The brake pedal goes to the floor, hissing | A circuit has lost pressure | The car may not stop |
| Crunching and clattering under the floor on the move | An exhaust or undertray part has come loose | The part can go under the wheels |

Two more cases belong here without a noise in the usual sense: a sharp
bang with the car pulling to one side (a tyre blow-out) and a burning
smell with smoke from under the bonnet. Both mean stop immediately, for
the same reason — driving on multiplies the damage.

Engine knock deserves its own note: it has many variants, from harmless to
fatal, and the descriptions on the symptom page
[knocking in the engine](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-engine/) help tell them
apart. Braking noises are catalogued on
[grinding when braking](/en/symptoms/grinding-when-braking/).

## How to stop properly

Braking hard in the middle of a lane is more dangerous than the noise
itself. The order is simple:

1. **Put the hazard lights on** and look for somewhere to pull off.
2. **Shed speed smoothly**, without emergency braking, if the brakes still
   work. If the pedal has gone to the floor, use lower gears and the
   parking brake in measured amounts.
3. **Stop off the carriageway** and set out the warning triangle.
4. **Switch the engine off** if the noise was from the engine: with oil
   starvation every extra minute of running means more scoring.
5. **Look the car over**: a puddle underneath, smoke, a smell, a part
   hanging down.

Sometimes the inspection settles it at once — for instance the clattering
turns out to be a detached undertray, and securing it is enough.

## What next: a tow truck or under your own power

Make the decision on the cost of being wrong, not on the distance. Compare
a tow across town with what is at risk: an engine rebuild, a destroyed
brake disc, a ruined hub. When engine knock or brakes are involved the
arithmetic is almost always in favour of the tow truck.

There is one more argument that is easy to overlook: a car that broke down
and was driven on tells the workshop a different story. Scoring, a spun
bearing or a disc worn through by a backing plate all say plainly that the
car kept going after the signal, and that changes both the repair and any
warranty conversation.

If the noise turned out to be high, rattling and coming from inside the
cabin, there is no hurry: things like that are found calmly, with the car
parked and a helper pressing on trim panels.
