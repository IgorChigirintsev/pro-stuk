A universal joint is the propshaft's hinge, transmitting rotation from the
gearbox to the rear differential while letting the shaft flex as the
suspension works over bumps. Inside its four trunnions sit needle
bearings, packed with grease and closed by seals. The service life is
decent but finite: over time the grease washes out, the needles wear the
trunnions, and play appears. And almost always the sound comes first and
the vibration second. Here are the signs by stage, so you know where your
car is.

## Stage one: a clunk as the load changes

The earliest sign is a short metallic click or dull thump under the floor
at the moment torque changes direction: pulling away, lifting off sharply,
selecting reverse. The play is taken up and the trunnion strikes its cup.

A characteristic detail: the sound comes not from a wheel but from under
the centre of the car, roughly from the front seat area or a little
further back. It is a single dry event and does not repeat while driving
at a steady speed.

## Stage two: vibration at speed

As the play grows, the shaft loses its balance. Vibration appears from a
certain speed — often somewhere around 80–100 km/h — and grows beyond it.
It comes through the floor and the gear lever rather than the steering
wheel: an important divider from wheel problems.

| Sign | Universal joint | Wheel imbalance | Centre bearing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where it is felt | Floor, gear lever | Steering wheel | Floor, centrally |
| Speed dependence | Grows above 80 km/h | Peaks in a narrow band | Hum within a band |
| Reaction to throttle | Changes under load | None | Weak |
| Sound | Clicks, thumps | Silent | An even hum |
| After wheel balancing | Unchanged | Goes away | Unchanged |

If the vibration comes with an even hum, the neighbouring components are
worth checking; a rattle from underneath at speed has its own breakdown in
[rattling under the floor at speed](/en/articles/rattling-under-the-floor-at-speed/),
and separating driveline hum from a wheel bearing is covered in
[humming: gearbox or wheel bearing](/en/articles/humming-gearbox-or-wheel-bearing/).

## Stage three: rumbling, grinding and danger

Destroyed needles turn the joint into a source of constant grinding and
crunching, audible at any speed. Sometimes a squeak at low speed joins in —
a dry trunnion rubbing in its cup without grease. The vibration at this
stage becomes a shake the passengers feel.

This is the stage where nothing can be postponed. A ruined joint fails
quickly, and a propshaft that tears loose on the move can damage the
floor, the fuel line and the brake pipes. The catalogue of such sounds
from underneath is on the symptom page
[rattling under the car](/en/symptoms/rattling-under-car/).

## How to check it

The simple check is done on a lift or over a pit with the engine off and
the gearbox in neutral: take the shaft in both hands next to the joint and
try to twist and rock it. A healthy joint gives no perceptible free
movement. An audible click or noticeable play is a verdict. Also look for
traces of rusty dust around the trunnions: a sure sign of a bearing that
has run dry.

## How the repair is decided

The first question is whether the joint is serviceable on your shaft. If
it is, the joint alone is the cheapest route; if the shaft is a sealed
assembly, ask whether converting it is genuinely appropriate for your car
before agreeing.

The second is what else is checked while the shaft is off. Ask the
mechanic to look at both universal joints and the centre bearing at once:
dropping a shaft for one joint and coming back a month later for its
neighbour is a painful waste of money.

The third is balancing. Longer shafts are balanced after a joint is
replaced, and not every workshop offers it. On a two-piece shaft that step
is the difference between a repair that removes the vibration and one that
merely removes the clunk. Ask whether the shaft will be marked before
disassembly so the flanges go back in the same orientation — that alone
prevents most post-repair vibration.
