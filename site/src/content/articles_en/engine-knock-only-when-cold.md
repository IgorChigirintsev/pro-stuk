A knock audible for the first few minutes after starting that dissolves as
the engine warms is one of the most common engine stories. It is also the
most deceptive: the same "cold" knock hides both a harmless design
characteristic and the first signs of scoring in the bores. The key to
telling them apart is not the sound itself but its duration, its character
and its trend from month to month. Here is the whole list of causes in
order, from the innocent to the serious.

## The physics: where the knock goes

An engine is built from metals with different thermal expansion. While the
engine is cold the clearances between parts are at their largest: an
aluminium piston sits more loosely in an iron or steel bore, the valve
clearances are wider, the oil is thick and slow to reach the top of the
engine. As it warms the parts expand, the clearances converge on their
working values, the oil thins — and the sounds born of enlarged clearances
fall silent one after another.

Hence the rule: a knock strictly "when cold" is nearly always a story
about clearance. The only question is which clearance is enlarged — by
design or by wear.

## Causes by likelihood

| Source | Sound | How long it lasts | Danger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydraulic lifters | Ticking from above | Seconds, up to a couple of minutes | Low |
| Piston skirts (moderate wear) | Dull muttering | 1–5 minutes | Medium |
| Bore scoring | Diesel-like clatter, dull knock | Longer every month | High |
| Timing chain and tensioner | Rustle, rattle for 1–3 sec | The first seconds | Medium |
| Cam phasers | Rattle at start-up | 1–2 seconds | Medium |
| Exhaust manifold (cracked) | Puffing, blowing sound | Until warm-up | Low |

Hydraulic lifters — small hydraulic supports that take up the valve
clearance automatically — tick until they fill with oil, as set out in
[how to check hydraulic lifters](/en/articles/how-to-check-hydraulic-lifters/).
Cam phasers — the couplings that rotate the camshaft to change valve
timing — rattle for a second or two at start-up while there is no oil
pressure in them,
[cam phaser rattle when cold](/en/articles/cam-phaser-rattle-when-cold/). A
cracked exhaust manifold blows exhaust when cold and seals up as the metal
expands — the sound is closer to a puff than a knock,
[burnt exhaust manifold gasket signs](/en/articles/burnt-exhaust-manifold-gasket-signs/).

## The harmless scenarios

You can breathe out if the knock is short and has been stable for years.
The classic is a few seconds of lifter ticking after standing overnight:
the oil has drained to the sump and for the first revolutions they work
without a supply. The same goes for quiet muttering from the piston skirts
on engines for which an enlarged cold clearance is a design feature — some
engines clatter when cold from the factory. The signs of normal: the
duration does not grow, the sound does not coarsen, oil consumption does
not increase.

If the knock is only heard on the first start after a week standing, that
is its own story, connected with the oil draining down: it is covered in
[a knock at start after long standing](/en/articles/knock-at-start-after-long-standing/).
The general case of a knock that clears with temperature is set out in
[a knock that goes away with warm-up](/en/articles/knock-that-goes-away-with-warm-up/).

## The worrying scenarios

Three signs call for attention. The first is the trend: the cold knock
lasts longer and sounds coarser every month. The second is the company it
keeps: oil consumption, blue smoke on a throttle blip, or rough running
have joined it. The third is the character: not a soft tick from above but
a dull diesel-like clatter from the block. That trio is the typical
picture of worn piston skirts and scoring in the bores, described in
[signs of cylinder bore scoring](/en/articles/signs-of-cylinder-bore-scoring/).

The timing chain deserves a separate note: a rattle in the first one to
three seconds after starting means the tensioner cannot tension the chain
while there is no oil pressure. That is not fatal in itself, but a worn
chain can jump a tooth, with the risk of valves meeting pistons —
[signs of a stretched timing chain](/en/articles/signs-of-a-stretched-timing-chain/)
and
[timing chain tensioner failure signs](/en/articles/timing-chain-tensioner-failure-signs/).
You can compare your sound with typical recordings on the symptom pages
[engine knock when cold](/en/symptoms/engine-knock-when-cold/) and
[engine ticking](/en/symptoms/ticking-engine/).

## What to do and how the repair is decided

The first step is always the same and always cheap: check the oil level
and recall the date of the last change. Fresh oil of the correct viscosity
with a new filter resolves a noticeable share of "cold" ticking. If the
knock remains, the order of investigation runs from measurements to parts:
an oil pressure test, a scan tool read of the cam timing offset, and an
endoscope into the bores. Only those three answers separate lifters from a
chain and a chain from scoring — and each of them costs far less than the
part it rules out.

Two things worth asking before agreeing to a big job. Which measurement
led to the diagnosis, in numbers rather than impressions. And whether the
oil history explains the wear: a fresh set of lifters or a new chain fed
by the same neglected oil regime starts ticking again within a year.
Endoscope photographs of the bores are worth asking to see with your own
eyes — that is the one finding that decides between a repair and a rebuild.
