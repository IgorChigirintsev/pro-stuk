A knock felt through the sole of your foot as well as heard is more
unsettling than most car sounds: the brakes are the last system in which
you want to hear something new. Yet feedback through the pedal by no means
always signals a fault. It has three fundamentally different sources:
normal anti-lock braking, play inside the brake mechanism, and running
gear wear that simply shows itself under braking load. They are told apart
by when the sound appears.

## The three sources

The first is the ABS. Under hard braking or on ice the system starts
cycling the pressure in the circuit, and the pedal pulses underfoot with a
chatter like a ratchet. That is normal, and it does not happen during calm
town braking.

The second is play inside the brake mechanism. The pad sits in its guides
with a small clearance. When the retaining shims are worn and the seats
are hammered out, the pad moves a millimetre at the first contact with the
disc and strikes its stop. Hence a single click at the start of braking
and a second as the pedal is released — the detail is in
[a knock when braking: pads or caliper](/en/articles/braking-knock-pads-or-caliper/).

The third is the suspension and steering. Under braking the weight
transfers forward, the ball joints, control arm bushes and track rod ends
are loaded differently, and a worn part knocks at exactly that moment
while the brakes are healthy. The general picture of knocks under load is
on the symptom page
[knocking in the suspension](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-suspension/).

## Identifying your case

| When the knock appears | Likely source |
|---|---|
| Only under emergency braking, the pedal shakes rapidly | ABS at work, normal |
| A single click at the start and end of braking | Pad play in the caliper |
| A knock when braking over bumps | Suspension: links, bushes |
| A knock braking in reverse | Caliper slide pins, pads |
| Rhythmic pulsing, frequency rising with speed | Disc geometry, not a knock |
| A knock with the pedal sinking | Needs an urgent circuit check |

The last row is the only one that cannot be postponed. If the pedal has
gone soft, sinks or needs a second press, that is no longer about sound
but about pressure, and the sensible move is to stop driving.

## The order of checks

1. **Rule the ABS out.** A calm stop from 40 km/h on dry tarmac. If there
   is no pulsing and the knock was only in hard braking, it was the
   system.
2. **The reverse test.** Drive back a couple of metres and brake. Pad play
   in the guides gives a knock with the opposite sign: the pad moves the
   other way and the click is clearer.
3. **Separate brakes from suspension.** Braking on a level surface and
   braking on a bump. If the knock appears only along with suspension
   movement, the brake mechanism is not involved.
4. **Check the steering.** A knock with shaking in the wheel points at the
   disc and the hub; separating wheel noises is covered in
   [humming: wheel bearing or tyres](/en/articles/hum-wheel-bearing-or-tyres/),
   and judder itself in
   [brake judder when braking](/en/articles/brake-judder-when-braking/).
5. **Inspect the mechanism.** Take the wheel off and rock the pad by hand:
   noticeable movement and hammered stops are visible at once.

A separate situation is a knock on a recently serviced car: after new pads
some sounds count as normal, and a morning squeal after standing has its
own explanation in
[brake squeal after standing](/en/articles/brake-squeal-after-standing/).

## Urgency and how the repair is decided

The urgency depends on what is found. Pad play is a matter for the next
scheduled visit. A worn ball joint is a matter of a week: that joint holds
the wheel in the suspension geometry, and its failure takes the car out of
control —
[signs of a worn ball joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-ball-joint/). A
sinking pedal is a matter of today.

Two things worth asking at the workshop. That the caliper slide pins are
stripped and regreased rather than glanced at — that is what cures most
mechanism-side knocks, as covered in
[signs of seized caliper slide pins](/en/articles/seized-caliper-slide-pins-signs/).
And that the pad fitting kit — shims and springs — goes in with new pads,
because it is what stops the pad moving in the first place.

Telling by ear whether a caliper or a control arm is knocking is hard even
for an experienced driver: the sound arrives through the body and loses
its address. A recording made in the Stuk app during a series of stops,
together with answers to short questions, gives a list of likely causes
with percentages.
