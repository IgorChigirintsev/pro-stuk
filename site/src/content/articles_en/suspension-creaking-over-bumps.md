A creaking suspension is a sound drivers put up with for months: the car
rides fine, nothing knocks, but on every bump something groans or sings.
Most often that really is harmless — dry rubber creaking. But a creak has
serious sources too, and it matters not to miss them. Below: where a creak
comes from, how to find its source without a lift, when grease is enough
and when replacement is needed.

## Where a creak comes from

A creak is the sound of friction rather than impact, and that is what
fundamentally separates it from a knock: a knock speaks of play, a creak
of dry contact between two surfaces. In a suspension three pairs mainly
rub:

- **rubber on metal** — anti-roll bar bushes on the bar, control arm
  bushes, damper bushes;
- **metal on metal** — ball joints and track rod ends left without grease
  after a boot tore, coils of a sagged spring on its seat;
- **rubber on rubber** — bump stops and damper boots, occasionally body
  seals that creak “like the suspension”.

The good news: a creak is almost always audible without driving — rocking
the parked car is enough. That makes it far easier to locate than a knock,
which often only reproduces at a certain speed on certain bumps. So a
creak can usually be pinned down on your own driveway.

## The main sources

| Source | How it sounds | When it appears | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anti-roll bar bushes | Groaning, creaking over gentle bumps | After rain, a wash, in frost | Grease or replace |
| Control arm bushes | Creak on suspension compression | More often in frost, on older cars | Replace when worn |
| Ball joint, track rod end | Dry creak when steering and over bumps | Constantly, progressing | Replace without delay |
| Strut top mounts | Creak when steering while parked | When steering and over bumps | Diagnosis, replacement |
| Spring on its seat | Creak-scrape when rocked | Under load | Inspect: is a coil broken? |
| Boots and bump stops | Rubber squeak on compression | On larger bumps | Grease or replace, cheaply |

The most common find is the anti-roll bar bushes: two rubber blocks in
which the bar rotates on every roll and every bump. Grease leaves them,
dust works as an abrasive — and the rubber starts to sing. Their neighbour
by symptoms is the anti-roll bar links: worn joints usually knock, but at
an early stage, while the joint is dry, they creak too. Their signs are in
[signs of worn anti-roll bar links](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-anti-roll-bar-links/)
and the bushes' own in
[signs of worn anti-roll bar bushes](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-anti-roll-bar-bushes/).

Control arm bushes creak differently — duller, and usually on compression,
when deformed or torn rubber slips against its sleeve. Cracks, tears and
squeezed-out rubber are visible to the eye; the details are in
[signs of worn suspension bushes](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-suspension-bushes/).

## Creaking and the weather

A creak has a characteristic feature — it depends on the weather, and that
tells you a lot before any inspection:

- **after rain and washing** — water has removed grease and dust from the
  rubber bushes; that creak usually clears as things dry;
- **in frost** — the rubber stiffens and slips with a creak; as the
  suspension warms over a few kilometres the sound fades;
- **the creak disappears in hot weather** — soft rubber slides more
  quietly; that is not a recovery, and it returns in autumn.

A creak that ignores the weather and sounds the same at any temperature is
an argument for metallic friction: ball joints, track rod ends, a spring.
That source matters more and should be found faster.

## How to find the source

1. **Rock each corner.** With the car parked, push firmly down on each
   corner. If the creak reproduces, the source is in that corner; a helper
   listens at the wheel and under the arch.
2. **Steering while parked.** Turn the wheel lock to lock without moving:
   a creak from above under the wing suggests the strut top mounts, from
   below the ball joints or track rod ends.
3. **Test greasing.** Silicone grease on the anti-roll bar bushes is a
   legitimate diagnostic trick: the creak goes, the source is found.
4. **Inspect the boots.** A torn ball joint or track rod end boot combined
   with a creak is grounds for replacing the part without further
   experiments — see
   [signs of a worn ball joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-ball-joint/).

If a knock has appeared alongside the creak, start with the knock — play
matters more than dry rubber: the search route is on the symptom pages
[knocking in the suspension](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-suspension/) and
[knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/).

## Grease or replace

The general rule: rubber that creaks from dryness can be greased to buy
time; rubber that creaks because it is breaking up, and metal creaking
without grease, can only be replaced. Saving on cheap bushes is rarely
justified — a second workshop visit costs more than the parts.

One thing worth asking when bushes are replaced: that the bracket bolts
are torqued rather than guessed, and that new bushes match the bar
diameter exactly. An oversized bush is the classic reason a creak survives
the repair.

## A short checklist before the workshop

Five observations are enough to arrive prepared: which side creaks (left
or right, front or rear); whether the creak depends on the weather;
whether it appears when you rock the parked car by a wing; whether it
changes after the underside is washed; and whether it coincided with any
recent suspension work. Those five answers narrow the list of suspects
faster than half an hour on a lift, and a mechanic will thank you for the
specifics.
