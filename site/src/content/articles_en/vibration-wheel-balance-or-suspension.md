Vibration at speed is the complaint people most often take to a tyre shop,
and in roughly half of cases they need not have. Shake in the steering
wheel, the seat or through the whole body comes from unbalanced wheels,
from worn running gear parts and from brake disc run-out alike. One
symptom, different parts, and a fair amount of money goes on working
through the options. Simple logic sorts the versions out: each source has
its own link — to speed, to braking or to bumps.

## Imbalance: a narrow window of speeds

Unevenly distributed wheel mass creates a centrifugal force that grows
with the square of speed. Hence the characteristic picture: everything is
calm up to 60–70 km/h, then vibration appears, peaks around 90–110 km/h
and may retreat slightly above that. It does not depend on load, braking
or surface quality.

The location suggests the axle: front wheel imbalance goes mainly into the
steering wheel, rear into the seats and floor. Extra reasons to suspect
the wheels are a recent tyre change, an impact with a pothole, a lost
weight or a bulge in a sidewall. Balancing is cheap, so it is sensible to
do it first — but as a test of the theory, not as a universal cure.

## Suspension and steering: vibration with conditions

The running gear produces vibration differently. It is either linked to
bumps, or accompanied by knocks, or arrives together with a hum. Worn
bushes and ball joints let the wheel wander within its play, and at
certain speeds that play turns into shake in the wheel. Dead dampers do
not control the oscillation, and after every pothole the car floats. Worn
track rod ends add vagueness in a straight line to the vibration.

| Sign | Wheel imbalance | Suspension and steering | Brake discs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Link to speed | Narrow window 80–120 km/h | Vague, wider | Any, but under braking |
| Under braking | Unchanged | Unchanged | Appears or grows sharply |
| Over bumps | Unchanged | Grows, knocks join in | Unchanged |
| Companion sounds | None | Knocks, creaks, hum | Grinding, squealing |
| After balancing | Goes | Stays | Stays |

The braking row matters most: vibration exclusively when the pedal is
pressed almost always means run-out or uneven thickness in a brake disc
rather than the wheels or the suspension —
[brake judder when braking](/en/articles/brake-judder-when-braking/).

## The order of checks

1. The wheels first: balancing four wheels is inexpensive and closes the
   commonest cause.
2. If that did not help — the lift: play in the track rod ends, ball
   joints and bushes, the state of the dampers and wheel bearings. How to
   read that play is covered in detail in
   [a knock: steering rack or track rod ends](/en/articles/knock-steering-rack-or-track-rod-ends/)
   and
   [signs of a worn ball joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-ball-joint/).
3. Then the brakes: disc run-out is measured with a gauge and indirectly
   judged by pulsation in the pedal.
4. Separately, note whether a hum appeared before the vibration: the
   sequence "hum, then vibration" is described in
   [signs of a worn wheel bearing](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-wheel-bearing/).

The general picture of vibration and its companion sounds is on the
symptom page
[vibration at idle](/en/symptoms/vibration-at-idle/) — which also covers
the case where the shake appears while parked rather than on the move, and
its roots are in the engine.

## How the repair is decided

That order is not accidental: it runs from cheap to expensive and from
quick to slow, and each step either solves the problem or honestly rules
its cause out. Balancing is the diagnostic as much as the fix — a
balancing machine shows a bent rim or a bulge that no amount of weight
will cure.

Two things mechanics do not always say. After any work involving the tie
rods or the arms, an alignment is compulsory, and it belongs in the budget
from the start. And if a disc has run-out, ask whether it has enough
thickness left to machine — below the minimum stamped on the disc,
machining is not an option, and a shop that does it anyway is selling you
a disc that will warp again within months.

Vibration is hard to describe in words but records well: make short
recordings in the Stuk app at a steady speed, under braking and over
bumps. The app matches them with your answers to a few questions and shows
the likely causes with percentages, so at the workshop you name the
conditions rather than just the fact of the shake.
