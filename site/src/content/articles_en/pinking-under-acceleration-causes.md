Pinking — a bright metallic ticking under acceleration — has been
described in workshops for half a century as "the little pins ringing".
The phrase stuck, although the sound almost never has anything to do with
the gudgeon pins: in nine cases out of ten what rings is detonation,
explosive combustion of the fuel mixture. The good news is that the
commonest cause is cured by changing where you fill up. Here is what
actually rings, the full list of causes from common to rare, and how to
tell the harmless scenario from the genuinely worrying one.

## What actually rings

In normal combustion the flame spreads from the plug as an even front and
the piston receives smoothly rising pressure. In detonation part of the
mixture in the far corners of the chamber ignites on its own without
waiting for the flame — and a pressure wave strikes the metal. The
cylinder walls and the head answer with a high ringing, heard through the
bonnet as ticking or like ball bearings rattling in a tin.

The old name was inherited: on early engines a worn gudgeon pin joint also
rang, and drivers did not separate the two phenomena. Today the term lives
its own life, while the full breakdown of detonation itself — the
mechanics, the signs and the consequences — is in
[signs of engine detonation](/en/articles/signs-of-engine-detonation/). A
short description of the symptom is on the page
[pinging when accelerating](/en/symptoms/pinging-when-accelerating/).

## Causes, from common to rare

| Cause | Mechanism | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel below the required octane | The mixture self-ignites under compression | Fill up at a different station |
| Carbon deposits in the chambers | Smaller chamber volume, red-hot spots | Diagnosis, decarbonising |
| Worn or wrong spark plugs | An overheated plug ignites the mixture early | Replace to the correct heat range |
| A lean mixture | Burns hot, prone to detonation | Check injectors and air leaks |
| A badly set LPG system | The gas mixture is lean | Retune the gas equipment |
| Engine overheating | Hot walls provoke ignition | Check the cooling system |
| A faulty knock sensor | The electronics stop protecting the engine | Replace the sensor |

The octane rating is petrol's resistance to self-ignition under
compression: the higher an engine's compression ratio, the higher its
requirement. Putting lower-grade fuel into an engine designed for higher
is the shortest route to pinking, especially in summer heat and with a
loaded car. Cars converted to LPG add another item: pinking often appears
after a conversion or a re-tune, because a lean gas mixture burns hot and
detonates more readily than petrol.

## When it really is the gudgeon pins

A worn gudgeon pin is a rare scenario, but it exists. Three signs separate
it from detonation:

1. **The sound is independent of the fuel.** Changing stations and moving
   to a higher octane changes nothing.
2. **It rings outside load too.** A clear bright knock, often doubled, is
   heard on a throttle blip while parked and at a steady cruise, and its
   frequency is tied to the revs.
3. **The sound is stable or growing.** Detonation comes in episodes and
   dies on lift-off; a mechanical knock goes nowhere.

The full portrait is in
[gudgeon pin knock: how to identify it](/en/articles/gudgeon-pin-knock-how-to-identify/).
Wear in other piston group joints shows itself similarly — if the knock is
dull and grows with load, start with
[an engine knock under load](/en/articles/engine-knock-under-load/). And if
the sound only appears once the engine is fully warm, the breakdown in
[an engine knock when hot](/en/articles/engine-knock-when-hot/) helps: on
hot oil the clearances are heard best. The general symptom page is
[knocking when accelerating](/en/symptoms/knocking-when-accelerating/).

## Narrowing the cause yourself

- **The fuel test.** Put half a tank of fuel of the correct or higher
  octane in at a different station. The pinking has gone — question
  closed, and remember the bad station.
- **The load test.** Pinking only when labouring and on climbs is the
  handwriting of detonation. A knock at any revs, including idle, is a
  reason to think about mechanics.
- **A look at the plugs.** A whitish insulator with small black specks and
  eroded electrodes are traces of detonation; black velvety carbon means a
  rich mixture; oily deposits mean oil consumption. Plugs come out in ten
  minutes and tell you more about an engine than an hour of listening.
- **Watching the trend.** If after "curing" the pinking the car has become
  sluggish and thirsty, the electronics have most likely just retarded the
  ignition — the detonation has not gone, it has hidden.

Allow for the season too: pinking shows up more often in summer. Hot
intake air and a hot combustion chamber lower the detonation threshold, so
a car that stayed silent all winter on a given fuel starts pinking on
every climb in July heat. If the pinking is strictly a summer thing and
goes in the cool of the evening, the fuel theory becomes more likely
still — but the right conclusion is not to wait for autumn, it is to move
to the octane rating the manufacturer requires.

## How the repair is decided

There are few expensive scenarios here, and nearly all of them are the
consequence of a drawn-out search: regular detonation gradually destroys
the pistons, and the bill grows by an order of magnitude. So the criterion
is simple — work through the cheap, reversible checks in order, and only
call it mechanical when a tank of correct fuel, a fresh set of plugs to
the right heat range and a scan tool read all fail to change anything.

Two things mechanics do not always say. If detonation is diagnosed, ask
whether the knock sensor and the cooling system were actually checked or
only the fuel blamed — a sensor that has stopped reporting lets the engine
detonate silently, and that is the version that damages pistons. And if a
decarbonising service is proposed, ask what evidence of carbon there is:
plug condition and a borescope look at the piston crowns are the honest
answer, an assumption from mileage is not.
