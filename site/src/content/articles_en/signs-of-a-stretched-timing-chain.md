The timing chain synchronises the crankshaft and the camshafts: it decides
that the valves open exactly in step with the pistons. It rarely fails
overnight — it reports its condition with sound, fault codes and
behaviour. Below are the signs of a stretched chain by stage, the checks
that can be done without stripping the engine, and how the job is decided.

## What “the chain has stretched” means

The metal does not stretch like an elastic band. A chain consists of
hundreds of links joined by pins and bushes, and each joint develops a
microscopic clearance over time. Multiplied by the number of links, that
wear adds length: **0.5–1 % of elongation** is already the limit for most
engines.

The slack is first taken up by the tensioner, a hydraulic ram that presses
the chain through a plastic shoe using oil pressure. While its travel
lasts, the engine runs quietly. When its plunger is almost fully out, the
chain starts whipping the guides and the valve timing starts to drift.
That is why the signs appear gradually rather than all at once.

The wear is accelerated by infrequent oil changes, a low level, frequent
cold starts and long spells in traffic: the chain is lubricated by the
same oil as the rest of the engine, and dirty oil works as an abrasive in
the joints.

## How a stretched chain sounds

The classic picture is **a metallic rumble or rustle in the first seconds
after a cold start**, disappearing as soon as the tensioner builds
pressure. The sound resembles a diesel-like clatter or metal rings pouring
over each other, and comes from the timing drive end — usually the front
of the engine, or the side nearest one wheel on a transverse engine.

As the wear progresses the sound changes:

- the rumble after starting lasts longer — not 1–2 seconds but 10–30 or
  more;
- the rustle appears **on a warm engine** at idle;
- on a sharp lift-off or throttle blip there is a **rattle** — the slack
  chain hitting the shoes;
- at a late stage the rumble becomes constant and loud.

The chain has a lookalike — a worn or air-locked tensioner. It produces
the same sounds with a healthy chain and is noticeably cheaper to cure, so
workshops check it first;
[signs of a failing timing chain tensioner](/en/articles/timing-chain-tensioner-failure-signs/)
covers it. The reverse also happens: after a long stand or an oil change
even a healthy drive can be briefly noisy — the time to worry is when the
sound repeats at every start and gets longer.

Do not confuse the chain with other cold sounds:
[hydraulic lifters ticking when cold](/en/articles/hydraulic-lifters-ticking-when-cold/)
sound higher and more even than a chain's rumble. The quick symptom
reference is
[engine knock when cold](/en/symptoms/engine-knock-when-cold/), and the
full sound portrait is in
[what a stretched timing chain sounds like](/en/articles/what-a-stretched-timing-chain-sounds-like/).

## Signs without sound: codes and behaviour

A stretched chain shifts the shafts' relative position, and the
electronics see it before the ear hears anything:

- **Check engine with correlation codes** — most often P0016/P0017
  (crankshaft and camshaft position mismatch), sometimes P0008;
- **a rough idle**, slight misfiring;
- **flat acceleration and rising consumption** — the timing has drifted
  and cylinder filling has worsened;
- **hard starting**, especially when cold: the starter turns longer than
  usual;
- on engines with cam phasers, faults from them too, because the
  actuators can no longer compensate for the drift.

None of these convicts the chain on its own: camshaft sensors, the phasers
themselves and a dirty variable timing system give a similar picture —
see [signs of a failing VVT actuator](/en/articles/vvt-actuator-failure-signs/).
So the next step is a check.

## The stages of stretch

| Stage | What you hear and see | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Early | Rumble for 1–3 seconds after a cold start, no codes | Watch, check the chain at the next service |
| Middle | Rustle lasting 10–20 seconds, rattle on a throttle blip | Diagnosis within a couple of weeks |
| Marked | P0016/P0017, rough idle, noise when warm too | Measure the stretch, plan the replacement |
| Critical | Constant clatter, the engine stalls or will not start | Do not drive: a jump is possible |

The rate of progression is hard to predict: on some engines a chain is
noisy for tens of thousands of kilometres, on others less than a season
passes from the first codes to a jump. What that risks, and when driving
yourself to a workshop is acceptable, is covered in
[can you drive with a stretched timing chain](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-a-stretched-timing-chain/).

## How the stretch is measured

The good news: on most engines the verdict is reached **without stripping
the engine**.

1. **With a scan tool.** The cam phaser correction angles and the
   correlation between the crankshaft and camshaft sensors show how far
   the timing has drifted. This is the usual first step.
2. **By the tensioner's extension.** The tensioner is removed and the
   travel it has already used is measured. A plunger that is almost fully
   out is a sure sign of a stretched chain.
3. **By the timing marks.** The crankshaft mark is aligned and the
   camshaft marks are checked for how far they lag their proper position.
4. **With an endoscope** — the shoes and guides are inspected for wear
   without removing the cover.

Listening for it yourself is described in
[checking timing chain tension by ear](/en/articles/checking-timing-chain-tension-by-ear/).

## How the replacement is decided

The labour varies several-fold: on some engines the chain is changed in
half a day, on others the front cover, the accessories and sometimes the
engine itself have to come out. Ask for the quote split into parts and
labour — that makes it easier to compare workshops and to see where
savings are being suggested.

The kit is the standard: chain, tensioner, guides and sprockets. Fitting a
new chain onto worn sprockets and an old tensioner means returning to the
same problem within a short mileage. While everything is apart, the seals
and gaskets you have already reached are usually renewed too, and on
engines where the water pump or oil pump runs on the same chain, those as
well.

Two things workshops do not always volunteer. Some crankshaft and camshaft
bolts are single-use stretch bolts, and reusing them is a known cause of a
repeat failure. And after assembly the engine should be turned by hand
through two full revolutions with the marks re-checked before it is
started — the step that catches a mistake while it is still free to fix.

After the job, ask to see the old chain next to the new one — on a worn
set the difference in length is visible to the naked eye.
