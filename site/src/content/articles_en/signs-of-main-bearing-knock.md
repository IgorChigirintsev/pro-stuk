Main bearings are the plain bearings the crankshaft rests on inside the
block. They hold the shaft along its whole length and take the load from
every cylinder at once. When their clearance grows, the engine starts
producing a low, heavy knock that experienced mechanics recognise almost
unfailingly. Here are its signs, the symptoms that come with it, and why
this fault calls for stopping immediately rather than watching.

## What it sounds like

Main bearing knock is **dull, low and heavy**, as though someone were
striking metal from inside through a cushion. It comes from the bottom of
the engine, from the sump side, and carries well into the body. The
markers:

- **Reaction to a sharp throttle.** Open the throttle sharply at idle and
  the knock answers clearly as the revs rise and fall.
- **Audible when warm.** After warm-up the oil thins, the clearance works
  more freely, and the sound usually gets worse rather than better.
- **Vibration.** Often accompanied by a perceptible shake that was not
  there before.
- **Oil pressure.** The light flickers or stays on at idle; a gauge
  needle sags.

The dividing line from the neighbouring fault is simple: big-end bearings
sound sharper and come from the middle of the block. Their portrait is in
[signs of big-end bearing knock](/en/articles/signs-of-big-end-bearing-knock/),
and the full catalogue of engine sounds is on the symptom page
[knocking in the engine](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-engine/).

## The accompanying symptoms

Main bearings rarely go alone. A characteristic picture usually forms
around the knock:

1. **Falling oil pressure** — the first and the main one; its signs are in
   [signs of low oil pressure](/en/articles/signs-of-low-oil-pressure/).
2. **Metallic particles** on the sump plug magnet and in the filter.
3. **Loss of power and rising fuel consumption** — the shaft turns against
   increased friction.
4. **A hunting idle** and vibration in every regime.
5. **The oil heating quickly** during ordinary driving.

A separate nuance: not every loud noise from the engine bay is bearings.
Whistles and whines do not belong here at all.

## How it is checked

| Check | What it gives |
|---|---|
| Stethoscope along the bottom of the block | The point of maximum volume near the sump |
| Oil pressure with a mechanical gauge | Confirms the loss of the oil wedge |
| Cutting cylinders one at a time | Mains barely react, big ends weaken |
| Inspecting the magnet and the filter | Metal dust and swarf |
| Sump off, clearances measured | The final diagnosis |

A diagnostic scan gives little here: oil pressure codes only point at the
problem indirectly. It is decided mechanically — by ear, by gauge and by
disassembly.

## What follows

The answer to “how much further can I drive” is: not at all. A spun main
bearing scores the journal and in a bad case seizes the engine on the
move, which is no longer only a question of money. The correct order is:
switch off, do not restart, call a tow truck — the wider list of sounds
that mean stopping is in
[noises that mean stop driving now](/en/articles/noises-that-mean-stop-driving-now/),
and where the line runs in
[can you drive with engine knock](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-engine-knock/).

It helps to understand the scope of work. Main bearings are replaced with
the engine out, or at minimum the sump and the crankshaft removed, and if
the journals are already scored the shaft is ground to an undersize or
replaced. So the quote is drawn up after the strip-down, and any figure
named over the phone before that is a very rough guide.

Two questions worth asking when it comes to that. Whether the journals
will be measured with a micrometer rather than judged by eye — new shells
on a damaged journal do not last. And whether the cause was found: more
than half of spun bearings start with oil starvation rather than mileage,
so a blocked pick-up screen, a worn pump or a stretched oil interval must
be found or the rebuild repeats.

Prevention here is dull but effective: keep to the oil change intervals
with an allowance for town conditions and dust, watch the level, and do
not ignore the first flickers of the pressure light.
