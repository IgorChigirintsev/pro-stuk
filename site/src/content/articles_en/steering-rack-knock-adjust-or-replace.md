Once a workshop confirms that the knock really is the steering rack, the
owner has three roads: adjust it, rebuild it, or replace the unit
outright. Parts sellers predictably vote for replacement, adjusters vote
for an adjustment. The truth depends not on opinions but on where exactly
the clearance appeared and how evenly the rack and pinion have worn. Here
are the criteria and the usual mistakes.

## What an adjustment actually is

Inside the rack, a spring-loaded yoke presses the toothed bar against the
pinion. With mileage the pinion-and-bar pair wears, the clearance grows,
and on small bumps the bar starts hitting the pinion — that is the
familiar dull knock, one of the most common on the symptom page
[knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/). An adjustment
means turning the screw that loads the yoke: the clearance shrinks and the
knock goes. Nothing inside is replaced or restored — the adjustment simply
takes up the play that has developed.

Hence the main limitation: an adjustment only helps while the clearance is
small and even along the whole travel of the bar. Wear always concentrates
in the centre, where the steering works most. Adjust a badly worn rack "by
the centre" and the pair will be pinched at the ends of the travel: the
steering starts binding in bends, which is more dangerous than the
original knock.

## Adjust, rebuild or replace

| Situation | The sensible decision |
|---|---|
| Light knock, small play, dry bellows | Adjustment |
| Knock returned soon after an adjustment | Rebuild |
| Large play, binding at the ends of travel | Rebuild |
| Fluid leaking from the bellows (hydraulic) | Rebuild with new seals |
| Deep wear on the bar, scoring, corrosion | Replace the unit |
| Damaged housing, repair impossible | Replace the unit |

A rebuild means stripping the unit and replacing bushes, seals and the
yoke from a repair kit, with the shaft reground if needed. Replacement is
justified when the geometry of the bar can no longer be restored: a new or
remanufactured rack goes in with a warranty.

One aside about power assistance. On hydraulic racks, before deciding,
make sure the noise and heavy steering are not down to the pump: its voice
is clearly heard when the wheel is turned to full lock. On racks with
electric assistance built into the rack or the column, only the mechanical
part can be adjusted, and the "electrical symptoms" — heavy steering,
unequal effort side to side — have nothing to do with the yoke; their list
is in
[electric power steering fault signs](/en/articles/electric-power-steering-fault-signs/).

## How the repair is decided

The order of thinking is straightforward: while the wear is even, an
adjustment honestly postpones a rebuild by tens of thousands of
kilometres. When the knock comes back quickly, every further adjustment
only pinches the pair and brings replacement closer. It is cheaper to stop
and rebuild.

Ask two things before agreeing to work. Whether the adjuster still has
travel left — an adjuster already at its limit means the answer is a
rebuild, not a tweak. And whether an alignment is included: any work
involving the tie rods changes the toe, and without a fresh alignment the
tyres pay for it.

## How not to pay twice

Three practical rules. First: before any decision, be sure it is the rack
knocking and not the rod joints, the mountings or the subframe — the dull
knocks of these parts are indistinguishable by ear even to an experienced
one. The way to separate them is set out in
[a knock: steering rack or track rod ends](/en/articles/knock-steering-rack-or-track-rod-ends/),
and the anti-roll bar links, the cheapest of the suspects, in
[checking anti-roll bar links for knocking](/en/articles/checking-anti-roll-bar-links-for-knocking/).

Second: after an adjustment check the self-centring — coming out of a
bend, the wheel should want to return to centre by itself; sticky,
"clinging" steering means it is over-tightened, and that needs correcting
straight away. Third: during a rebuild replace the bellows and check the
track rod ends — stripping the unit again for a small part is doubly
annoying. And do not confuse the knock with a hum: an even hum that grows
with speed is not the rack but most likely a wheel bearing, which has a
completely different diagnostic and repair path —
[signs of a worn wheel bearing](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-wheel-bearing/).
The full symptom list for the mechanism itself is in
[signs of a faulty steering rack](/en/articles/steering-rack-fault-signs/).
