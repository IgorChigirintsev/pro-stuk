Anti-roll bar bushes are rubber rings costing the price of a couple of
coffees that can sound like a serious suspension failure. A dull knock on
every bump, creaking when the car is rocked — from the description you
could suspect anything, up to and including the dampers. Here is how the
part works, how to identify the bushes specifically, how to check them
without a workshop, and what the replacement involves.

## What anti-roll bar bushes are

The anti-roll bar is a springy metal bar linking the left and right wheels
of one axle. As the body rolls in a corner, the bar twists and resists the
roll. It attaches to the subframe or body through bushes — rubber mounts
inside which the bar rotates on every suspension movement.

The bushes' rubber ages from loads, temperature swings and road salt: it
hardens, loses its elasticity and wears through from inside. A gap appears
between the bar and the bush — and the bar starts moving in it with
impacts. That is the source of the sound.

## What worn bushes sound like

The signature is recognisable:

- **a soft dull knock over small bumps** — ripples, joints, tram rails; on
  large potholes it is lost in the general work of the suspension;
- **a knock over speed humps** and entering car parks — wherever the body
  rolls;
- **creaking when the car is rocked** by hand, noticeably louder after
  rain, a wash or in frost;
- the location is **at the front, low down, closer to the centre** of the
  car rather than at a wheel;
- on smooth roads, silence — the sound is strictly tied to bumps, as on
  the symptom page
  [knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/).

An important difference from the dangerous causes: a bush knock does not
change in corners at speed and is never felt through the steering wheel.

## Bushes or links: telling them apart

Anti-roll bar links — the short rods with joints connecting the bar to the
suspension — wear from the same loads and sound similar. The differences:

| Sign | Bushes | Links |
|---|---|---|
| Character of the sound | Soft, dull, closer to the centre | Chattering, sharper, closer to the wheel |
| Creaking | Often, especially in damp | Rarely |
| Check by hand | The bar moves inside the bush when rocked | Play in the link when rocked by the rod |
| Part cost | Very low, sold in pairs | Low, per side |

In practice you do not have to separate them before the workshop visit:
both are checked in five minutes on the same lift and are often replaced
together, since they age as a pair. The links' own signs are in
[signs of worn anti-roll bar links](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-anti-roll-bar-links/).
If the sound fits neither portrait, widen the circle: bushes and dampers
give similar thumps, and their signature is covered in
[a dull knock from the front over potholes](/en/articles/dull-knock-from-the-front-over-potholes/).

## How to check them yourself

Two tests work without a lift. The first is rocking: push rhythmically on
the wing; a dull knock or creak from below, centrally, points at the
anti-roll bar. The second needs a helper: they rock the car while you
listen from under the arch to work out which side and what height the
sound comes from.

On a lift it is simpler: the mechanic levers the bar with a pry bar and
watches whether it moves inside the bushes. That check takes minutes and
is often free when you book the repair.

## How the replacement is decided

This is one of the cheapest suspension repairs, and no alignment is needed
afterwards — anti-roll bar parts do not affect the wheel angles.

Three things are worth knowing before the job. New bushes must match the
bar's diameter exactly: a bush one size up is the classic reason the knock
survives the repair. The bracket bolts should be torqued rather than
guessed, because an over-tightened bracket squeezes the rubber out of
shape and it fails within a season. And the bar itself deserves a look
where the bushes sit — if it has been running loose long enough to wear a
groove into the metal, new bushes on a grooved bar will not sit properly.

Leaving it is not worthwhile either: a loose bar wears not only the bushes
but its own mounting points, and replacing the bar is a different order of
cost.
