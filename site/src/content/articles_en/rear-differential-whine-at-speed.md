A rear differential is a box with a pair of bevel gears that turns the
power flow through ninety degrees and shares it between the rear wheels.
The teeth in it work with constant sliding and live in an oil bath, so
wear here is almost always heard first and felt later. The classic
handwriting is a whine that appears in a particular band of speeds and
sounds different on power and on the overrun. That is what separates it
from every other source of noise at the rear.

## Why the whine changes with the throttle

When the engine is pulling, the teeth of the pinion press on one working
face of the crown wheel's teeth. When the driver lifts off and the car
coasts, the load changes sign and the teeth bear on the opposite faces. If
the wear is uneven or the backlash in the mesh is set wrongly, those two
faces sound different — hence the signature effect: at 70 km/h on power it
whines, at the same 70 km/h coasting it is nearly silent, or the other way
round.

The second source of whine inside the differential is the shaft bearings.
They give a steadier hum that depends less on the pedal but rises with
speed. Often both sounds overlap, and by ear you get a layered whine with
different shades at different speeds.

## Telling it from other sources

| Source | Reaction to power and overrun | Reaction to manoeuvres |
|---|---|---|
| Differential, gear mesh | Clearly different on power and coasting | Almost none |
| Differential bearings | Weak, rises with speed | Almost none |
| Wheel bearing | None | Changes noticeably in bends |
| Propshaft centre bearing | Weak | None |
| Tyres | None | Changes with the surface |

The practical procedure is simple: find the speed at which the whine is
clearest, and at that speed hold the throttle, lift off, and weave gently
left and right in turn. A difference with the pedal points at the
differential, a difference in the bends at the hub, and the absence of
both usually means the propshaft centre bearing or the tyres —
[signs of a worn wheel bearing](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-wheel-bearing/)
and
[signs of a worn propshaft centre bearing](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-propshaft-centre-bearing/).
The full catalogue of this family of noises is on the symptom page
[humming while driving](/en/symptoms/humming-while-driving/), and the
gearbox version of the same logic in
[gearbox whine at a particular speed](/en/articles/gearbox-whine-at-a-particular-speed/).

And if instead of an even whine you hear a chattering click in turns, see
[signs of a worn outer CV joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-outer-cv-joint/).

## What a workshop checks

A differential diagnosis starts with the simple things: the level and
condition of the oil. Dark oil with metal particles in suspension and a
bristle of shavings on the magnetic plug point directly at worn teeth or
bearings. The seals are inspected at the same time: weeping means some of
the oil has gone and the unit has been running short.

Next the differential is listened to on a lift with the wheels hanging,
and the play is checked — end float in the pinion and backlash in the
mesh. The result decides what is enough: an oil and seal change, adjusting
the backlash and replacing bearings, or rebuilding the unit completely. On
many models a used complete differential turns out to be the more
economical answer.

## How the repair is decided

The logical order is a cheap diagnosis with an oil inspection first, and
only then a decision about the scale of the work — the cost of the
differential repair itself depends heavily on the model and on whether the
answer is bearings or a gear set, so it is quoted after the strip-down.

Two things mechanics do not always say. A crown wheel and pinion are a
matched pair and are replaced together, never singly — a quote that offers
one of them is a quote that comes back. And after any work inside, the
backlash and the contact pattern have to be set with marking compound
rather than by feel: a differential assembled "by ear" whines again within
months, and that is the commonest reason a rebuild fails.
