The engine starts with a tick or a dull knock, and a minute or two later
the sound has dissolved — a familiar picture, especially in winter. A
knock that goes away with warm-up is its own class of symptom: it is
almost always linked to cold thick oil and larger cold clearances, and in
most cases it is the least dangerous of all engine knocks. But it has its
unpleasant exceptions. Here is what knocks before warm-up, where normal
ends and when a workshop is due.

## Why a cold engine knocks louder

After standing overnight an engine is in its least favourable state. **The
oil has drained into the sump** and thickened: at −20 °C even good
synthetic oil flows noticeably worse than at a working +90 °C. In the
first seconds after starting the pump is only distributing oil through the
galleries, and the components that depend on its pressure run dry.

The second reason is **thermal clearances**. Engine parts are designed to
reach their correct sizes at operating temperature. A cold aluminium
piston sits more loosely in an iron bore than a hot one; the valvetrain
clearances are different when cold too.

Hence the main diagnostic sign: if the sound honestly and completely
disappears with warm-up, the source is almost certainly linked to oil or
thermal expansion, and there is time for a calm diagnosis.

## Hydraulic lifters: the most common culprit

A hydraulic lifter is a small plunger in the valve drive that uses oil
pressure to take up the clearance automatically. Overnight the oil partly
leaves them, and until the pressure is restored they tick — an even
tick-tick-tick from the top of the engine at half crankshaft speed. The
symptom page is
[engine knock when cold](/en/symptoms/engine-knock-when-cold/).

Normal is ticking in the first seconds after starting. If it lasts
minutes, coked lifters, wear or unsuitable oil are likely. The full
breakdown of that case is in
[hydraulic lifters ticking when cold](/en/articles/hydraulic-lifters-ticking-when-cold/).

## Piston slap and the other cold knocks

**Piston slap** is a dull knock from the block at start-up that clears as
the engine warms. A cold piston has a larger clearance in its bore and
rocks from one wall to the other as the side load reverses. Moderate slap
when cold is considered acceptable on many engines — manufacturers
describe it in service bulletins. The concerning version is when the knock
lives longer every month and starts being audible on a warm engine: that
is a sign of piston and bore wear, covered in
[signs of cylinder bore scoring](/en/articles/signs-of-cylinder-bore-scoring/).

**The timing chain tensioner** does not tension the chain before oil
pressure arrives — hence chatter or clatter in the first one to three
seconds. A short sound in frost is borderline normal; several seconds at
every start is a sign of a worn tensioner, its check valve, or a stretched
chain, as set out in
[signs of a stretched timing chain](/en/articles/signs-of-a-stretched-timing-chain/).

**Cam phasers** on some engines rattle at a cold start if the locking
mechanism is worn — a short rattle that disappears with oil pressure, in
[cam phaser rattle when cold](/en/articles/cam-phaser-rattle-when-cold/).

**A cracked exhaust manifold** gives a cutting tick resembling a valve:
cold, the exhaust escapes through the gap; after warm-up the metal expands
and the crack closes. The distinguishing feature is a smell of exhaust
under the bonnet and a sound synchronous with the revs, as described on
[engine ticking](/en/symptoms/ticking-engine/) and in
[signs of a burnt exhaust manifold gasket](/en/articles/burnt-exhaust-manifold-gasket-signs/).

## A reference table

| Cause | How it sounds | How long | Danger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydraulic lifters | Ticking from above | Seconds; minutes when worn | Low; medium if ignored |
| Piston slap | Dull knock from the block | Until warm-up | Low, watch the trend |
| Chain tensioner | Chatter, clatter at start-up | 1–3 seconds | Medium; high if repeated |
| Cam phaser | Rattle at start-up | 1–2 seconds | Medium |
| Cracked manifold | Cutting tick | Until warm-up | Low, but with exhaust smell |

## What you can do yourself

Three steps before a workshop visit:

1. **Check the oil.** Level on the dipstick, date of the last change,
   viscosity against the manufacturer's specification.
2. **Time the knock.** Literally with a stopwatch, at comparable outside
   temperatures. That figure and its trend week by week is the most useful
   information a mechanic can have.
3. **Record the sound.** A cold start is easy to record on a phone: it
   happens once a day, and at the workshop on a warm engine it may already
   be gone.

A separate question is whether to idle the engine to warm it so it does
not knock. Modern engines do not need a long idle warm-up: the oil reaches
operating temperature faster while driving. A sensible compromise in frost
is a minute or two at idle, then calm driving without high revs for the
first five to ten minutes. That both shortens the knock and reduces cold
wear.

If the knock is audible on a warm engine too, even faintly, that is a
different task.

## How the repair is decided

The order runs from cheap to expensive: oil and filter to the correct
specification first, then a diagnosis with a stethoscope and a scan tool,
and only then the parts. Two things are worth asking before agreeing to
lifters or a timing kit: what was actually measured — chain stretch, cam
timing offset, lifter play — and whether the oil history explains the
wear, because a fresh set of parts fed by the same neglected oil regime
ticks again.

A useful habit is to record a cold start in the Stuk app and repeat the
recording a month later. The app matches the sound with typical causes,
shows probabilities and urgency, and comparing the recordings shows
whether the problem is growing — without pressure or extra workshop trips.
