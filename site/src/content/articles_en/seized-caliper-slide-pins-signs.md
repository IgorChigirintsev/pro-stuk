A floating caliper is a simple thing: the carrier with the piston slides
on two steel pins, so it can centre itself and press both pads evenly
against the disc. While the pins move freely, the brake works as designed.
But boots tear, grease ages and washes out, water and road salt get into
the clearance — and a slide pin seizes. Then the characteristic set of
symptoms begins, which drivers usually describe as “the car does not roll
as well and pulls to one side”.

## What happens when a pin seizes

When a pin stops moving, the carrier loses its freedom. The piston still
presses the inner pad, while the outer one either barely takes part in
braking or, conversely, cannot come away from the disc after you release
the pedal. Either way the mechanism stops fully releasing: a pad stays
pressed and rubs on the disc constantly.

Constant friction generates heat. The disc and the hub get hot, the heated
brake fluid in the circuit expands, the brake binds harder — a vicious
circle that in bad cases ends with a disc heated blue and boiling fluid
with a sinking pedal.

## What the driver notices

- **One hot wheel.** After a calm drive the disc on the problem side is
  noticeably hotter than the others, sometimes with a burning smell.
- **The car does not roll as well.** In neutral it slows itself, fuel
  consumption rises and the engine works harder on climbs.
- **Pulling under braking.** The steering pulls towards the side where the
  brake works harder.
- **Grinding and roaring** from the problem wheel: the overheated pad
  wears down to the backing and starts cutting the disc. That stage is
  covered on the symptom page
  [grinding when braking](/en/symptoms/grinding-when-braking/) and in
  [telling brake pad wear by ear](/en/articles/brake-pad-wear-by-ear/).
- **Uneven wear.** The inner pad almost gone, the outer one like new — or
  the reverse.
- **Judder and pedal vibration** after the disc has overheated.

## What it can be confused with

| Sign | Seized slide pins | Another source |
|---|---|---|
| Grinding strictly when the pedal is pressed | Yes, at a late stage | Worn pads on both sides |
| Crunching and clicking in a tight turn under power | No | [A worn outer CV joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-outer-cv-joint/) |
| A hum that grows with speed | No | Wheel bearing |
| Chattering and whining from under the bonnet | No | [A/C compressor clutch](/en/articles/ac-compressor-clutch-failure-sounds/) |
| A wheel hot after calm driving | Characteristic | A seized piston, a rusted handbrake cable at the rear |

Two similar faults are worth separating: seized slide pins and a seized
piston in the caliper bore. The symptoms are close, but with seized pins
the uneven pad wear is more pronounced, while with a seized piston the
pads wear more evenly and the pedal goes wooden while the wheel heats up
more. The exact diagnosis comes with the wheel off: the mechanic frees the
mechanism, checks the carrier's travel by hand and pushes the piston back
with a tool. The full set of driver-level checks is in
[how to check a brake caliper for sticking](/en/articles/how-to-check-a-brake-caliper-for-sticking/).

## How the repair is decided

The standard procedure is a caliper service: the mechanism comes off, the
pins are removed, cleaned of corrosion, new boots are fitted and a special
grease is packed in — one that withstands high temperature and does not
attack rubber. Ordinary chassis grease is not suitable: it is squeezed out
and destroys the boots. If the pins are pitted, they are replaced.

Two questions worth asking. Whether the pins are being stripped and
regreased or merely “looked at” — dry pins are the single most common
reason the fault returns a month later. And whether the disc was measured
against its minimum thickness after overheating, because a disc that has
been cooked and skimmed below the limit has to be replaced rather than
reused.

It makes sense to service both sides of an axle at once: they live in the
same conditions and the second caliper is usually not far behind the
first. A sensible preventive interval is to look into the slide pins at
every pad change: it adds a little time but renews the boots and the
grease before water gets in. Where winter salt and spring slush are part
of life, that habit pays for itself fastest — pin corrosion starts with a
damaged boot, not with mileage.
