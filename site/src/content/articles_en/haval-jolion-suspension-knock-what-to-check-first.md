The Haval Jolion is a mass-market compact crossover, and the complaint
about a knock at the front sounds much the same as on any other car in its
class: a dry click or short thump over small bumps, slab joints and speed
humps, with silence on smooth tarmac. Below is a sensible order of checks:
from the most common and cheapest to the rarer and more expensive. That
sequence saves both time and money, because in roughly half the cases
there is no need to go past the first item.

## Start here: the anti-roll bar links

An anti-roll bar link is a thin rod with two joints connecting the
anti-roll bar to the strut. Its joints are small and take very frequent
alternating loads, so it is the first thing to wear on almost every
front-wheel-drive crossover, whatever the badge.

The characteristic sign is a knock on small, frequent bumps rather than
large potholes, and noticeably louder on a cold car. The check is simple:
on a lift, or even with the car on the ground, take the rod in your hand
and rock it — a healthy one gives no perceptible play and does not click.
The second method needs no pit: rock the body over the wheel and listen.
The full signs are in
[signs of worn anti-roll bar links](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-anti-roll-bar-links/).

## The second round: top mounts and bushes

If the links are sound, three things come next.

**The strut top mount bearing** — the part the strut rotates on with the
wheel. Its signature is a click or crunch when steering while parked and
at low speed, rather than over bumps —
[a noisy strut top mount bearing](/en/articles/strut-top-mount-bearing-noise-symptoms/).

**Control arm bushes** — the rubber-to-metal sleeves that damp vibration.
Worn ones give a dull knock over larger bumps and vague steering in ruts.
Tears in the rubber are visible; hidden wear is checked with a pry bar —
[signs of worn suspension bushes](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-suspension-bushes/).

**The dampers and their mountings.** A knock on bottoming out, oil traces
on the body, the car rocking for more than one cycle after being pushed.

The ball joints are checked separately — not because they often fail at
this mileage, but because they are the only part on the list whose failure
is genuinely dangerous:
[signs of a worn ball joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-ball-joint/).

## What knocks outside the suspension

| Source | When it is heard | How to tell |
|---|---|---|
| Outer CV joint | Rattling in a tight turn under power | Silent in a straight line, not over bumps |
| Track rod ends | Knock through the steering on fine ripples | Play when rocking the wheel horizontally |
| Heat shield | Rattle at particular revs | Follows revs, not the road |
| Luggage, the jack | Knock over any bump | Gone once unloaded |
| Cabin plastics, seals | Creaks in the cold | Changes when you press the trim |

The first row is the one most often confused with the suspension: CV joint
clicks resemble a joint cracking and are clearly heard during a U-turn.
Their sound portrait is in
[signs of a worn outer CV joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-outer-cv-joint/),
and the general catalogue of road knocks is on the symptom page
[knocking in the suspension](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-suspension/).

## What to ask for at the workshop

While the warranty runs, start with the dealer and have the complaint
recorded in writing. Ask for the play to be shown to you on the lift
rather than described — a mechanic loading each joint with a pry bar can
point at exactly what moves, and that conversation prevents a parts list
built on guesswork.

Ask, too, which of the work needs an alignment afterwards: links do not,
arms and struts do, and knowing that in advance keeps the quote honest.
