Half the difficulty in diagnosing by ear is not hearing the noise but
working out where it comes from. Under the bonnet the sources stand twenty
centimetres apart, and to the ear they blend together. A mechanic's
stethoscope solves the problem literally: it carries vibration from one
part's casing straight into your ear and cuts out everything else. You can
build one in a couple of minutes from what is already in the garage.

## Three versions that work

**A screwdriver with a long shaft.** The quickest option. The tip rests
against the casing of the part, the end of the handle presses against your
ear or the cheekbone next to it. The longer and thicker the shaft, the
better it conducts. A plastic handle damps less than you would think, and
a wooden one works even better.

**A hose.** A length of rubber or silicone hose 60–80 cm long with an
8–12 mm bore: one end in your ear, the other held near the unit but not
pressed against it. This version catches airborne sound and is good for
finding air leaks, whistles and hissing.

**A tube with a tip.** A metal tube, or a long bolt with a piece of hose
taped to it. A hybrid of the first two: the rigid tip picks up casing
vibration, the soft part carries it to your ear without losses.

| Version | What it hears best |
|---|---|
| Screwdriver | Knocks and rustling inside a part's casing |
| Hose | Whistles, hissing, air leaks |
| Tube with hose | Accessory bearings, water pump, alternator |
| A bought stethoscope with a diaphragm | The same, but more precisely and with less background |

## Where to hold it

The order in which you work round the engine bay matters: listen unit by
unit, not at random.

1. **Alternator** — on the casing near the rear cover, where the bearing
   sits. A hum or whine that grows with revs is the typical picture of a
   dying bearing.
2. **Idlers and the belt tensioner** — on the bracket or the idler
   spindle. Rustling and rumbling live there too.
3. **Water pump** — on the pump housing. A hum and a whine, often with
   traces of coolant.
4. **Air conditioning compressor** — with the a/c on and off, to catch the
   difference.
5. **Rocker cover** — valvetrain ticking.
6. **Timing cover at the end of the block** — chain rustle.

If the sound disappears with the belt removed, the source is an accessory
rather than the engine itself. Telling the two main suspects apart is
covered in
[whine under the bonnet: alternator or water pump](/en/articles/alternator-or-water-pump-whine/),
and the sound portrait is on the symptom page
[belt squeal](/en/symptoms/belt-squeal/).

## Safety rules

A running engine forgives fewer mistakes than you would think. The minimum
set of rules:

- handbrake on, gearbox in neutral or park, chocks under the wheels;
- sleeves buttoned, laces and drawstrings tucked away, hair tied back,
  watches and bracelets off;
- bring the tool in from the side, not over the belt or the fan;
- the exhaust manifold and hoses are red hot — do not touch them, not even
  briefly;
- listen in short bursts of 5–10 seconds and step back between them.

The electric cooling fan starts on its own, without warning, even on a hot
engine that has been switched off. Never keep a hand in its path under any
circumstances.

A separate word on volume: pressing the tool against a casing amplifies
the sound several times over, and next to a running engine that is a real
load on your hearing. Start with a light touch and increase the pressure
gradually rather than putting your ear straight onto the handle at full
force.

## Where a stethoscope is powerless

The tool works with the car stationary, so anything that only makes noise
on the move is beyond it: wheel bearings, dampers, control arm bushes,
brakes. Those are checked by other means — rocking, a pry bar, driving
over bumps and a lift. A sticking brake caliper, for instance, gives
itself away by a hot disc and the car pulling rather than by a noise at
idle; the procedure is in
[how to check a brake caliper for sticking](/en/articles/how-to-check-a-brake-caliper-for-sticking/).
Wandering thumps under load more often come from the engine mounts, and
those are checked using the method in
[checking engine mounts under load](/en/articles/checking-engine-mounts-under-load/).

A bought stethoscope with a diaphragm is not expensive and hears more
cleanly than a home-made one, but the home-made version still answers the
main question: this unit or the one next to it. And so you do not have to
rely on memory alone, start a recording in the Pro-Stuk app while you listen.
The app matches the sound with your answers about the regime and the
location and shows the likely causes with percentages.
