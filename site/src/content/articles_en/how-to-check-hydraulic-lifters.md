A hydraulic lifter is a small plunger cylinder in the valve train that
takes up the thermal clearance automatically and saves the owner from
adjusting valves. It runs on engine oil: pressure holds the plunger out,
and the clearance stays at zero. As soon as there is not enough oil or the
galleries clog up, the clearance appears — and the engine starts ticking.
Here is how to check the lifters yourself and when the sound calls for
action.

## The cold and warm test

The test is built on comparing two states. First start the engine after a
night's stand and time how long the ticking lasts. Then warm the engine to
operating temperature and listen again.

- **Ticks for 10–60 seconds and stops.** The typical picture: the lifters
  are filling with oil and going quiet. Usually not a fault, but a reason
  to check the level and the age of the oil.
- **Ticks for several minutes.** The oil is too thick, worn out, or there
  is not enough of it. Often cured by a change to fresh oil of the right
  viscosity.
- **Ticks constantly on a warm engine.** One or more lifters are not
  holding pressure: a sticking plunger, wear, a blocked passage.
- **Ticking only appears under load or at high revs.** Oil pressure is
  probably falling — here the pump and the pickup matter more than the
  lifters themselves.

A useful detail: valve train noise comes from the top, under the valve
cover, and its frequency is half the crankshaft speed. That is hard to
judge by ear alone but quite clear with a stethoscope against the cover.

## What ticks in a similar way

| Source | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|
| Hydraulic lifters | Ticking from the top, louder when cold, follows the oil |
| Injectors | Dry, even clicks, the same cold and warm |
| Valve clearances | Even knock, unchanged by warm-up or oil |
| Timing chain | Chatter and rustle from the end of the engine, not ticking |
| Exhaust manifold | Metallic puffing from below, worse under load |

The key to telling them apart is the reaction to warm-up and to an oil
change. Injectors and mechanical valve clearances barely follow
temperature; lifters follow it directly. The full set of sounds in this
family is on the symptom page
[ticking engine](/en/symptoms/ticking-engine/).

## Where to start

Oil first. Check the level on a cold engine and look at its condition:
black, thick oil smelling burnt almost always produces lifter noise. Look
up the viscosity recommended for your engine and compare it with what is
in there: oil that is too thick in winter does not reach the plungers in
time.

Next, oil pressure. Some cars have a sender, but the accurate picture
comes from a mechanical gauge fitted in its place at a workshop. Low
pressure explains the ticking and other noises at once, and its cure has
nothing to do with replacing lifters.

Only as a third step does dismantling come into the conversation. Before
that it is worth ruling out sounds from elsewhere: vibration and dull
thumps under load may come from the engine mounts rather than the valves.

## How the repair is decided

Starting with the oil is sensible not out of thrift but because it is the
most common cause. If the ticking disappears after fresh oil of the right
viscosity, the question closes cheaply. If it stays and becomes permanent
on a warm engine, dismantling follows.

Two things belong in that conversation. The whole set of lifters is
replaced rather than the noisy one: they share an age and a mileage, and
doing them separately means paying for the same disassembly twice. And the
camshaft lobes are inspected while the engine is open — a lifter that has
been ticking for months leaves marks on the lobe, and finding that out
afterwards is the expensive route.
