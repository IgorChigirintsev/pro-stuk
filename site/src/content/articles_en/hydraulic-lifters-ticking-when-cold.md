A hydraulic lifter is a small piston in the valve train that uses oil
pressure to take up the thermal clearance automatically, so the valves
never need adjusting by hand. After a cold start these parts often tick —
and this is the single most common “frightening noise” that owners bring
to a workshop for nothing. Below is where the line runs between normal and
faulty, and what to check first.

## Why it happens specifically when cold

Inside a lifter there is a plunger pair and a check valve. While the
engine runs, the plunger chamber is full of pressurised oil and the lifter
holds the clearance between the camshaft lobe and the valve at zero.
Overnight some of that oil drains into the sump and air is left in the
chambers.

At startup the clearance in the valve train is not taken up by anything
for a while — the lobe strikes the lifter, and you hear the characteristic
dry **ticking**, at half the frequency of crankshaft speed, because the
camshaft turns half as fast. The oil pump raises pressure, oil pushes the
air out of the plungers, and the noise goes.

That is a designed-in process, not a fault: a lifter is built to tolerate
a short spell running dry after the car has stood.

## When ticking when cold is normal

The markers that mean there is nothing to worry about:

- the ticking lasts **from a few seconds up to 10–30 seconds** after
  starting;
- in hard frost, up to a minute or two: thickened oil takes longer to
  reach the cylinder head;
- the sound is **even, high-pitched, like a sewing machine**, without a
  metallic rumble;
- once warm, the engine runs quietly, with normal power and consumption
  and no fault codes.

If that is your picture, keeping an eye on the oil level and the usual
change intervals is enough. A note for cars that have stood a long time:
after two or three weeks parked, the engine may tick longer than usual for
a start or two — the oil has drained almost completely — and that is not a
worry either, as long as the sound then returns to its usual few seconds.

## When it is no longer normal

| How the ticking behaves | Verdict | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 30 seconds after start (1–2 minutes in winter) | Normal | Nothing, routine servicing |
| 2–5 minutes after start | Borderline | Check the level and age of the oil, change oil and filter |
| More than 5 minutes, but quiet when hot | Wear or sticking in some lifters | Diagnosis, viscosity check, possibly replacement |
| Ticks on a warm engine too | A fault | Diagnose without postponing |
| Not ticking but a rumble or rattle | Probably not the lifters | Check the timing chain and other sources |

Two clarifications. First, a knock when hot is more serious than one when
cold: it means the lifter is not holding pressure and the clearance is
permanent. Over time that hammers the contact surfaces and can lead to a
burnt valve. Second, ticking that changes neither with warm-up nor with
revs is often unrelated to the lifters — compare it with the symptom page
[ticking engine](/en/symptoms/ticking-engine/).

The character of the sound separates lifters from a stretched timing
chain: a chain gives a low rumble and rustle from the timing drive end,
while lifters give an even, high tick from the top, under the valve cover.

## Oil is the main suspect

Before replacing the lifters, it is almost always worth dealing with the
oil — in most cases prolonged ticking starts right here:

- **the age of the oil**: past 10,000–15,000 kilometres without a change
  the additives are spent, the oil gets dirty, and plungers start to
  stick;
- **the wrong viscosity**: oil that is too thick reaches the cylinder head
  slowly in winter, oil that is too thin does not hold in the plungers;
  the reference is the engine manufacturer's specification, not general
  advice;
- **a low level** — at idle the pump draws in air and the lifters take in
  air with it;
- **a clogged oil filter or pickup screen** — pressure sags at idle.

For city driving there is a nuance worth knowing: the change interval is
more honestly counted in engine hours than in kilometres. An hour in
traffic ages the oil almost as much as an hour on the motorway, while the
odometer counts only a few kilometres. So for mostly urban use a
15,000-kilometre interval is sensibly shortened to around 7,000–10,000 —
the lifters are the first part to say thank you for fresh oil.

A practical order: check the level, change the oil and filter for fresh
ones meeting the manufacturer's specification, drive 300–500 kilometres
and assess the result. Aggressive five-minute flushes on a high-mileage
engine are at your own risk: lifted dirt can block passages and make the
noise worse.

## If the oil did not help

The next step is diagnosis: the engine is listened to with a stethoscope,
the ticking lifters are located, and oil pressure is measured. Sometimes
the mechanic presses each lifter in turn with the valve cover off — a
healthy one resists noticeably, a worn one collapses easily.

Replacing lifters is a medium-difficulty job: the valve cover has to come
off, and on many engines the camshafts as well. Two things are worth
agreeing in advance. The whole set is replaced rather than the noisy one:
if one lifter is worn, the rest are usually close behind, and doing them
separately means paying for the same disassembly twice. And the camshaft
lobes are inspected while the engine is open, because a lifter that has
been ticking for months leaves marks on the lobe.
