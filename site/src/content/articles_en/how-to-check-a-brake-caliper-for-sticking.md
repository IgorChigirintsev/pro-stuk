A sticking caliper is one of those faults that lives unnoticed for a long
time and then suddenly turns into an overheated disc, ruined pads and
extra fuel consumption. The cause is almost always mechanical: the slide
pins seize or the piston binds in its bore, and the pad stops coming away
from the disc. You can check for it yourself, without a lift, if you know
what to watch.

## Four tests you can do yourself

**The coasting test.** On a level, empty area, accelerate to 30–40 km/h
and select neutral. The car should roll for a long time and evenly. If it
slows itself, as though someone were holding the pedal, and the
deceleration is noticeably stronger than usual, there is constant
friction.

**The temperature check.** Drive 10–15 kilometres calmly, braking as
little as possible, and immediately after stopping bring a hand near each
disc without touching it. The difference between wheels on one axle is the
main marker. One wheel noticeably hotter than the rest, with a smell of
hot metal coming off it, puts that caliper under suspicion.

**The pulling test.** On an empty straight, brake gently and loosen your
grip on the wheel. If the car consistently pulls to one side, braking
forces are unequal.

**Pad inspection.** Through the wheel spokes you can see the thickness of
the outer pad. If it is worn almost to nothing on one side while the other
still has half its material, the pad is permanently pressed and the piston
or slide pins are not releasing.

## What each picture means

| Symptom | Most likely cause |
|---|---|
| One disc hot, the car pulls | Seized piston or slide pins |
| Both discs on an axle hot | The master cylinder or hoses not releasing |
| Pads worn unevenly | Seized slide pins |
| Grinding when braking | Pads finished, metal on metal |
| A hum that ignores the brake pedal | More likely a wheel bearing than the brakes |

That last row matters: a hum is often blamed on the brakes although the
source rotates with the wheel. A simple rule separates them — bearing
noise does not react to the brake pedal, while pad grinding appears
exactly when you press it. The catalogue of braking sounds is on the
symptom page
[grinding when braking](/en/symptoms/grinding-when-braking/), and finding
the side and axle is covered in
[working out where a hum comes from](/en/articles/is-the-hum-from-the-front-or-the-rear/).

## What a workshop checks

On a lift the diagnosis takes minutes. The mechanic raises the wheel and
spins it by hand: the disc should turn freely, with a light touch of the
pads rather than resistance. Then the caliper comes off, the slide pins
are checked — they should move by hand — and the boots inspected. The
piston is pushed back with a tool: if it moves stiffly or not at all, the
bore has seized.

The brake hoses get looked at separately. Rubber ages from the inside and
starts acting as a one-way valve: fluid passes under pressure but does not
return. Such a hose looks intact from outside while the wheel stays
partly braked after you release the pedal.

The condition of the brake fluid is checked too. It is hygroscopic — it
absorbs moisture from the air over time, and with moisture comes
corrosion inside the cylinders. That is exactly why regular fluid changes
indirectly extend caliper life, and why skipping them for years so often
ends in a seized piston.

## How the repair is decided

The first fork is service or replace. A caliper whose bore is sound is
stripped, cleaned and rebuilt with new boots, grease and seals; one with a
corroded bore is replaced, because new seals in a pitted cylinder fail
again within months. Ask which of the two your caliper is and why — the
answer should refer to the state of the bore, not to convenience.

The second is what else is included. If the caliper has been sticking, the
disc and pads on that side have almost certainly suffered, and the disc
has a minimum thickness stamped on it that decides whether it can stay. A
quote for a caliper alone, with no word about the disc, is worth
questioning.

Do not postpone. An overheated disc distorts, the pad burns away, and the
wheel bearing and seal suffer as heat travels into the hub. A rebuild
costs less than replacing disc, pads and caliper together later.
