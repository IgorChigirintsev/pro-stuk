The top of a front strut is where the suspension meets the body. There
sits a mount with a bearing that lets the whole strut rotate with the
wheel when you turn the steering. The part is small and cheap, but its
wear spoils how the car feels more than you would expect: clicking
appears, the steering gets heavier, and over small bumps a knock develops
that people stubbornly hunt for in the control arms.

## What the wear sounds like

The most recognisable sign is clicking and crunching as you turn the wheel
with the car parked. The sound comes from above, from the strut tower area
near the windscreen, and is easily felt with a palm on the top of the
wing. It often repeats cyclically: one click at each particular steering
angle.

The second sign is knocking over small bumps, especially while making
small steering corrections. A worn bearing gives vertical play, the strut
shifts slightly in the mount, and a dull thump appears. The third is heavy,
notchy steering: it stops returning to centre by itself after a turn, and
parking feels like it is binding.

## How to check it yourself

1. **Palm on the mount.** Put a hand on the top of the wing above the
   strut and have a helper turn the steering slowly with the car parked.
   Crunching and clicking are felt more clearly than they are heard.
2. **The parked test.** Rock the steering left and right through 30–40
   degrees: if the sound follows the steering rather than the movement of
   the car, the running gear over bumps is not involved.
3. **The bounce.** Push the wing up and down with your hand on the mount:
   a knock under your palm means vertical play.
4. **Compare sides.** The same sound left and right is usually age-related
   wear in both mounts; a difference points to one problem side.

| Check | Top mount bearing | Other parts |
|---|---|---|
| Steering with the car parked | Crunching and clicking | CV joint and suspension are silent |
| Knock over small potholes | Present, from above | The anti-roll bar knocks lower and sharper |
| Heavy steering | Grows, wheel does not return | Power steering is noisy but the wheel is light |
| Location of the sound | Top of the strut, by the windscreen | Wheel arch, hub area |

## What it is most often confused with

The main neighbour by symptoms is the outer CV joint. It also crunches
when turning, but only on the move, usually driving in circles on a car
park under power; the confirmation test is in
[checking a CV joint by driving circles](/en/articles/checking-a-cv-joint-by-driving-circles/).
The second candidate is track rod ends: they give a single click as you
change steering direction and play that is visible with the wheel raised —
see
[checking track rod ends for play](/en/articles/checking-track-rod-ends-for-play/).
The third, and the nastiest, is the ball joint; its failure is an
emergency, and what it means is set out in
[what a broken ball joint means](/en/articles/ball-joint-failure-danger/).
Compare your case with the sound portraits on the symptom page
[clicking when steering](/en/symptoms/clicking-when-steering/).

A useful rule: a sound tied to the steering with the car stationary is the
top of the strut or the steering. A sound tied to motion is the
driveshafts and the running gear.

## How the repair is decided

The main cost here is not the part but stripping the strut with spring
compressors. That single fact drives every sensible decision about the
job.

It is exactly why top mount bearings are almost always replaced together
with the dampers: paying for the same labour twice is the classic regret
here. For the same reason it makes sense to fit the bump stop and the
dust boot at the same time — they cost little and live in the same
assembly.

Two things to ask about. First, whether the alignment will be redone
afterwards: stripping a strut disturbs the camber setting on many cars,
and skipping it shows up as tyre wear a few thousand kilometres later.
Second, whether the spring is going back in the same orientation — a
spring seated wrongly on reassembly is a common source of a new knock that
appears right after the repair.

If the crunching is indistinct and you are not sure where it comes from,
