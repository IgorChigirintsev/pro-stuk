An oil change is the most common service operation, and a knock afterwards
is a frequent complaint with a bitter aftertaste: you did the right thing
and the engine started knocking. The good news: in most cases the link is
either illusory or fixable cheaply — with the level, the filter or the
viscosity. The bad: occasionally a knock after a change really does signal
something serious. Here are the causes in order, from normal to the
scenarios that mean going back.

## The first start after a change: brief ticking is normal

During a change the oil is drained and the new filter goes on empty (on
many engines it cannot be pre-filled). At the first start the pump needs a
few seconds to fill the filter and the galleries — during which the
hydraulic lifters run without pressure and tick.

Normal looks like this: ticking in the first 5–30 seconds after the first
start, then silence; the oil pressure light goes out within a second or
two. If the sound went and did not return, that is the standard scenario
needing no intervention. How the mechanism works and where normal ends is
covered in
[hydraulic lifters ticking when cold](/en/articles/hydraulic-lifters-ticking-when-cold/).

## If the knock stays: the main suspects

| What changed | How it shows | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Viscosity outside the specification | Ticking when hot (too thin) or long ticking when cold (too thick) | Compare the invoice with the handbook, change the oil |
| Underfilled | Knock louder in corners and under acceleration, possible pressure light | Top up immediately |
| Overfilled | Oil foaming, unstable ticking | Draw off the excess |
| A filter without a working anti-drainback valve | Long ticking at every cold start | Fit a quality filter |
| A flush before the change | The knock appeared and is growing | Diagnosis, possibly another oil change |
| Counterfeit oil | A knock after a few hundred kilometres, dark oil | Change oil and filter from a trusted source |

**Viscosity.** The most common real cause. A worn engine that ran for
years on a thicker grade can knock when hot after a switch to a
fashionable thin one: thin oil holds enlarged clearances less well. The
reverse switch is a stress too: thick oil in frost takes longer to reach
the lifters. The reference is the engine manufacturer's specification,
with an allowance for wear and climate.

**Level.** Underfilling risks oil starvation: the pump picks up air, the
pressure sags, the lifters tick and in bad cases the crankshaft bearings
knock. Overfilling is more insidious than it looks: the crankshaft whips
the oil into foam, and foam pumps badly. A dipstick check on level ground
takes a minute and removes half the questions — the wider list is in
[signs of low oil pressure](/en/articles/signs-of-low-oil-pressure/).

**The filter.** A filter has an anti-drainback valve that keeps oil in the
galleries while the car stands. On cheap filters it can be stiff or
leaky — then the oil drains overnight and every morning starts with long
ticking that was not there before the change.

**Flushing.** An aggressive flush on a high-mileage engine lifts deposits
that can block the oil pick-up screen and the galleries. If the knock
appeared after a change with a flush and is growing, that is a reason for
a prompt diagnosis.

One nuance rarely mentioned is the method. With an extraction change the
oil is drawn out through the dipstick tube, and more old oil and sediment
can remain in the sump than with a classic drain through the plug. That is
not a failure in itself, but if the engine is sensitive to oil and ticks,
a gravity drain with the plug removed is wiser next time — and the
mechanic gets to see whether there is swarf in the drained oil.

## Coincidence, not cause

A separate scenario: the engine would have knocked without the change. The
owner starts listening to the car after a service — and “hears” a sound
that quietly lived there before. Beyond that, fresh oil sometimes honestly
reveals wear that the older, thicker oil was masking. The character helps:
ticking from above is the lifters and valves; a dull low knock from deep
in the block is the bearings, and that cannot wait —
[signs of big-end bearing knock](/en/articles/signs-of-big-end-bearing-knock/).
To place the shades of ticking, see the symptom page
[engine ticking](/en/symptoms/ticking-engine/) and
[how to check hydraulic lifters](/en/articles/how-to-check-hydraulic-lifters/).

The alarming companion of a knock is the oil pressure light, or its
flicker at idle. That is no longer about sound but about pressure.

## Step by step

1. **Check the level** with the dipstick on level ground, engine cold. Top
   up or draw off as needed.
2. **Find the invoice** and compare the oil that went in with the
   specification: viscosity, approval, quantity.
3. **Look at the parking spot and the filter.** Weeping at the filter or
   the plug is a reason to go straight back.
4. **Judge the sound.** Ticking from above only when cold — watch for a
   couple of days; a knock when hot, a dull knock from the block or the
   pressure light — do not drive, find the cause at once.
5. **Go back to where it was done.** A decent workshop checks its own work
   free; the conversation is easier with recorded facts — a recording of
   the sound, a photo of the dipstick, the invoice.

Even a full second oil change costs less than any repair that driving with
an unexplained knock leads to.
