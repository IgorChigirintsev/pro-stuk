An automatic gearbox almost never dies in a day. Between the first “it
seemed to think about that” and a tow to a workshop, months usually pass —
sometimes years — and all that time the car is sending signals. The
trouble is that they build up gradually and the driver gets used to them.
Here is what to watch and listen for, to catch the wear at the stage where
the repair still fits a sensible figure.

## Early signs: the gearbox is still alive

At the first stage the automatic works but loses the invisibility it was
bought for. A delay appears: the lever goes to D and the car moves a
second or a second and a half later. Shifts become harsher — the moment of
the change starts to be felt as a nudge. There are hesitations on
kickdown: the throttle is on the floor and the gearbox takes longer than
usual to choose a gear.

Sound belongs to this stage too. An even whine in neutral that changes as
the selector is moved often comes from the pump and the bearings and
deserves its own breakdown, collected in
[gearbox whine in neutral](/en/articles/gearbox-whine-in-neutral/) and
[early signs of automatic transmission whine](/en/articles/automatic-transmission-whine-early-signs/).
Half the early symptoms at this stage are cured by fluid: old fluid loses
its viscosity and anti-wear properties, pressure in the valve body falls,
and the gearbox starts running late.

## The middle stage: time for a specialist

| Sign | What lies behind it |
|---|---|
| Shunts on a warm gearbox | Valve body, solenoids, worn clutch packs |
| One gear disappears | Failure of a specific pack or solenoid |
| Limp mode (third only) | A fault in the control module, gearbox protection |
| Vibration at 60–80 km/h | Torque converter, lock-up clutch |
| Dark fluid with a burnt smell | Overheating and friction material wear |
| A knock at the moment of a shift | Often the mounts, not the gearbox |

The last row matters: not everything that looks like an automatic's death
is one. Worn engine mounts turn a normal torque surge into a thump through
the body, and outwardly that resembles a gearbox problem closely — see
[signs of worn engine mounts](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-engine-mounts/).
The principle is the same as everywhere: rule out the cheap things first.

## The late stage: driving is no longer an option

Here the signs need no interpretation. Slipping — the engine revs rise,
the speed does not. Jerking at every shift. Metallic grinding from under
the floor. Swarf on the sump magnets and in the fluid. A warning light
together with a switch to limp mode.

At this stage every journey works against you: the wear debris circulates
with the fluid through the valve body and blocks the passages, so the
pressure falls and the surviving clutches burn. A repair that yesterday
was one pack and a solenoid becomes a full rebuild. If grinding has
appeared or the gearbox no longer holds a gear, calling a tow truck is
wiser than driving there. The general reference for noises that must not
be tolerated is on the symptom page
[humming while driving](/en/symptoms/humming-while-driving/), and the
manual gearbox equivalent in
[can you drive with a whining gearbox](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-a-whining-gearbox/).

## How the repair is decided

The cost of a rebuild cannot be named in advance: it depends on the
gearbox model and on what has already been destroyed. But the order of
steps is the same everywhere, and it is worth insisting on.

First, a scan and a fluid inspection — colour, smell and what is on the
sump magnets. Those three things separate “this needs fresh fluid” from
“this needs opening”.

Second, the question of the fluid change itself. On a gearbox with early
symptoms a change usually helps; on one that is already slipping it
sometimes makes things worse by washing deposits into the passages, and an
honest workshop says so rather than selling a change as a cure. Ask which
of the two situations yours is and why.

Third, what is included in a rebuild: the valve body, the torque converter
and the cooler. A rebuilt gearbox fed by a blocked cooler or paired with
an unopened torque converter full of the old debris comes back, and that
is the most common reason a rebuild fails within a year.

If you are at an early stage and unsure whether the gearbox's sound has
changed over recent months, record it in the Stuk app every few weeks — at
idle, in neutral and under acceleration. The app keeps the history,
compares the recordings and shows whether things are getting worse or the
sound was simply always like that.
