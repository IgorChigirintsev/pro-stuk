A broken catalytic converter has a voice of its own, unlike anything else.
It is not a knock or a hum but a dry, pouring rustle from under the floor,
easiest to describe as a handful of gravel rolling around in a tin can
beneath the car. The sound appears over small bumps, when pulling away and
when braking — and it is often the first clear signal that the ceramic
honeycomb inside the housing has stopped being a single block.

## Why the ceramic breaks up at all

Inside the converter sits a thin-walled ceramic block with hundreds of
channels, held in a metal housing by a sealing mat. The material is strong
in compression but brittle: it copes badly with impacts and with sudden
temperature swings.

Three scenarios destroy it. The first is overheating: if unburnt fuel
keeps reaching the exhaust because of misfires or over-fuelling injectors,
it burns off inside the honeycomb itself and the temperature goes far
beyond the design figure. The ceramic melts, loses strength and cracks.
The second is a mechanical impact: hitting a rock or a high kerb, or a
hard landing on a rough road. The third is thermal shock: a red-hot
housing meets cold water and the block cracks from the difference.
After that the gas flow grinds the fragments into debris, and the debris
starts pouring about inside the housing.

## Telling this sound from its neighbours

| Sound | When it is heard | Source |
|---|---|---|
| A pouring rustle | Bumps, pulling away, braking | Ceramic debris in the housing |
| Thin sheet-metal rattle | 1,500–2,500 rpm, idle | [Heat shield](/en/articles/finding-a-rattling-heat-shield/) |
| A low rumble, “tractor” note | Constant, louder on the throttle | A hole in a pipe or silencer |
| A ringing knock on the floor | Over potholes, when the body rolls | A slack exhaust hanger |
| Metallic grinding | Only when braking | Brake pads |

The practical check takes a couple of minutes. With the car cold, tap the
converter housing with a rubber mallet or the flat of your hand: an intact
block answers dully, a broken one with a characteristic dry rattling
inside. The second method: with the engine warm, have a helper blip the
throttle sharply a few times in neutral while you listen at the tailpipe —
fine black debris may come out of it. The general catalogue of foreign
noises from below is on the symptom page
[rattling under the car](/en/symptoms/rattling-under-car/).

## Why it matters

The rustle itself is harmless; its consequences are not. The fragments do
not stay put: the gas flow pushes them towards the outlet, and sooner or
later the debris packs into a narrow section and sharply raises exhaust
back pressure. The car then loses power in the course of a single drive —
acceleration dies away, the engine stalls under load, fuel consumption
climbs.

The second risk applies to engines where the converter sits right against
the exhaust manifold. When the throttle closes on the overrun, some of the
abrasive debris can be drawn back into the cylinders and work there like
sandpaper; that is one explanation offered for bore scoring on certain
petrol engines. There are no guaranteed statistics here, but the risk is
real, and it is another argument against dragging the repair out.

The third scenario is simpler: a destroyed converter stops doing its job,
the rear lambda sensor registers it and the check engine light comes on.
An emissions test will not pass with that fault stored.

## How the repair is decided

The sensible order of steps: first a diagnostic scan, which shows codes
for converter efficiency, mixture and misfires. Understanding the cause of
the overheating matters, or the new part repeats the fate of the old one —
a converter that failed because of misfires and a leaking injector will
fail again in a year if only the converter is changed. Then an inspection
of the whole exhaust on a lift: alongside the converter workshops often
find a rotten silencer or a split flex pipe, and welding them all in one
visit costs less than three separate visits.

The choice of part is the second fork. An original converter, an aftermarket
one and a universal unit welded in differ in price, in how long they last
and in whether the rear lambda sensor will accept them; ask which of the
three you are being quoted and how the sensor will be handled. A quote
that answers only “we will fix the noise” is worth a follow-up question.
