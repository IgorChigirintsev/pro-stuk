There is a simple technique that splits every transmission noise into two
groups in a couple of seconds: press the clutch and listen. If the stray
sound disappears, you have just cut out half the possible causes — the
source is behind the clutch, in the gearbox. Here is why the technique
works, what exactly falls silent at that moment and which parts remain
under suspicion.

## Why the pedal switches the sound off

While the clutch is engaged, torque runs from the flywheel through the
disc to the gearbox input shaft. The shaft turns, and with it the
constant-mesh gears and the layshaft — even in neutral with the car
standing still. A pressed pedal breaks that chain: the disc moves away
from the flywheel, the input shaft stops, and everything that was making
noise inside the gearbox goes quiet.

That is exactly why the check is so informative. The engine keeps running,
the alternator and water pump turn as if nothing had happened — so if the
sound has gone, they have nothing to do with it. And conversely: a hum
that appears only with the pedal down points at the release bearing, and
the logic there is the exact opposite — the details are collected in
[signs of a worn clutch release bearing](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-clutch-release-bearing/)
and
[a noisy release bearing: can you drive?](/en/articles/noisy-release-bearing-can-you-drive/).

## How to run the check properly

Do the test on a warm engine — cold oil muffles the sounds and blurs the
picture:

1. Put the car on the handbrake, select neutral, start the engine.
2. Listen with the pedal up: hum, rustle, whine at idle.
3. Press the clutch to the floor and hold it for five seconds. The sound
   has gone — the source is in the gearbox.
4. Repeat two or three times to rule out coincidence.

The same technique works on the move: accelerate to the speed at which the
hum is audible, press the clutch and coast. If the sound remains, it comes
from the wheels and hubs rather than the transmission. Worth remembering
separately that an automatic has no pedal trick, and the diagnosis there
is built differently: the typical signals are covered in
[signs of a failing automatic transmission](/en/articles/signs-of-a-failing-automatic-transmission/).

## Who remains under suspicion

| Part | Character of the sound |
|---|---|
| Input shaft bearing | Even hum in neutral, gone when pressed |
| Output shaft bearings | Hum on the move in particular gears |
| Synchronisers | Not a hum but crunching when selecting a gear |
| Worn gears | A whine tied to a specific gear and speed |
| Low gearbox oil level | Hum grows on a warm gearbox and on long drives |

It helps to note not only that the sound disappears but also in which
gears it is loudest. A hum that is the same in every gear and in neutral
usually comes from the input shaft — it always turns. A hum heard only in,
say, third and fourth points at a particular pair of gears or an output
shaft bearing. That detail noticeably shortens the diagnosis, so it is
worth writing down before the workshop visit — the same logic is applied
in
[gearbox whine at a particular speed](/en/articles/gearbox-whine-at-a-particular-speed/).

The first thing checked is always the level and condition of the oil —
the cheapest theory and the commonest cause of a hum that appeared
suddenly. If the sound is not a hum but knocks and jolts at the moment of
a gear change, the list of suspects is different and is described in
[automatic gearbox jolts and clunks when shifting](/en/articles/automatic-gearbox-jolts-and-clunks-when-shifting/)
and
[crunching when selecting a gear](/en/articles/crunching-when-selecting-a-gear/).
The general catalogue of noises heard in the cabin on the move is on the
symptom page
[humming while driving](/en/symptoms/humming-while-driving/).

## How the repair is decided

The sensible sequence runs from cheap to expensive: the oil first, then
two or three weeks of watching, and only then dismantling. An input shaft
bearing hum is not in itself an emergency: people drive with it for years,
until it grows into a whine and crunching.

Two things mechanics do not always say. Opening a gearbox means removing
it, and that is the same labour as a clutch replacement — so if the
gearbox does come off, the clutch kit is replaced at the same time, or you
pay that labour twice within a year or two. And the oil specification
matters more than the brand: gearbox oils are not interchangeable, and the
wrong one can produce exactly the noise you are trying to cure. Ask what
is going in and against which specification it was chosen.
