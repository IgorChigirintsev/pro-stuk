A damper controls the movement of the spring — without it the suspension
becomes a trampoline that keeps bouncing after every bump. The problem is
that it dies gradually and invisibly: the driver adapts to the changing
behaviour week by week and at some point accepts as normal something that
is not. Here is what actually happens to the car, and to the money, if you
leave it as it is.

## What the car loses on the road

The main consequence is loss of contact between tyre and road. A bouncing
wheel spends part of its time in the air or under reduced load, and during
those moments it transmits neither braking nor cornering force. In
practice that looks like this:

- stopping distance grows, especially on uneven surfaces;
- the car floats in corners and answers the steering late;
- on the motorway it wanders and needs constant small corrections;
- the ABS intervenes earlier and more crudely, because the wheels are
  skipping;
- on undulating tarmac the body starts to build up its own rhythm.

Aquaplaning deserves a separate mention: a bouncing wheel clears water
less effectively, and the threshold at which the car starts to float
arrives sooner.

The insidious part is how smoothly the degradation happens. A damper loses
effectiveness over years, the driver adapts to the new behaviour — braking
earlier, entering corners more gently, holding the wheel more firmly on
the motorway — and never notices that the safety margin has already been
spent. The difference usually becomes obvious only after the repair, when
the car stops floating.

## What breaks next

| Part | What happens to it |
|---|---|
| Springs | Work in conditions they were not designed for, break more often |
| Control arm bushes | Destroyed by constant impact loads |
| Anti-roll bar links and bushes | Wear out in months rather than years |
| Strut top mounts | Take impact loads and start to crunch |
| Wheel bearings | Accelerated wear from alternating loads |
| Tyres | Patchy wear, a sawtooth pattern |

The last row is the most tangible in money. A set of tyres eaten away in
patches within a single season costs about as much as a pair of dampers,
sometimes more.

## Making sure they really are dead

The classic bounce test — press down on a wing with your weight and let go
— works less well than people think: a modern damper can pass it having
already lost half its effectiveness. It is more reliable to look at the
combination of signs: oil traces on the body, knocking over bumps, the
body rocking for more than one cycle after a speed hump, uneven tread wear,
changed behaviour on the motorway.

An instrumented test on a damper tester gives an objective picture but is
not available everywhere. The compromise is an inspection on a lift, where
the neighbouring parts get checked at the same time: it almost always
turns out that the dampers did not die alone. The set of noises this
conversation usually starts from is on the symptom page
[knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/).

## How the repair is decided

Dampers are replaced by axle. Different damping left and right causes
pulling under braking, and half the point of the repair is lost.

Two things belong in the estimate and are worth asking about. The strut
top mounts and bump stops: they are only accessible with the strut
dismantled, they wear along with the damper, and doing them later means
paying for the same disassembly twice. And wheel alignment afterwards —
not an option but a required step, because replacing struts changes the
geometry.

One more question that decides how the car will feel: whether the
replacements match the original specification. Mixing a stiff aftermarket
damper on one axle with the original on the other produces a car that
behaves differently front and rear, and that is harder to live with than
the tired set you started from.
