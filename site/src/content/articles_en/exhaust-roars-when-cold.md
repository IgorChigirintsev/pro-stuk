An exhaust roar after a cold start that fades as the engine warms is such
a characteristic signature that you can form a preliminary diagnosis from
it without a lift. That is how leaks through narrow gaps behave: while the
metal is cold the gap is open and gases escape with a roar; a warmed
system expands, the gap closes and the sound goes. Here is where such gaps
appear, how to confirm the theory in five minutes, and how the repair is
decided.

## Where a cold roar comes from

- **The exhaust manifold gasket.** The classic: a burnt gasket between the
  cylinder head and the manifold gives a rasping roar from above, under
  the bonnet. As the manifold expands the gap is clamped and the sound
  disappears — the full breakdown is in
  [signs of a burnt exhaust manifold gasket](/en/articles/burnt-exhaust-manifold-gasket-signs/).
- **A cracked manifold.** Cast manifolds crack from temperature swings —
  after driving through deep water, for instance. The behaviour is the
  same: it roars cold and is silent warm.
- **A micro-crack in the flex pipe.** The flexible section with a torn
  braid hisses and roars when cold and is louder under load — see
  [signs of a torn exhaust flex pipe](/en/articles/signs-of-a-torn-exhaust-flex-pipe/).
- **A joint after the converter.** A burnt flange gasket leaks until
  warm-up; the sound is lower and duller, heard near the floor.
- **The normal warm-up.** Raised revs for the first minutes are normal,
  but they make any of the leaks above louder and more obvious.

If the roar does not fade after warm-up but stays constant, it is more
likely a hole right through the system, covered in
[signs of a rotten silencer](/en/articles/signs-of-a-rotten-silencer/). And
if bangs on lift-off come with the roar, start from the symptom page
[exhaust backfire](/en/symptoms/exhaust-backfire/).

## Confirming the leak in five minutes

The check is done in the morning, on a fully cold car:

1. **Listen to the first seconds.** Have a helper start the engine while
   you stand at the open bonnet. A manifold roar is heard from above and
   comes with a rapid rasp in time with the cylinders.
2. **Look for soot.** Gases always leave a black trace where they escape.
   Inspect the manifold-to-head joint and the flanges with a torch — a
   soot trail identifies the leak better than any stethoscope.
3. **Time it.** Note how many minutes it takes for the sound to fade. Two
   or three minutes is a thin gap at the manifold; ten or more means a
   bigger one, and delaying is riskier.
4. **Check its link to load.** A torn flex pipe roars louder under
   acceleration even on a warm engine; a manifold gasket usually goes
   completely silent once warm.

If your ear is not giving you certainty, an old mechanic's trick helps: a
length of garden hose with one end at your ear and the other near
suspicious parts of the system (on a cold engine only) works as a
stethoscope and localises the leak to within centimetres.

One nuance: a cold engine produces more than exhaust roar. A knock in the
first seconds after starting can come from the engine itself — from
lifters to the timing chain; the lookalikes are collected on
[engine knock when cold](/en/symptoms/engine-knock-when-cold/).

## What to fix and when

| Cause | How the sound behaves | The repair |
|---|---|---|
| Manifold gasket | Roars 2–10 minutes after starting | New gasket; the studs decide the labour |
| Cracked manifold | Roars cold, sometimes a thin whistle | Welding or a new manifold |
| Flex pipe | Roars cold and under load | New flex section welded in |
| Joint, flange | Leaks near the floor until warm | Gasket and fasteners |
| Rotten silencer | Constant rumble, louder cold | Weld a patch or replace the box |

The general logic: the closer the leak is to the engine, the more the
labour costs and the less sense there is in waiting — a gap at the
manifold grows fastest.

## Why “it will clear when it warms up” is a bad plan

An exhaust leak is one of those faults that progress quietly. Gases at
hundreds of degrees gradually wash away the metal around the gap, and one
day a cold roar becomes a constant rumble. In parallel the engine's
accuracy suffers: air drawn in at the gap distorts the oxygen sensor
readings, fuel consumption rises, and on some cars the check engine light
comes on — so the metalwork repair gains an electronics hunt.

There is a seasonal argument too. In autumn a gap that closed after two
minutes of warm-up in summer starts roaring for ten, and in winter frost
the car wakes the neighbours. A repair at the first complaint in warm
weather is always easier: the fasteners still undo rather than being cut
off along with seized studs.
