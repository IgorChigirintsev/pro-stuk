A tie rod is the bar connecting the steering rack to the track rod end and
passing effort from the steering wheel to the wheel. At its inner end sits
an axial joint, screwed straight into the rack and hidden under a pleated
bellows. The part works in constant motion and, together with the track
rod ends, is the first to take impacts from the wheels, so on broken roads
it wears before most of the running gear. Here are the signs of wear in
increasing order, the checks you can do without a workshop, and what
decides the repair.

## Signs by stage of wear

| Stage | What you hear and feel | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Early | Occasional soft knock over small bumps | Watch, check at the next service |
| Middle | Constant chatter on washboard surfaces, slight steering play | Diagnosis within a couple of weeks |
| Late | A knock over any bump, pulling, wandering in a straight line | Replace without delay |
| Critical | Large steering play, the car floats in its lane | Stop driving it |

The first alarm is almost always audible: a fine chatter on rough tarmac
felt through the steering wheel. Over time free play at the rim joins it,
and you find yourself correcting even in a straight line. Similar sounds
come from other parts, so the general breakdown is on the symptom page
[knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/).

## Indirect signs that are easy to miss

- **The car pulls to one side.** A worn joint changes the steering
  geometry and the car stops holding a straight line.
- **Uneven tyre wear.** An eaten inner or outer tread edge on one side is
  a common result of play in a rod.
- **An imprecise centre.** After a turn the wheel does not return to the
  same place and straight-line running has to be hunted for.
- **Wandering under braking.** With play present, the wheel has freedom
  and changes direction slightly as the car dives.

Each of these has other explanations too — from tyre pressure to
alignment. But combined with a knock they add up to the characteristic
picture of worn steering joints.

## How to check tie rods yourself

The most informative test needs a helper and five minutes. The car is on
the ground with the engine running. The helper rocks the steering left and
right through 10–15 degrees in short movements while you place a palm on
the rack bellows where the rod enters the housing. Thumps and clicks under
your palm mean play in the inner joint. Then move your hand to the track
rod end: that separates the two parts, which are often confused — their
own breakdown is in
[signs of worn track rod ends](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-track-rod-ends/).

The second test is with the wheel raised. Take the wheel at the 9 and 3
o'clock positions and rock it towards and away from you. A knock with
noticeable free movement points at the steering joints — the rod or the
end; play at 6 and 12 is a different story, the hub or the ball joint. The
detailed procedure is in
[checking track rod ends for play](/en/articles/checking-track-rod-ends-for-play/).

It is important not to confuse a rod with the rack itself: clearance in
the rack mechanism also gives a knock when the steering is rocked. A rack
that hums when you turn is a different fault again, covered in
[power steering whine when turning](/en/articles/power-steering-whine-when-turning/).

## How the repair is decided

Two things are worth settling before the work starts.

First, the alignment. It is a compulsory line in the estimate, not an
option: the length of the new rod sets the toe angle, and without the rack
the car will pull and wear tyres.

Second, the bellows. The rack bellows has to come off to reach the inner
joint, and a bellows that is already cracked should be replaced while it
is off — refitting a torn one lets water into the rack and turns a cheap
job into a rack rebuild later. Ask whether a new bellows and clamps are in
the quote.

Replacing rods in pairs is not compulsory: if the other side has no play,
one is enough. But if the car has covered more than a hundred thousand
kilometres and one joint is finished, the other is usually close behind —
sometimes it is more sensible to close the question in one visit and one
alignment.

## What driving with worn rods leads to

A worn tie rod does not create “the wheel will fall off” danger until the
final stage: the joint knocks first, then develops play, and only with
extreme wear can it separate — at which point the wheel stops obeying the
steering. Long before that come the smaller costs: an unevenly eaten set
of tyres, lost alignment, tiring corrections on the motorway. A separate
risk is a torn bellows: water and dust kill even a fresh joint within a
season, which is why inspecting boots is part of any running gear
diagnosis.
