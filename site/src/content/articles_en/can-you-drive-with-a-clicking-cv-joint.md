Clicking as you turn the wheel under power is a sound almost every
long-standing owner of a front-wheel-drive car has heard. The question is
usually not “what is it” but “how much longer can I drive like this”. The
honest answer: for a while you can, but that margin is not measured in
years and it depends on the stage the joint has reached. Here is how to
judge the stage yourself and where the line is beyond which you should not
drive.

## What happens inside the joint

A CV joint — a constant velocity joint — transmits torque from the gearbox
to the wheel through a changing angle. Inside are balls in cages, packed
with special grease and sealed by a rubber boot. While the boot is intact
the unit lasts a very long time: the grease stays in, sand and water stay
out.

Practically all CV joint wear starts with a crack in the boot. Centrifugal
force gradually throws the grease out, abrasive gets in, the cage tracks
wear, play appears — and at large steering angles under load the balls
start skipping over the worn tracks. That is the clicking.

## Three stages and the time each leaves you

| Stage | Signs | How much time |
|---|---|---|
| Early | Barely audible clicking only at full lock under power | Weeks, planned repair |
| Middle | Clear clicking in any tight turn, starts clicking in reverse | Days to weeks, book the workshop |
| Late | Knocking in a straight line, vibration, jerking when pulling away | Do not drive except to the workshop |

The difference between the stages is not loudness in itself but which
regimes produce the sound. While the clicking only lives at large angles
under load, the joint is still coping with straight-line driving. As soon
as the sound appears in a straight line and vibration joins it, the wear
has reached the point where failure is a matter of the near future. The
inner joint, incidentally, knocks differently — not in a turn but under
acceleration.

## How to drive while you wait for the repair

If you have decided to drive there yourself, it makes sense to reduce the
load on the worn joint:

1. **Do not turn the wheel to full lock under power.** That combination
   loads the cages the most. On a car park, manoeuvre in short movements.
2. **Pull away gently.** A sharp start puts a shock load through the whole
   driveshaft.
3. **Avoid the motorway.** At high speed a worn joint gets hotter and
   breaks up faster.
4. **Do not spin the wheels.** Wheelspin in snow or mud with the wheels
   turned is the fastest way to finish a CV joint off.

It is also worth making sure the source has been identified correctly.
Clicking in a turn does not come only from joints: strut top mounts and
steering components sound similar. The catalogue of such sounds and how to
separate them is on the symptom page
[clicking when steering](/en/symptoms/clicking-when-steering/), and the
confirmation test is in
[checking a CV joint by driving circles](/en/articles/checking-a-cv-joint-by-driving-circles/).

## How the repair is decided

The first fork is the joint or the whole driveshaft. If the splines and
the shaft are sound, the joint alone is enough and the part is cheaper. If
the mileage is high and the shaft already vibrates, workshops often
suggest the complete assembly — the labour is the same and there is no
repeat visit six months later. The decision is made once the shaft is off
and inspected, so the final figure is named during the job, not before it.

The second is whether a boot alone will do. While there is no clicking, a
new boot with the joint cleaned and repacked with fresh grease is a
fraction of the cost of a new joint — which is exactly why the boots are
worth checking at every service. Once clicking has started, a new boot
only seals in a joint that is already worn, and the noise comes back.

The third is a question worth asking outright: what grease is going in.
CV joints use a specific molybdenum-based grease, and the wrong one
shortens the life of an otherwise good repair.
