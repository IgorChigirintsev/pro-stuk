A frequent complaint at diagnosis: the cold engine runs smoothly in the
morning, but once it reaches operating temperature a knock or tick
appears. That scenario is the opposite of the "morning" knock which goes
away with warm-up, and it has a different list of suspects: nearly all of
them are connected in some way with lubrication. Here is why a hot engine
knocks more readily than a cold one and how to narrow the cause down by
the behaviour of the sound.

## Why warming up changes the engine's voice

The main factor is the oil. Heated to a working 90–100 °C it becomes many
times more fluid: the pressure in the lubrication system falls, especially
at idle, and the oil film in the friction pairs gets thinner. While the
engine is cold, thick oil hides worn clearances; when hot they are left
alone with thin lubricant and start to knock.

The second factor is geometry. In some friction pairs the clearance
actually shrinks with heat: an aluminium piston skirt expands faster than
an iron block, which is why "piston" knock is usually heard in the morning
and fades with warm-up. If the sound behaves the other way round —
appearing when hot — suspicion shifts to the parts that live on an oil
film: the crankshaft bearings (plain bearings) and the hydraulic lifters.
The reverse scenario is covered on the symptom page
[engine knock when cold](/en/symptoms/engine-knock-when-cold/) and in
[an engine knock only when cold](/en/articles/engine-knock-only-when-cold/).

## Common causes: from oil to bearings

**Hydraulic lifters** — small plungers in the valve drive that use oil
pressure to take up the thermal clearance. When hot they tick if the oil
is old or too thin: warmed lubricant is pushed more easily through worn
plungers and the lifter collapses. The sound is a bright tick from above,
from under the cam cover —
[signs of valve clatter](/en/articles/signs-of-valve-clatter/) and
[how to check hydraulic lifters](/en/articles/how-to-check-hydraulic-lifters/).

**Crankshaft bearings** — the most worrying option. A dull knock from deep
in the block grows when hot, because thin oil no longer holds the load
wedge in an enlarged clearance. The full breakdown of that scenario is in
[signs of big end bearing knock](/en/articles/signs-of-big-end-bearing-knock/)
and
[signs of main bearing knock](/en/articles/signs-of-main-bearing-knock/).

**Detonation** — explosive combustion of the mixture — also favours a hot
engine: the higher the temperature in the chambers, the more easily it
occurs. A bright pinging on acceleration in heat or uphill is its
signature; the details are in
[signs of engine detonation](/en/articles/signs-of-engine-detonation/).

**Exhaust heat shields** — tin shields expand with heat, and loose
fasteners start rattling at certain engine speeds. It sounds alarming and
costs next to nothing.

| Sound when hot | Likely source | Urgency |
|---|---|---|
| Ticking, clicking from above | Hydraulic lifters, oil | Scheduled diagnosis |
| Dull knock from deep down, grows with load | Crankshaft bearings | To a workshop immediately |
| Bright pinging on acceleration | Detonation | Check fuel and cooling |
| Tinny rattle over part of the rev range | Exhaust heat shield | Not dangerous, fix when convenient |
| Knock plus the oil light at idle | Oil pressure | Stop the engine, recovery truck |

## The role of the oil: viscosity, level, age

Before thinking about mechanics it is worth ruling out the oil itself. The
typical situations in which the oil is what creates a hot knock:

- **viscosity outside the specification** — for example, thin
  energy-saving oil in a high-mileage engine designed for something
  thicker;
- **the oil has served its time** — with age it loses viscosity and
  additives, and a hot engine is the first to report it with a tick;
- **a low level** — in heat and in traffic, a shortage of oil raises the
  temperature of what remains and drops the pressure;
- **summer overheating** — in a heatwave the oil temperature in traffic is
  noticeably higher than on the motorway, so the knock often appears "in
  town in summer".

If the knock coincided in time with an oil change or a service, start by
checking what was put in and how much. That is cheaper than any diagnosis.
For engines past two hundred thousand kilometres a shortened change
interval is reasonable insurance: oil in a worn engine ages faster, and
its viscosity for such engines often makes sense at the upper end of the
specification — a question worth discussing with an engine specialist who
knows that particular unit.
[Signs of low oil pressure](/en/articles/signs-of-low-oil-pressure/) is
worth reading alongside.

## Narrowing the cause without stripping anything

The observations take ten minutes and need no tools, but they are what
separates a specific workshop visit from guesswork. It helps to do them in
a quiet place with the bonnet open, on a fully warmed engine.

1. **Revs.** On a warm engine raise the revs smoothly to 2000. The knock
   disappeared — hydraulic lifters or oil pressure are likely; it sped up
   with the revs — the source is in the crank mechanism.
2. **Load.** Note whether the sound grows on acceleration and uphill. A
   knock "under throttle" argues for bearings or detonation.
3. **Location.** From above — the valve drive; from deep down and below —
   the crankshaft; from behind and below — possibly the exhaust.
4. **The oil pressure light.** If it comes on or flickers at hot idle,
   that points straight at the lubrication system and you should not drive
   any further.
5. **Idle only.** If the knock is heard only at idle and disappears on the
   move, the scenarios are different — see
   [an engine knock at idle](/en/articles/engine-knock-at-idle/).

## How the repair is decided

The order runs from the cheapest answer to the most expensive one, and
each step rules something out. Fresh oil of the correct specification with
a new filter comes first — it closes a noticeable share of hot ticks by
itself. If the sound remains, the next step is a mechanical oil pressure
measurement: that single figure separates the lifters from the bearings
and is worth far more than any opinion. Only after it does anyone open the
engine.

Two things worth asking. What the pressure measured, in numbers, at hot
idle and at 2000 rpm — and how that compares with the manufacturer's
figure. And, if lifters are proposed, whether the oil history explains
their state: a fresh set fed by the same neglected oil regime ticks again
within a year.

## When to stop the engine at once

Three combinations demand stopping without discussion: a dull growing
knock from deep in the block; any knock together with the oil pressure
light on; metal shavings or glitter on the dipstick. In those cases a
recovery truck is cheaper than "just making it there": the cost of a spun
bearing is in a different league. In all other cases it is enough to book
a diagnosis within the next few days and not to load the engine on the
way. The general breakdown of engine knocks is on the symptom page
[knocking in the engine](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-engine/).
