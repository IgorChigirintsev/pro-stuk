A short metallic rattle in the first seconds after a cold start is the
best-known complaint from owners of early chain-driven 1.4 TSI turbo
engines. The sound lasts a second or two, then the engine runs smoothly,
and it is exactly that brevity which lulls you: it seems to have grumbled
and stopped. In reality this is a conversation not about noise but about
the reserve left in the drive. Here is where the rattle comes from, how to
tell it from other knocks and what the fix involves.

## Why it rattles at start-up specifically

The timing chain is tensioned by a hydraulic tensioner that works off oil
pressure. While the engine stands the oil drains, the pressure in the
system falls, and the tensioner loses part of its push. At the moment of
starting the chain is briefly left slack: it slaps against the guides and
dampers, and you hear the characteristic chatter. A couple of seconds
later the pressure is restored, the chain is tensioned, the sound goes.

While the chain is new the effect is barely noticeable. With mileage the
chain's pins and rollers wear, its effective length grows, and the
tensioner extends further and further to compensate for the slack. When
the tensioner's travel is nearly used up there is nothing left to
compensate with — the rattle at start-up gets longer and appears more
often, including on a warm engine on a sharp lift-off.

Oil is a separate factor. Thickened, worn-out oil or oil of the wrong
viscosity takes longer to build pressure, and the "weak tensioner" stage
is stretched out. That is not a cause of wear, but it is a noticeable
accelerator.

## Telling the chain from other knocks

| Sound | When it is heard | Likely source |
|---|---|---|
| Chattering rattle 1–2 sec after start-up | Only when cold | Chain and tensioner |
| Even ticking, rises with revs | Constantly | Hydraulic lifters, valves |
| Bright knock under load | On acceleration | Detonation, fuel, plugs |
| Hum and whine, unrelated to starting | At revs | Accessories, idlers, water pump |
| Knock from below when pulling away | Over bumps | Engine mounts, exhaust |

## What a workshop checks

Diagnosis runs from simple to complex. First they listen to the engine at
start-up and on lift-off, then read the codes from the camshaft and
crankshaft position sensors: a cam timing offset is direct evidence for a
stretched chain. Next the top cover comes off and the position of the
tensioner plunger and the state of the guides are assessed: a plunger
extended a long way and pressed-through plastic guides are visible at
once. How the same check is done by ear is in
[checking timing chain tension by ear](/en/articles/checking-timing-chain-tension-by-ear/).

Importantly, the answer is almost never "tighten it". The tensioner is not
manually adjustable, and wear of the guides and chain is irreversible. So
the outcome is one of two: either the chain is still within limits and you
watch it, or the drive is replaced as a kit.

## How the repair is decided

The criterion is the measurement, not the sound: the cam timing offset
read with a scan tool and the extension of the tensioner plunger. While
both are within limits, the sensible course is to watch — record the
start-up and compare it a month later. Once the offset is outside
tolerance or the plunger is near the end of its travel, the drive is
replaced as a kit.

Two things worth knowing before agreeing to the work. First, the strip-down
is the bulk of the labour, so saving money by replacing only the tensioner
means paying almost the same labour again when the chain finally goes —
ask what the chain measured before choosing. Second, the guides and
dampers are plastic and are what the chain actually beats against; a kit
without them is not a repair. Ask to see the old parts: pressed-through
guides tell the story of the oil regime more honestly than any estimate.

Oil deserves a separate mention. Regular changes at the recommended
viscosity do not make a chain eternal, but they noticeably slow the wear
of its joints and shorten that second of weak tension at start-up. The
habit of topping up rather than changing costs the most here: how the
drive works depends directly on how fast the system builds pressure.
