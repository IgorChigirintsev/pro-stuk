Hydraulic power steering is a pump, driven by a belt from the engine, that
moves fluid and helps turn the wheels. When the system is healthy it works
almost silently. A whine or hum when turning is the most common signal
that something is getting in its way, and in most cases the cause is
inexpensive — provided you do not leave it. Below are the causes by
frequency and the simple checks you can do without a workshop.

## Where the whine comes from

The source is almost always the pump. Three things make it whine: not
enough fluid, air in the system, or wear in the pump itself. Turning the
wheel raises system pressure and the load on the pump — which is why the
sound appears or grows in corners.

It is important to separate normal from faulty. A short hum with the wheel
at full lock is the relief valve doing its job; even healthy cars behave
that way. The worrying signs are different:

- the whine is there on **any** steering input, not only at full lock;
- the sound **grows with engine revs**;
- the steering has become **heavier**, or the effort is uneven;
- the fluid in the reservoir is **dark, smells burnt or is foaming**.

## Causes by frequency

| Cause | How it shows | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Low fluid level | Whine when turning, heavier steering | Top up, find the leak |
| Air in the system | Whine, foam in the reservoir, jerky effort | Bleed it, find where air gets in |
| Old fluid | Whine when cold, dark colour | Change the fluid |
| Pump wear | Constant whine, grows with revs | Repair or replace the pump |
| A loose drive belt | Squeal plus whine under load | Tighten or replace |
| Worn steering rack | Whine together with knocking | Inspection on a lift |

The first three are the most common and the cheapest. A pump rarely whines
on its own account — it usually wears out after long service on a low
level or on old fluid.

The belt is a special case. On most cars the power steering pump is driven
by the same accessory belt as the alternator, so a slipping belt produces
both a squeal and a drop in system pressure. How that fault sounds is
covered on the symptom page [belt squeal](/en/symptoms/belt-squeal/).

## Checking it yourself

Three steps that need no pit and no tools:

1. **The reservoir.** With the engine cold, check the level between the
   MIN and MAX marks. Assess the fluid while you are there: fresh fluid is
   clear, reddish or greenish; dark brown with a burnt smell means it
   needs changing. Foam or bubbles mean air in the system.
2. **The stationary test.** With the engine running, turn the wheel from
   lock to lock while parked. If the whine is just as audible standing
   still, the hydraulics are involved rather than the suspension. If you
   hear clicks or knocking instead of a whine, that is a different story.
3. **The moving test.** If the sound appears only in corners while driving
   and the wheel turns quietly when parked, the power steering may be
   innocent: a loaded wheel bearing produces a similar hum in corners. The
   differences are on the symptom page
   [humming when turning](/en/symptoms/humming-when-turning/).

## Hydraulic or electric: check which one you have

A point that matters on newer cars: a large share of 2010s models and
practically every mainstream new car uses electric power steering rather
than hydraulic. It has no pump and no fluid reservoir, so the classic
hydraulic whine does not belong to it: electric steering either works
quietly or, when it has problems, buzzes with its motor, becomes heavy in
steps and lights the steering warning symbol on the dash.

Checking which you have is easy: look under the bonnet for a fluid
reservoir with a steering-wheel pictogram on the cap. No reservoir means
electric steering, and advice about levels and bleeding does not apply;
its diagnosis goes through reading fault codes. There is an in-between
variant too — electro-hydraulic steering, where an electric motor drives
the pump: it can whine in the hydraulic manner and throw electrical fault
codes at the same time.

## How the repair is decided

Start cheap. Fresh fluid and bleeding the system solve a noticeable share
of whining cases; replacing the pump without checking the level and the
condition of the fluid is paying for the symptom rather than the cause.

If the pump does turn out to be worn, one question is worth asking: what
happens to the rest of the system. A worn pump sends metal debris
downstream, so a decent workshop flushes the system rather than simply
bolting on a new pump — otherwise the debris left inside goes to work on
the new one and on the rack seals. Ask about the flush before you agree to
the job; it is not an upsell, it is what makes the repair last.

## Can you drive with a whining pump

For a short time, yes — the car remains controllable. But two processes
are working against you. First, a pump whining because of low fluid or air
wears out fast, and within weeks a simple problem turns into a
replacement. Second, a worn pump pushes metal debris through the system,
which finishes off the rack seals — and the rack is the most expensive
part of the steering. So treat a whine as a reason to look in the
reservoir within a day or two, not to “watch it for a month”.
