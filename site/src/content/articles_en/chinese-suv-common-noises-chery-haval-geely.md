Chery, Haval and Geely have taken a visible share of the roads within a
few years, and a fairly recognisable set of complaints came into workshops
with them. Breaking those down by exact model and year is thankless work:
trim levels change and so do component suppliers. It is far more useful to
understand which groups of noises are typical of this fleet and why, so
you do not pay for searching where there is nothing to find.

## Where the typical noises come from

The main factor is age. Most of these cars are under five years old, and
that shapes the whole picture. The wear that produces “grown-up” noises —
knocking bearings, a stretched timing chain, a collapsed catalytic
converter — does not usually arrive by that mileage. What is very audible
instead are things that would simply be lost in the background on an older
car: a settled bush, a loose clamp, a rattling trim panel.

The second factor is good cabin insulation. The quieter the car, the more
noticeable any foreign note. The owner hears what would have gone
unnoticed on a noisier car of the same class, and quite reasonably goes to
find out what it is.

The third is roads. A large part of the fleet lives on city expansion
joints and patched tarmac, and that is exactly the load that tires small
joints and rubber components fastest. The same car used on motorways and
in a city cycle reaches its first knock tens of thousands of kilometres
apart.

## Suspension is the most common group

That is where most enquiries land. The typical scenario: in the second or
third year a dry click or knock appears over small bumps — slab joints,
speed humps, patched surfaces. In the vast majority of cases the first
candidate is the anti-roll bar links (the thin rods connecting the
anti-roll bar to the strut). They are a consumable across the whole class,
not just on Chinese brands, and they are cheap to replace.

Next on the list come strut top mounts, control arm bushes
(rubber-and-metal sleeves that damp vibration) and subframe mountings. The
order to work through is the same as on any modern SUV, and it is set out
in
[finding a suspension knock without a lift](/en/articles/finding-a-suspension-knock-without-a-lift/).
The general sound portrait is on the symptom page
[knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/).

## What else makes noise: engine, gearbox, cabin

| Source | Typical sound | Where to start checking |
|---|---|---|
| Accessory belt and idlers | Squeal at a cold start, chirp under load | Inspect the belt, tensioner, idlers |
| Dual-clutch gearbox | Hum and clicks at low speed, jerking when pulling away | Dealer diagnosis, adaptation |
| Brake pads | Squeak at low speed, wear indicator chirp | Measure pad and disc thickness |
| Exhaust heat shields | Rattle at particular revs | Look underneath, tighten the fasteners |
| Cabin plastics, trim | Creaks in the cold and over bumps | Localise with a palm, check seals |

Belt squeal deserves a separate mention: on turbocharged engines with
tightly packed accessories it is especially audible and often written off
as “Chinese quality”, though the cause is the usual one — tension, wear or
water on the pulley grooves. The sound portrait is on the symptom page
[belt squeal](/en/symptoms/belt-squeal/), and telling a belt from the
units it drives is covered in
[whine under the bonnet: alternator or water pump](/en/articles/alternator-or-water-pump-whine/).

## The order of action

If the car is under warranty, the first step is always the same: have the
noise recorded by the dealer on the work order. Even if the verdict is
“within normal limits”, you are left with a dated document. Repairing a
component yourself when it later turns out to be a warranty case usually
closes off any claim.

The second point is what to insist on being checked before parts are
replaced. A knock over bumps is diagnosed on a lift with a pry bar, not by
guesswork: the mechanic loads each joint and looks for movement. If you
are offered a set of parts without that check, ask which joint showed play
and how it was found.

Before the visit it helps to arrive with specifics rather than “something
is knocking”: at what speed, on what surface, cold engine or warm. You can
record the sound and break it down by conditions in the Stuk app — it
matches the recording with your answers to a few questions and shows the
likely causes with percentages, which makes the conversation at the
service desk noticeably easier.
