You press on a wing and the body answers with a creak, like an old sofa.
Sometimes the same sound is heard on the move: over undulations, speed
humps, entering a car park. A creak frightens people less than a knock,
and broadly rightly so: it is the sound of friction, not impact. But it
has its own sources, its own search method and a couple of situations
where it should not be left. Here they are in order.

## What a creak actually tells you

A creak is friction between two surfaces that should either slide with
lubrication or not move at all. In a suspension that is almost always
rubber: hardened, dried out or torn. The important difference from a knock
is that a creaking joint does not yet have significant play, so a creak
is often an earlier and cheaper stage of the same problem. If rocking the
car produces both a creak and dull thumps, the play is already there, and
that is the profile of the symptom page
[knocking in the suspension](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-suspension/).

## The main sources

| Source | How it sounds | The repair |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-roll bar bushes | Creak when rocked and over undulations | Cheap part, quick job |
| Control arm bushes | Creak with a knock in potholes | Pressed out or the arm replaced |
| Strut top mount | Creak from above, and when steering | Strut stripped, done with the damper |
| Spring isolators | Creak when rocking one corner | Cheap while the strut is apart |
| Ball joint without grease | Creak in turns while driving | Replaced, not greased |

The leader is the anti-roll bar bushes: rubber rings in which the bar
rotates as the body rolls. When their rubber hardens, every movement of
the bar comes with a rubbery creak — see
[signs of worn anti-roll bar bushes](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-anti-roll-bar-bushes/).
Second most common are the control arm bushes: their creak means the
joint's rubber has started to tear or wear against its sleeve, as covered
in
[signs of worn suspension bushes](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-suspension-bushes/).

## How to find the creaking point

The search is easier with two people: one rhythmically rocks a corner by
the wing or bumper, the other listens from below — an ear at wheel-arch
level is usually enough to separate “at the front, centrally” (the
anti-roll bar) from “at the top of the strut” (the top mount, covered in
[a noisy strut top mount bearing](/en/articles/strut-top-mount-bearing-noise-symptoms/)).

Then the simple tests:

1. **Water.** Pour water from a bottle over the anti-roll bar bushes. If
   the creak changes or stops temporarily, the source is found.
2. **Silicone grease.** Treat the suspect rubber component; silence for a
   few days confirms the diagnosis. Petroleum-based greases are not
   suitable for this test — they destroy rubber.
3. **The steering.** A creak when turning the wheel while parked points at
   the strut top mount or a ball joint rather than the anti-roll bar.

If rocking produces a metallic rattle as well as a creak, look for a loose
fastener rather than rubber — that case is covered in
[metallic clatter under the car over bumps](/en/articles/metallic-clatter-under-the-car-over-bumps/).

## Creaking and safety

Rubber creak in itself is a comfort question: you can drive with it for
months, and the only real damage is to the driver's nerves. Planning to
replace the bushes at the next workshop visit is sensible, all the more so
because it is one of the cheapest suspension repairs there is.

But there are two exceptions. The first is a creaking ball joint: a joint
left without grease wears quickly and unpredictably, and its final stage
is wheel play —
[signs of a worn ball joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-ball-joint/). The
second is a creak that has gained a distinct knock over time: the rubber
is no longer merely rubbing but letting parts move. In both cases it helps
to judge the timescale soberly — see
[can you drive with a knocking suspension](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-a-knocking-suspension/)
and the symptom page
[knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/).

A suspension inspection on a lift is one of the cheapest things a workshop
does, and it is often free when you book the repair — for a creak that is
usually enough, because the source is found in minutes by rocking and
looking at the rubber.
