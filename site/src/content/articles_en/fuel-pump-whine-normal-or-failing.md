A fuel pump always makes noise — it is electric, it sits inside the tank
and it pushes fuel at several atmospheres of pressure. The question is not
whether you can hear it but whether the sound has changed. An even
background hum you only notice in silence is normal. A distinct whine
audible over the radio while driving, changing with the level of fuel in
the tank, is a signal to start checking.

## What the pump sounds like when healthy

The standard picture looks like this. Turn the key to the ignition
position and a short buzz of two or three seconds comes from under the
rear seats or the boot floor — the pump builds pressure in the rail and
switches off. Once the engine is running it works continuously, but its
sound is lost in the general background.

Loudness depends on things that have nothing to do with health: on an
almost empty tank the pump is noticeably easier to hear than on a full
one, because fuel damps the sound; in a quiet garage it also seems
louder. So a one-off “mine is humming” means nothing by itself — what
matters is the trend.

## Signs the pump really is on its way out

| Sign | What it means |
|---|---|
| The whine has grown noticeably over weeks or months | Pump wear, or resistance from a blocked filter |
| The sound changes sharply on an almost empty tank | The pump is drawing air and running without cooling |
| Flat spots under load, on climbs, when overtaking | Not enough pressure at high demand |
| Difficult hot starting, long cranking | Pressure dropping, or a leaking check valve |
| Jerking at a steady speed | Unstable fuel delivery |
| Silence when you turn the ignition on | Pump, relay or wiring failure |

One loud pump with not a single sign from the lower part of the table is
most often just age-related noise. A whine combined with flat spots and
hard hot starting is a genuine warning of imminent failure, and that one
should not be left.

It helps to separate this sound from others. Fuel pump hum comes from the
rear, from the tank area, follows the ignition, and has nothing to do with
road speed. A hum that grows with speed is the chassis, and its portrait
is on the symptom page
[humming while driving](/en/symptoms/humming-while-driving/). A whine from
under the bonnet tied to engine revs is the accessory drive. Chatter from
the top, out of the valve cover, is a different family again — see
[ticking engine](/en/symptoms/ticking-engine/).

## What gets checked first

The right sequence starts not with the pump but with whatever is stopping
it from working.

The fuel filter comes first. A blocked filter makes the pump work under
overload: the noise grows and its life shortens while the pump itself is
still healthy. On many cars the filter sits inside the pump module and is
replaced together with the pre-filter screen — that screen is worth
checking too, as it clogs with sediment from the bottom of the tank.

Next is a fuel pressure test with a gauge: at idle, on a sharp throttle
opening, and after switching the ignition off to see whether the system
holds pressure. The voltage at the pump connector is checked as well:
degraded wiring or a burnt relay causes weak delivery with a perfectly
healthy pump, and replacing the pump in that case achieves nothing.

Only when the filter is clean, the voltage is right and the pressure still
falls short does the question move to the pump itself.

## How the repair is decided

Two things are worth clarifying before agreeing to any work. The first is
what exactly is being replaced: on many cars the pump comes as a complete
module with the level sender and the housing, on others the pump element
alone can be changed. The difference in price is large, and it is a fair
question to ask.

The second is access. On some cars the pump is reached through a hatch
under the rear seat — a short job. On others the tank has to be dropped,
which means draining the fuel and considerably more labour. Knowing which
of the two your car is explains an estimate that would otherwise look
strange.

A useful habit for the future: do not run down to the warning light
routinely, and change the fuel filter at the recommended interval. Those
are the cheapest ways to extend a pump's life.
