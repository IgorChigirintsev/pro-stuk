An automatic gearbox should shift imperceptibly — ideally the driver only
learns about a gear change from the revs dropping. When instead there is a
jolt in the back and a dull clunk somewhere under the floor, it is always
worth investigating. But not always worth panicking about: some causes are
cured by replacing a mount in a couple of hours, some really do mean
opening the gearbox. Here is how to tell them apart.

## What exactly clunks during a shift

A jolt and a clunk are different things, and it helps to separate them. A
jolt is a jerk you feel through your body: the gearbox takes up or drops
the torque abruptly. A clunk is the sound of an impact, usually metallic
or dull, arriving from under the floor or the bulkhead.

They often come as a pair, and the connection is direct. The gearbox
passes the torque jerk into the power unit, the unit rocks on its mounts —
and if they are worn, it hits its limiters. So the source of the jerk is
inside the gearbox and the source of the clunk is outside it. That is
exactly why a diagnosis starts with the mounts: they are cheaper, more
accessible and wear noticeably faster than a valve body —
[signs of worn engine mounts](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-engine-mounts/).

## The order of checks, from simple to complex

1. **Engine and gearbox mounts.** With the car stationary and the brake
   applied, shift from D to R and back. A sharp clunk through the body
   with a mild jerk almost certainly means the mounts —
   [checking engine mounts under load](/en/articles/checking-engine-mounts-under-load/).
2. **Fluid level and colour.** Dark fluid smelling of burning, and a level
   below the mark, explain half of all late and harsh engagements.
3. **Cold and warm gearbox.** Jolts only when cold are usually the
   hydraulics and fluid viscosity. Jolts on a warm gearbox are more
   serious.
4. **A scan tool.** Fault codes for the solenoids and speed sensors often
   produce exactly the picture that feels like a clunk.

A manual gearbox behaves differently with similar symptoms, and the search
logic there is its own — it is covered in
[a knock when changing gear](/en/articles/knock-when-changing-gear/). If
the clunk is heard not at the moment of a shift but under acceleration
generally, it is more useful to start with the symptom page
[knocking when accelerating](/en/symptoms/knocking-when-accelerating/) and
with
[a driveline clunk on and off the throttle](/en/articles/driveline-clunk-on-and-off-the-throttle/).

## A table of causes

| What is clunking | When it shows itself | How urgent |
|---|---|---|
| Engine or gearbox mount | On D/R changes, pulling away, over bumps | Scheduled repair |
| Valve body, solenoids | Delay and a thump on the 1–2 and 2–3 shifts | Diagnosis within weeks |
| Worn clutches | Slipping, revs rising without acceleration | Urgent, driving inadvisable |
| Old fluid | Jolts when warm, wandering revs | Check first |
| CV joint, driveshaft | Clunk when pulling away, clicking in turns | A separate story from the gearbox |
| Wheel bearing | Hum at speed, unconnected with shifts | See its own diagnosis |

The last two rows are a reminder that not everything clunking near the
moment of a shift belongs to the gearbox. The coincidence in timing can be
accidental: a hum you attribute to the transmission often turns out to be
the tyres, and separating them is covered in
[humming: wheel bearing or tyres](/en/articles/hum-wheel-bearing-or-tyres/).
The full symptom list for the gearbox itself is in
[signs of a failing automatic transmission](/en/articles/signs-of-a-failing-automatic-transmission/).

## How the repair is decided

The order above is the decision procedure, and it is deliberately built
from the cheap and reversible to the expensive and irreversible. Mounts
and fluid first, scan tool second, the inside of the gearbox last. Valve
body work and a rebuild are left out of any estimate on purpose: the
spread is too wide, and a figure quoted without a diagnosis of your
specific gearbox is false precision.

It is also worth budgeting time for observation. An automatic is the one
unit where rushing into a repair costs more than a couple of weeks of
careful driving with notes: many adaptive gearboxes change their behaviour
after an adaptation reset or a fluid change not immediately but over
several hundred kilometres. So mechanics usually ask you to come back for
a second diagnosis, and that is not an attempt to stretch the bill.

Two things mechanics do not always say. The fluid specification matters
more than the brand — the wrong friction modifier gives exactly the harsh
shifts you are trying to cure, so ask which specification is going in. And
if a fluid change is refused at high mileage, that is usually caution
rather than laziness: fresh fluid washes deposits that were holding
worn-out clutches together.
