A squeal from under the bonnet in the first seconds after turning the key
is one of the commonest sounds people bring to a workshop. Almost always
its source is the accessory drive: the belt that turns the alternator, the
water pump and the air conditioning compressor, along with the tensioner,
idlers and pulleys of that drive. Here is why the squeal appears at
start-up specifically and how to tell one cause from another without
taking anything apart.

## Why it squeals at the moment of starting

A squeal is the sound of slipping: the rubber belt cannot keep up with the
metal pulley and rubs against it. At start-up several conditions come
together at once.

First, the starter spins the crankshaft with a jerk — the load on the belt
grows instantly from zero to full. Second, the alternator works at its
limit in those first seconds: it is replacing the charge the battery spent
on starting, and its pulley resists rotation noticeably harder than usual.
Third, overnight the belt cools and stiffens while moisture condenses on
the pulleys — the grip between rubber and metal gets worse.

A healthy drive survives those seconds in silence. A squeal means there is
no grip in reserve: the belt is worn, the tension has slackened, or one of
the driven units resists rotation more than it should.

The symptom is seasonal too. The first squeals usually appear in autumn
with the night frosts, become regular in winter, and "go away by
themselves" in spring. They go away only to the ear, of course: the wear
that made the belt squeal at −10 °C has not gone anywhere and will show up
louder next time the cold arrives.

## Five typical sources

- **A worn belt.** The ribs wear down, the working face glazes over,
  cracks appear. Such a belt slips even at normal tension.
- **A weak tensioner.** The spring of an automatic tensioner tires over
  time and the belt sags. A squeal at start-up is usually the first
  symptom.
- **Idler bearings.** Here it is not the rubber that squeals but the
  bearing whose grease has dried out. The sound is thinner, closer to a
  squeak, and unconnected with alternator load —
  [signs of a worn idler pulley](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-idler-pulley/).
- **The alternator decoupler pulley.** A pulley with a built-in one-way
  clutch that absorbs jerks in the belt. When worn it slips and squeals as
  the engine starts and stops —
  [alternator decoupler pulley failure signs](/en/articles/alternator-decoupler-pulley-failure-signs/).
- **The water pump.** A coolant pump with a worn bearing adds a hum to the
  squeal and often leaves traces of coolant under the pulley —
  [signs of a failing water pump](/en/articles/signs-of-a-failing-water-pump/).

Statistically the first two are most often to blame — the belt itself and
its tension.

## How the sound behaves

| Source | How it sounds at start-up | What else is noticeable |
|---|---|---|
| Belt | Bright squeal for 1–5 seconds | Louder with lights and heaters on |
| Tensioner | Squeal at start-up and on a throttle blip | Visible flutter of the belt with the engine running |
| Idler | Thin squeak, independent of load | Also heard at idle after warm-up |
| Decoupler pulley | Squeal at start-up, rattle after switching off | Possible undercharging of the battery |
| Water pump | Squeal with a hum | Coolant weeping, a smell under the bonnet |

## Narrowing it down without tools

First, time it. A squeal that stops within two or three seconds almost
always means the belt slipping: as soon as the revs stabilise the grip is
restored. A sound that lasts longer and turns into a constant squeak or
hum points at a bearing — an idler, a decoupler pulley or the water pump.

Second, check the reaction to load. With the engine running, switch on
main beam and the rear screen heater. If the squeal appears or grows
louder, slipping is confirmed: the alternator needed more torque and the
belt did not transmit it. A bearing sound does not react to electrical
load.

Third, inspect the belt with the engine off: cracks across the ribs, a
shiny working face and fraying at the edges are visible without removing
anything.

The fourth technique is the water test, familiar to any mechanic. With the
engine off, spray a little clean water onto the working face of the belt
and start the engine straight away. The squeal disappeared for a couple of
seconds and then came back — slipping is proven, because the water briefly
changed the friction in the grooves. The sound did not change at all — the
odds on a bearing cause have risen sharply. Belt dressing aerosols,
however, are best avoided: they mask the symptom for a few weeks while
degrading the rubber and contaminating the pulleys, after which even a new
belt starts to slip.

If the squeal is tied not to starting but to temperature — there in the
morning, gone after warm-up — the picture is slightly different and is

## How the repair is decided

The practical approach is to replace the belt together with the idlers:
their service lives are comparable and the labour to remove the belt has
already been paid for. Saving an old idler under a new belt often means a
second visit within a few months. The tensioner goes the same way if its
pointer sits at the end of the range.

Two things worth asking. Whether the belt part number was checked against
the VIN — one engine can have versions with and without air conditioning,
with different belt lengths, and the wrong length cannot be tensioned
correctly. And whether the pulleys are clean: if oil or coolant killed the
old belt, the leak must be fixed first, otherwise the new one repeats the
story.

There is an organisational detail too: a squeal at start-up is a floating
symptom, and on a warm car at the workshop it may simply not repeat. So
that the diagnosis does not come down to "come back when it squeals", turn
up early on a cold engine or bring a recording. That is enough for a
mechanic: inspecting the belt, checking the tensioner travel and listening
to the idlers with a stethoscope takes ten to fifteen minutes, and many
workshops do that check free when you book the repair.
