Sagging springs are the quietest suspension defect: the car does not
knock, hum or pull, it simply gets a little lower year after year. The
driver adapts to the change, and most often it is noticed not by them but
by a fitter at a tyre shop or a neighbour in the car park. Yet sagged
springs change the geometry of the whole suspension and start a chain of
wear in the neighbouring parts. Here is how to catch the sag by symptom,
how to confirm it by measurement, and what the replacement involves.

## The direct symptoms

- **The car sits lower.** The wheels sit deeper in the arches; fewer
  fingers fit between the tyre and the arch edge than before. Compare with
  photographs of your car from two or three years ago — the difference can
  be striking.
- **The rear squats under load.** A couple of passengers on the back seat
  or some luggage and the rear drops noticeably, the headlights aim higher
  and oncoming drivers flash you.
- **The car catches where it used to clear.** A familiar speed hump, a
  driveway entrance, a kerb edge — the undertray or the sill has started
  scraping with no change of route.
- **Bottoming out on moderate potholes.** A sagged spring has less
  compression travel, so the suspension reaches its hard stop more
  often — how that sounds is on the symptom page
  [knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/).

Sagging is rarely perfectly symmetrical. More often one spring tires
faster — the rear left on a car driven for years by one person with the
load on that side, for instance. So a slight body tilt on one corner is a
symptom too, even if the overall height looks normal.

## The indirect signs

Sagging changes the wheel angles, and that leaves traces. The tyres start
wearing on the inner edge, the car holds a straight line slightly worse
and needs constant corrections. The steering feels heavier over bumps: the
arms and rods work at angles they were not designed for.

The second group of traces is sounds. A sagged spring itself is silent,
but because of it the bump stops — the compression buffers that take the
impact when the suspension bottoms out — are destroyed early. Dull thumps
in potholes with healthy dampers are the typical picture, covered in
[signs of worn bump stops](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-bump-stops/). The
control arm bushes suffer too: working under a permanent skew, the rubber
cracks faster and a dull knock over small stuff appears, as in
[signs of worn suspension bushes](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-suspension-bushes/).

## Confirming the sag by measurement

Impressions are a poor instrument, so sagging is checked with a tape
measure. The procedure: the car on level ground, tyres at the correct
pressure, boot empty. Measure vertically from the centre of the hub to the
edge of the arch at all four corners.

| Measurement | What it means |
|---|---|
| Left and right differ by up to 10 mm | Normal, within the manufacturer's tolerance |
| A side-to-side difference of 10–15 mm or more | Sagging or a broken spring on one side |
| Both sides of an axle below the model's figure | Fatigue sag of the pair |
| The rear lower than the front beyond the factory difference | The rear springs have sagged — typical of cars that carry loads |

The figure for a specific model is in the workshop manual; without
documentation, measuring the same model with low mileage helps. At a
workshop the sag is confirmed during a suspension inspection, which also
assesses the dampers and the rubber.

A second measurement is worth taking under load. Put two passengers on the
back seat and repeat: a healthy suspension sags by a comparable amount on
both sides and returns to its original figures once unloaded. A tired
spring sticks lower and recovers more slowly — visible even with a tape
measure.

## What driving on sagged springs leads to

The short answer: expensive repairs to the neighbouring parts. A damper on
a sagged spring works permanently compressed and overheats, the bump stops
crumble, the bushes tear, and the tyres are eaten at the edges within a
season. Safety suffers too: less compression travel means bottoming out
sooner, worse tyre contact over undulations and a longer stopping distance
on rough surfaces.

A separate scenario is a broken coil. A spring weakened by corrosion
eventually breaks, usually at the bottom coil, and then a distinct
metallic knock joins the sag — the general picture of such sounds is on
[knocking in the suspension](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-suspension/). A
broken piece can shift and cut a tyre, so a broken spring is not something
to drive on.

## How the replacement is decided

Springs are replaced with the strut removed and stripped on spring
compressors, so the labour matches a damper replacement. Three things
follow from that.

They are replaced in pairs on an axle, and selected by VIN: even within
one model there are versions with heavy-duty suspension, for poor roads
and for different engines — a spring from the wrong specification gives
the wrong height while nominally fitting.

The strip-down is the moment for the bump stops and boots, and past
100,000 km for the top mount bearings — the parts cost little and the
labour is already paid for.

And an alignment follows: restored ride height brings the wheel angles
back, and skipping it shows up as tyre wear.

On the temptation to economise: rubber inter-coil spacers and
“air-bags-in-a-spring” do not restore the spring's rate, they only lift
the body and make the suspension behave unpredictably. On a car where
safety matters more than looks, there is no place for them. And on the
full picture of spring wear, see
[signs of worn suspension springs](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-suspension-springs/).
