An even whine from under the bonnet that grows with engine revs and
ignores road speed is the typical voice of a worn water pump bearing. The
part is small and not expensive, but the answer to “how long can I keep
driving” depends not on the pump itself but on what drives it. On some
engines a whining pump is a reason to calmly book a workshop; on others it
is a job for the coming weeks.

## Why a pump starts to whine

The pump circulates coolant and turns constantly while the engine runs.
Its shaft rides on a bearing, and a seal separates that bearing from the
coolant. When the seal starts to pass, coolant washes the grease out, the
raceways corrode — and the bearing begins to howl. The second scenario is
the reverse: the bearing wears with age, the shaft develops play, and the
play then destroys the seal.

Hence two companions of the whine worth checking straight away: whitish or
coloured stains at the pump's weep hole, and a falling coolant level in
the expansion tank. If they are there, the part is at its final stage.

There is a third scenario, seen after a poorly done repair: an
over-tightened accessory belt. Excess tension overloads the pump bearing,
and a new part starts whining within a few thousand kilometres. So if the
whine appeared soon after a belt change, check the tension first, not the
pump.

## The difference between the two drive types

| Drive type | What happens if it seizes | Sensible horizon |
|---|---|---|
| Accessory belt | The belt slips or snaps, charging and cooling stop | Thousands of km, replace as planned |
| Timing belt | The belt breaks, the shafts lose sync, on many engines valves meet pistons | Weeks, replace urgently |
| Timing chain (rare) | Load on the chain and tensioner | Thousands of km, but watch for leaks |

The difference here is fundamental. In the first case the worst outcome is
a car stopped on the hard shoulder with an overheated engine and a flat
battery. In the second the stakes are different: a snapped timing belt on
most modern engines means cylinder head work, and sometimes a full rebuild.

## How to check the pump without dismantling

1. **Listen with a stethoscope or a screwdriver.** Rest the tip on the
   pump housing and your ear on the handle. Bearing whine will be
   noticeably louder here than on the neighbouring units. The tool is easy
   to improvise —
   [a mechanic's stethoscope you can make yourself](/en/articles/diy-mechanics-stethoscope/).
2. **Take the accessory belt off** (if the pump runs on it) and turn the
   pulley by hand. Roughness, crunching or noticeable play is a verdict.
3. **Check the weep hole** at the bottom of the housing: coolant stains
   mean the seal has already given up.
4. **Compare the sound cold and warm.** A pump usually whines louder as
   the engine warms and the coolant thins.

Do not confuse pump whine with squeal from the belt itself or the idlers —
different sounds with different consequences. The differences and the
conditions in which each appears are on the symptom page
[belt squeal](/en/symptoms/belt-squeal/), and the step-by-step check is in
[a whining water pump: how to check it](/en/articles/water-pump-whine-how-to-check/).

## How the repair is decided

The first question is what else comes out with the pump. If the pump is
driven by the timing belt, the belt, the idlers and the tensioner are
already off, and replacing them at the same time costs a fraction of doing
it separately later; refusing the kit and keeping only the pump saves
money today and spends it twice within a year or two. Ask what is in the
kit being quoted.

The second is the coolant. Any pump job drains the system, so the coolant
goes back in either as fresh fluid of the correct specification or as the
old fluid strained back in. Mixing coolant types is a genuine cause of
corrosion and of a new pump failing early, so it is worth asking which
fluid is going in and whether the system will be bled properly — an air
lock after the job shows up as an overheating gauge a week later.

The third is that a whine is not the only reason to change a pump. A
weeping seal with no noise at all is just as much a reason, because that
is the leak that empties the system on a motorway.

Comparing these figures with the cost of a rebuild explains why a whining
pump is not put off on engines with a belt-driven camshaft. It is the case
where a small part drags disproportionate consequences behind it — much
like ticking hydraulic lifters, whose risks are set out in
[what happens if you drive with noisy hydraulic lifters](/en/articles/driving-with-noisy-hydraulic-lifters/).
The signs that mean switching the engine off on the spot are collected in
[noises that mean stop driving now](/en/articles/noises-that-mean-stop-driving-now/).
