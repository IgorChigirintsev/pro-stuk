Timing idlers are the auxiliary wheels of the valve drive: the tensioner
pulley maintains the belt's tension, and the idler (also called a guide
pulley) sets the belt's path. Inside each is a rolling bearing packed with
grease for life. It is the idlers, not the belt itself, that most often
give the first audible signals that the timing drive needs attention — and
those signals matter.

## Why idlers make noise before the belt does

A rubber belt wears quietly: cracks and scuffing produce almost no sound.
An idler is different — a metal bearing turning at crankshaft speed and
above. When its grease ages and dries, and pits appear on the raceways,
the bearing starts announcing every revolution.

The tensioner pulley carries extra load: it does not merely spin but
presses constantly on the belt with spring or hydraulic force. Its bearing
works in the hardest regime and usually fails first. Idler life is
designed to match the belt's service interval — which is exactly why they
are replaced as a kit, as covered in
[signs of a worn timing belt](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-timing-belt/).

## What wear sounds like: from rustle to grinding

The sound evolution of a worn idler is fairly predictable:

1. **A light rustle when cold.** Audible for the first minutes after
   starting, going away with warm-up. An early stage with plenty of time.
2. **Whistling and chirping.** A thin sound that rises with revs; at this
   stage it appears on a warm engine too. It often resembles a slipping
   belt — the difference is that an idler's chirp does not react to the
   headlights and heaters. The comparison is on the symptom page
   [squeal on startup](/en/symptoms/squeal-on-startup/).
3. **Hum and rumble.** The raceways are broken and the bearing roars at
   any revs.
4. **Grinding and rattling.** The bearing cage is disintegrating — seizure
   is hours of running away.

A separate warning sign is rhythmic ticking from behind the cover: a
misaligned idler is cutting into the edge of the belt. The valvetrain and
hydraulic lifters tick similarly — see the symptom page
[engine ticking](/en/symptoms/ticking-engine/).

## Telling timing idlers from the accessories

The difficulty is that timing idlers hide behind a plastic cover while the
accessory drive turns right next to them with its own idlers, alternator
and pump — the sounds overlap. Three techniques separate them.

The first is location: a timing idler's sound comes from behind the cover
and is slightly muffled by the plastic. The second is the load test:
switching on the headlights and the air conditioning changes the sound of
the alternator and the compressor, but not of the timing idlers. The third
is the workshop method: a brief run with the accessory belt removed. If
the noise remains, the source is behind the timing cover.

Similar accessory drive sounds are covered separately: alternator hum and
whine in
[signs of a worn alternator bearing](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-alternator-bearing/),
and accessory idlers in
[signs of a worn idler pulley](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-idler-pulley/).

## Summary table

| Sound | When it is heard | Likely stage |
|---|---|---|
| Rustle when cold | First minutes after starting | Early wear of the grease |
| Whistle, chirp | Cold and warm | Medium bearing wear |
| Hum, rumble | Constant, grows with revs | Broken raceways |
| Grinding, rattling | Constant | Pre-failure condition |
| Rhythmic ticking | Under load | Misaligned idler, belt wear |

## What delay risks and how the job is decided

A seized idler means an instant stop or a snapped timing belt. On most
modern engines a break means the valves meeting the pistons: a repair with
the cylinder head off, several times the cost of the timing job. An
indirect precursor to seizure is grease squeezed out of the bearing and
visible on the inside of the cover.

Because the labour is entirely in stripping the drive, the sensible job is
always the complete kit — belt, tensioner and idlers, plus the water pump
when the timing belt drives it. Replacing one idler alone costs the same
labour and leaves an old belt running on a new pulley, which is a lottery.

Two questions worth asking. Whether the new tensioner will be set with the
engine turned by hand through two revolutions and the tension re-checked —
the step that is skipped most often. And whether the cause of any
misalignment was found: an idler that wore at an angle usually did so
because a bracket or a seal behind it was not right, and a fresh idler in
the same place wears the same way.
