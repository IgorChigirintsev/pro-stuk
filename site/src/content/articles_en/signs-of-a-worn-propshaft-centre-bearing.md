On rear- and four-wheel-drive cars the propshaft is rarely one piece: it is
long, and at high speed it would inevitably flex and whip. So the shaft is
split in two and the joint is supported by a centre bearing — a bearing in
a rubber mount bolted to the floor. It is an inconspicuous part, but when
it wears the car reports it in several ways at once: a hum from below,
vibration through the floor and a jolt when pulling away. Here are the
signs in order, and how to tell them from the neighbouring sources.

## A hum from under the centre of the floor

The first and most common sign is an even hum heard from under the middle
of the car rather than from the wheels. It appears from around 50–60 km/h,
grows with speed and does not go away if you coast in neutral: the shaft
keeps turning, so it keeps humming.

The lane-change test separates it from wheel noise. A wheel bearing
changes volume in curves — the weight transfers, the load changes. A
centre bearing barely reacts to manoeuvres: its load does not depend on
body roll. The general breakdown of this family of sounds is on the
symptom page [humming while driving](/en/symptoms/humming-while-driving/),
and separating driveline hum from a wheel bearing is covered in
[humming: gearbox or wheel bearing](/en/articles/humming-gearbox-or-wheel-bearing/).

## Vibration and a jolt when pulling away

The second group of signs comes from the rubber mount. When the rubber
cracks and loses its elasticity, the shaft gains freedom to move.
Vibration appears in the floor from a certain speed, along with a
characteristic dull jolt or thump at the moment you pull away — the shaft
has time to swing before the load presses it back into place.

A separate marker is shaking that grows noticeably under load:
accelerating uphill or with the car full. Another recognisable touch is
vibration that comes and goes with a particular speed band: accelerate a
little further and the shaking stops although the hum remains. That is how
a shaft behaves when it has lost its support but is not yet fully broken.
If the failure has gone further, a metallic knock against the body joins
in over bumps: the support sags and the shaft touches the undertray or the
tunnel.

## Telling it from its neighbours

| Part | Character of the sound | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| Centre bearing | Even hum from the centre of the floor plus floor vibration | Unchanged in curves, audible coasting |
| Wheel bearing | Hum tied to road speed | Changes volume during lane changes |
| Universal joint | Clicks and thumps as the load changes | Play when the shaft is rocked by hand |
| Rear differential | A whine that changes on and off the throttle | Different sound under drive and coasting |
| Dual-mass flywheel | Hum and shake at idle, clatter on shutdown | Follows engine revs, not road speed |

The last two rows are the ones most often confused with a centre bearing.
If the sound changes depending on whether the engine is pulling or the car
is coasting, look at the rear differential. If the hum and vibration
follow engine revs and are especially noticeable at idle, see
[what a worn dual-mass flywheel sounds like](/en/articles/what-a-worn-dual-mass-flywheel-sounds-like/).
Clicks as the load changes belong to
[signs of a worn propshaft universal joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-propshaft-universal-joint/).

## Checking it and how the repair is decided

On a lift the part is checked by hand: take the shaft next to the support
and rock it up and down and sideways. A healthy bearing gives only the
springiness of the rubber; a worn one gives noticeable play and a
characteristic dry rustle when turned. At the same time the mount itself
is inspected: cracks, delamination and signs that the shaft has dropped
are visible without instruments.

Experience says these parts are worth replacing together: the shaft comes
off either way, and most of the labour is in removing and refitting it.
Paying once for the disassembly is cheaper than coming back six months
later for the neighbouring joint.

Two things worth asking. First, whether the shaft will be marked before it
is separated so the flanges go back in the same orientation — that alone
prevents most post-repair vibration. Second, whether the shaft will be
balanced afterwards on a longer two-piece shaft; not every workshop offers
it, and it is the difference between removing the vibration and removing
only the noise.

If the hum is still quiet and it is unclear whether it comes from the
centre below or from a wheel, record it in the Stuk app on a smooth
motorway and repeat the recording during gentle lane changes. The app
matches the recordings with your answers to a few questions and shows the
