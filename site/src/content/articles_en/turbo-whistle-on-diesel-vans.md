Diesel vans work in hard conditions: long distances, constant load, dusty
summer air. The turbocharger is the part that feels all of it first. And a
turbo always whistles — the whistle is standard — so the question is
almost never "does it whistle or not". The question is whether the sound
has changed. Here is where the line between normal and a problem runs.

## Where normal ends

A healthy turbo gives an even whistle that rises in pitch with the boost
and fades as you lift off. It is the same day after day, comes with no
foreign notes and is unconnected with any loss of pull.

Four things should put you on guard. First, growing volume: a whistle that
has become noticeably louder over the last few weeks. Second, a change of
character: instead of an even whistle you get a howl, a siren or a
grinding note. Third, a whistle together with a flat spot in the pull,
especially under load and uphill. Fourth, a sharp hiss as you press the
throttle, which usually means not the turbo but an air leak. The general
breakdown of whistling sounds is on the symptom page
[squeal on startup](/en/symptoms/squeal-on-startup/), and the wider
question of what counts as normal in
[turbo whistle: normal or a fault](/en/articles/turbo-whistle-normal-or-a-fault/).

## What usually lies behind a changed whistle

- **An air leak.** A cracked hose, a loose clamp, a damaged intercooler
  seal. The commonest and cheapest cause.
- **A blocked air filter.** The turbo works with increased vacuum at the
  inlet, the sound changes and the pull falls. Checked in five minutes.
- **Oil starvation.** Contaminated or pinched oil feed and drain lines
  kill the shaft bearings — hence the howl and the play.
- **Wear in the shaft and wheels.** Axial and radial play appears and the
  blades start touching the housing.
- **Variable geometry.** A coked-up variable geometry mechanism gives
  unstable boost, snatching and fault codes.

## Localising the cause

| Check | What to do | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Inspect the intake | Go along the hoses and clamps | Cracks and oil traces mean a leak |
| Air filter | Take it out and hold it to the light | Blocked — replace and listen again |
| Scan tool | Read codes and boost data | A deviation in pressure means the geometry |
| Oil | Level, condition, interval | The main factor in turbo life |
| Inspect the turbo | Shaft play, weeping, blade condition | A direct assessment of wear |

The first two checks an owner does themselves; the rest need a workshop.
The order matters: a noticeable share of "dying turbo" complaints end with
a replaced hose or filter.

Oil deserves separate attention. The turbo shaft turns very fast and runs
on plain bearings that need a continuous supply of clean oil under
pressure. Hence two practical rules for a van that works hard. First: the
change interval is set with an allowance for the conditions, not by the
figure in the manual — town delivery work with constant stops and dusty
roads ages oil faster. Second: after a long run under load, let the engine
idle for half a minute before switching off, so the turbo cools on
circulating oil rather than baking on hot metal.

## How the repair is decided

The criterion is the order of the checks above, and it is worth insisting
on it: the intake and the filter first, the scan tool second, the turbo
last. Before agreeing to have the turbo removed, ask for the intake to be
pressure-tested and the boost figures compared against the target — those
two answers close a large share of cases for the price of an hour's
labour.

Two things mechanics do not always say. If the turbo is genuinely worn,
the oil feed and drain lines are replaced or cleaned with it — a new
cartridge fed through a coked line dies the same way, and that is the
commonest reason for a repeat failure. And after the work the engine gets
a fresh oil and filter change, because whatever came off the old bearings
is still in the system.

For commercial vehicles there is one more point: with reduced pull,
drivers start working the clutch harder, and the next complaint often
comes from there. The signs are covered in
[Ford Focus clutch fork knock](/en/articles/ford-focus-clutch-fork-knock/)
and
[signs of a worn clutch](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-clutch/).
