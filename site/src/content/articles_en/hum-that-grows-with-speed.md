A hum that appears quietly around sixty kilometres an hour and turns into
an even low howl by a hundred is such a common complaint that almost any
mechanic has heard it today. The value of this symptom is that it rules
out half the car immediately: if the sound follows road speed rather than
engine revs, the source rotates with the wheels. What remains is to work
out which part — and three simple tests, done in a single drive, do most
of that work.

## Speed or revs: the first fork

The key distinction is this: some parts rotate with the speed of the car,
others with engine revs. Checking which is simple. Accelerate to the speed
where the hum is clearly audible and select neutral without touching the
throttle. If the sound is unchanged while the car coasts, it is born in
the chassis or in the transmission after the gearbox. If it disappears
along with the drive, look closer to the engine: accessories, the water
pump, idlers.

The second level of the check is consistency. Take one speed — 80 km/h,
say — and hold it first under power, then coasting, then with light engine
braking. Bearing hum barely changes; transmission hum reacts noticeably to
load; tyre noise stays even but changes with the road surface.

## Telling a bearing from tyres

| Sign | Wheel bearing | Tyres |
|---|---|---|
| Reaction to lane changes | Changes volume | No change |
| Change of road surface | The same sound | Changes noticeably |
| Coasting in neutral | Hum remains | Hum remains |
| Tone | Even howl, later a rumble | Rustling, swishing |
| How it appeared | Gradually, over months | Often right after a tyre change |
| Vibration in the steering | Possible at a late stage | Rare |

The main tool here is the lane-change test. On an empty straight road at
60–80 km/h, ease left, then right. Turning left transfers weight onto the
right-hand wheels, and the right bearing hums louder; turning right gives
the mirror image. Tyres barely react to those manoeuvres.

## What else hums at speed

Besides bearings and tyres, the list holds the transmission and
aerodynamics. A differential or gearbox produces a hum tied to speed but
sensitive to drive: louder under power, quieter coasting, and at certain
speeds it can turn into a distinct whine. Wind noise — a badly seated door
seal, a loose arch liner, a trim strip — is recognised by its abrupt
onset: it arrives almost in a step as you cross a particular speed and
does not change during lane changes.

A frequent scenario of its own is a hum that does not exist in town and
appears only on the motorway; it is covered in

## What the diagnosis and repair involve

The diagnosis starts on a lift: the wheels are raised and spun by hand
while the mechanic listens for noise and checks for play. It is a quick
procedure.

If a bearing is confirmed, two things shape the job. The first is whether
your car uses a separate bearing that is pressed into the existing hub or
a complete hub assembly replaced as a unit — that decides both the price
and the time. The second is the hub nut: its tightening torque matters
more here than on most work, because an over- or under-tightened nut
destroys a new bearing within thousands of kilometres. A torque wrench
rather than an impact gun is what you want to see.

If the wheels turn out to be the cause, the answer is usually cheaper —
balancing, or rotating and in some cases replacing tyres whose tread has
gone sawtooth. Uneven wear itself is worth investigating, though: it
usually means alignment is out or the dampers are tired, and new tyres on
an unfixed car go the same way.

A wheel bearing almost never fails instantly, but do not ignore the hum
for months: the next stage is a rumble and vibration, and after that the
cage breaks up and the wheel develops play. A sensible horizon is an
inspection within two weeks of the sound becoming permanent.
