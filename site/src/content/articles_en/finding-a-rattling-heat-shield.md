The rattle of thin sheet metal is one of the most deceptive sounds in a
car. It is loud, metallic, comes from somewhere below and is easily taken
for something serious: the suspension falling apart, or the exhaust
dropping off. In reality the culprit is usually a heat shield — a thin
sheet of aluminium or stainless steel covering the floor and the
components from the heat of the exhaust. You can find the source in ten
minutes, and securing it is usually cheaper than any other job in the
workshop.

## Why it rattles

Shields are attached to the body or to the exhaust pipe itself at
individual points — a few bolts, clips or folded tabs. Their metal is
deliberately thin: that way the sheet reflects heat better and weighs
less. The downside is low stiffness. As soon as one fastener rusts
through and releases the sheet, the free edge starts vibrating with the
general shaking of the car.

The rattle does not appear all the time, only in a narrow band of engine
speeds where the frequency from the engine and the gas flow matches the
natural frequency of the sheet. Hence its signature: the noise turns up
at, say, 1,800 rpm, holds for a few hundred rpm and disappears — and
comes back in exactly the same band. Shields also respond well to road
surface: a short metallic chatter over joints and washboard tarmac is
practically their calling card.

## Finding it in ten minutes

The simplest approach is to work in order:

1. **Warm engine, neutral, a helper.** Have them raise the revs smoothly
   and hold them where the rattle appears. The sound should reproduce
   reliably.
2. **Walk around and listen.** Go round the car and crouch by each wheel.
   A shield rattle is clearly audible from the floor side and along the
   whole exhaust, from the manifold to the rear box.
3. **The hand test.** With the engine off and cold, work along every
   shield, pressing it with your palm and rocking it. A loose sheet moves
   noticeably and answers with the same sound.
4. **The pressure test.** Have your helper return to the resonant revs
   while you press the suspect shield through a thick glove — if the
   noise stops, you have found the source.

Inspection is easier on a lift or over a pit, but for the front shields
near the manifold an open bonnet is often enough. Pay attention to the
rusty washers at the fastening points: they are the first thing to break
up, leaving the bolt in place and the sheet free. The general map of
noises from underneath is on the symptom page
[rattling under the car](/en/symptoms/rattling-under-car/).

## Similar sounds and how to separate them

| Sound | When it appears | Likely source |
|---|---|---|
| Thin sheet-metal rattle | A narrow band of revs, small bumps | Heat shield |
| Low roar, vibration at idle | Constantly, louder when pulling away | A torn flexible section |
| Dry rustling, like sand pouring | Bumps, pulling away, braking | Catalytic converter debris |
| Chain-like chatter from above | Cold start, idle | A stretched timing chain |
| Sharp knock over bumps | Only over bumps | Exhaust hanger, anti-roll bar link |

The key marker is the link to engine speed rather than road speed. A
shield rattles the same whether the car is parked or moving, as long as
the engine is in the right band. If the noise follows speed rather than
revs, the search moves to the chassis.

## How it is secured

The simple, reliable answer is to give the sheet its support back.
Mechanics usually fit wide body washers through the remaining fastening
points, add a clamp, or weld in a short bracket. If the sheet has rotted
along an edge and is crumbling, part of it is carefully removed, leaving
the intact section over the hottest areas. The folk method of a coin or a
piece of rubber as a shim does not last: the rubber burns and the coin
falls out.

If the inspection shows that it is not the shield but the system itself
that is rattling, the questions change: whether the flexible section is
being replaced on its own or with the front pipe, and whether the
silencer is being welded or replaced. Welding is cheaper, but the
corrosion that caused the failure is rarely local, and that is worth
asking about before agreeing to it.
