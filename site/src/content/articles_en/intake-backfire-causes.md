An intake backfire is a flash of the air-fuel mixture not in the cylinder
but in the intake manifold: a sharp sound from under the bonnet, from the
air filter side, often with the engine shuddering at the same moment. The
causes come down to three directions: a mixture that is too lean,
misfires, and valve timing that is out. Here is each of them, plus a table
of differences so that intake and exhaust do not get confused.

## The mechanics: why flame ends up in the intake

Normally the mixture burns in the cylinder with the valves shut. But a
lean mixture burns slowly: the flame front is still alive when the intake
valve opens on the next cycle — and fire breaks through into the intake
manifold, where a fresh charge is waiting. It ignites, and you hear a
bang.

The second mechanism is a misfire: an unburnt charge ignites later and in
the wrong place. The third is a valve open when it should be closed, which
is a story about valve timing. All three produce a similar sound, so the
cause is found through the signs that come with it.

## A lean mixture: air leaks and fuel delivery

The most common direction. The mixture goes lean when there is more air
than the control unit accounted for, or less fuel:

- **an air leak** — cracks and poor sealing in the intake manifold, hoses
  or gaskets: unmetered air leans the mixture out. Sometimes a leak gives
  itself away as a hiss with the engine running;
- **dirty or failing injectors** — delivering less than they should;
- **a weak fuel pump or blocked fuel filter** — rail pressure sagging
  under load;
- **sensors** — an airflow meter reading wrongly distorts the mixture
  calculation.

## Ignition and valve timing

**Ignition.** Worn spark plugs, breaking-down coils or leads cause
misfires: a charge does not burn in time and finishes burning with a
flash. The banging then comes with rough running, shuddering and a loss of
power.

**Valve timing.** If the timing belt or chain has jumped a tooth,
stretched, or a cam phaser has stuck, the intake valve opens early — and
the flame passes into the intake as a matter of course. A telling marker:
banging that appeared straight after a timing belt change is almost
certainly a mistake with the timing marks.

## Intake or exhaust: how not to confuse them

| Where it bangs | Typical cause | Where to look |
|---|---|---|
| In the intake, near the air filter | Lean mixture, early valve timing | Air leaks, timing, injectors |
| In the exhaust, at the back | Rich mixture, misfires | Plugs, coils, oxygen sensor |
| A sharp ringing under acceleration | Knocking inside the cylinder | Fuel, plugs, carbon |

Banging out of the tailpipe is the mirror problem with different causes:
see the symptom page
[exhaust backfire](/en/symptoms/exhaust-backfire/). A metallic ringing
under load is knocking, covered on
[pinging when accelerating](/en/symptoms/pinging-when-accelerating/). The
direction of the sound is half the diagnosis here, so it is worth asking
someone to listen from outside, at the front and at the rear.

## Diagnosis and risks

The starting point is a scan tool: it shows fuel trims — the main marker
of an air leak — misfire counters per cylinder, and camshaft correlation
faults. Air leaks are found quickly with a smoke machine.

If the timing marks turn out to be wrong, one thing is worth agreeing
before the work: whether the belt is being replaced as a kit with the
idlers and tensioner, or merely re-indexed. Re-timing an old belt is
cheaper today and a false economy on a car with unknown belt history — and
if the water pump is driven by that belt, it goes on the list too, because
the access is the same.

Do not stretch out the search. Flashes in the intake can tear the hose,
ruin the airflow meter and sensors, and running permanently lean overheats
valves and pistons. A backfire by itself is not a catastrophe, but it does
not appear on a healthy engine.
