A squeal from under the bonnet is a sound that is easy to get used to. It
turns up on a frosty morning, lasts half a minute and goes away, and by
the next week it feels like a normal part of starting the car. What is
squealing is the accessory belt — the rubber band that drives the
alternator and, on most engines, the water pump and the power steering
pump as well. Here is what the owner risks by leaving it, and where the
line runs between “I will get there” and “time for a workshop”.

## What a squeal means

A belt squeals when it slips on a pulley: the speed of the belt and the
speed of the groove stop matching, the rubber rubs on metal and produces a
high note. There are three reasons for slipping. First, the belt has
stretched or its ribs have worn, and it no longer grips the pulley as
tightly as when new. Second, the tensioner has given up: the spring or
hydraulic mechanism that keeps the belt under constant load. Third, fluid
has got onto the belt — coolant from a weeping pump, oil from a seal, or
simply water after a wash.

There is a fourth scenario often confused with belt squeal: a seizing
bearing in an idler, the alternator or the water pump. It also gives a
high note, but the belt has nothing to do with it — the unit is resisting
rotation and the belt slips over it.

## Working out how urgent it is

| Picture | What it means | How urgent |
|---|---|---|
| Squeal only when cold, 10–30 seconds | Tension at the bottom of the normal range | Planned replacement |
| Squeal when the a/c comes on or at full lock | The belt cannot hold peak load | Weeks |
| Constant squeal, unaffected by warm-up | Belt wear or a tired tensioner | Within days |
| Squeal plus a whine at any revs | Bearing in an idler, alternator or pump | Do not put it off |
| Visible cracks, missing ribs, delamination | The belt is at the end of its life | Immediately |

Check the simplest thing separately: switch the engine off and look at the
belt with a torch. Cross cracks in the ribs, frayed edges, a shiny
“varnished” surface — all of these are reasons not to drag it out. The
sound examples are on the symptom page
[belt squeal](/en/symptoms/belt-squeal/), and telling the belt from the
units it drives is covered in
[whine under the bonnet: alternator or water pump](/en/articles/alternator-or-water-pump-whine/).

## What you risk by carrying on

The squeal itself does not break the car. A snapped belt does — and here
everything depends on the engine's layout. If the belt drives only the
alternator, a break means no charging: the car will run from half an hour
to a couple of hours on the battery and then stop. If the same belt drives
the water pump, a break stops coolant circulation and overheating is
minutes away. Overheating means a warped cylinder head and a repair two
orders of magnitude dearer than a new belt.

A separate nuisance: a snapped belt can be dragged under a cover or wrap
itself round the pulleys, damaging sensors and wiring. So the logic here
is the same as with other “tolerable” noises: the situation is not
dangerous until it becomes dangerous instantly. The same applies to a
clicking CV joint —
[can you drive with a clicking CV joint](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-a-clicking-cv-joint/).

## How the repair is decided

The first question is whether the belt is the cause or the victim. A belt
that squeals because coolant is dripping on it will squeal again a month
after replacement unless the leak is found; the same goes for oil from a
front crank seal. Ask what was on the belt when it came off.

The second is what goes back on with it. The idlers and the tensioner have
a service life comparable to the belt, and the labour to reach them has
already been paid for — replacing the belt alone on a high-mileage engine
is the classic repeat visit. On engines with a decoupler pulley on the
alternator (common on diesels and modern petrol engines) that pulley is a
frequent cause of squeal in its own right and is worth checking while
everything is off.

The third is the tension itself. Many modern engines use a stretch-fit
belt that requires a special tool and must never be levered on with a
screwdriver, and an automatic tensioner has a wear indicator marking that
tells you whether it is still within range. A workshop that mentions
neither is guessing.
