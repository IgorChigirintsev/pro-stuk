The wheel bearing is the part the wheel turns on. It works under the
weight of the car for thousands of kilometres with no servicing, and as it
wears it almost always warns you honestly with sound long before there is
real danger. Below are all the signs by stage, simple tests without a
lift, and what decides the replacement.

## What a worn bearing sounds like

The classic sound is **an even low hum that grows with speed**. It is
often compared to an aircraft taking off. The key differences from other
noises:

- the hum follows **road speed**, not engine revs: coasting in neutral it
  does not go away;
- it **barely depends on the surface** — the same on new and coarse tarmac
  (unlike tyre noise);
- it **changes in gentle curves** at speed: quieter one way, louder the
  other.

At a late stage rumbling and vibration join in, felt through the floor and
the steering wheel. If you are not sure it is the bearing, the lookalike
is covered in
[humming: wheel bearing or tyres](/en/articles/hum-wheel-bearing-or-tyres/),
and the stage-by-stage sound portrait in
[what a failing wheel bearing sounds like](/en/articles/what-a-failing-wheel-bearing-sounds-like/).

## Signs by stage of wear

| Stage | What you hear and feel | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Early | Light hum at 60–100 km/h, not always noticeable | Watch, book a check |
| Middle | Constant hum from 40–50 km/h, changes in curves | Diagnosis within a week |
| Late | Rumbling, vibration, hum at any speed | Workshop without delay |
| Critical | Wheel play, grinding, a hot hub | Stop driving |

The rate of decline is unpredictable: from the first hum to play usually
takes thousands of kilometres, but after a pothole strike the process can
accelerate several-fold. The risks of driving with a hum are set out in
[can you drive with a humming wheel bearing](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-a-humming-wheel-bearing/).

## How to check it yourself

Three tests that need no lift:

1. **The surface test.** Drive one stretch over different tarmac. If the
   hum is unchanged, a bearing is likely; if it goes quieter on smooth
   surfaces, tyres are more likely.
2. **The lane-change test.** On an empty road at 60–80 km/h, ease smoothly
   in both directions. A hum that quietens turning right and grows turning
   left means the right side is loaded and the right bearing is the
   suspect, and vice versa.
3. **The coasting test.** Accelerate to the speed where the hum is audible
   and coast in neutral. If it remains, the source turns with the wheels
   and the engine is not involved.

The full set of self-checks is in
[how to check a wheel bearing yourself](/en/articles/how-to-check-a-wheel-bearing-yourself/).
At a workshop the diagnosis is confirmed on a lift: the raised wheel is
rocked vertically (there should be no play) and spun while the hub is
listened to. That takes a few minutes and is usually free when the
replacement is booked.

If the hum is at the back, do not confuse a bearing with the differential
on rear- and four-wheel-drive cars — the search direction is the one
described on the symptom page
[wheel bearing hum](/en/symptoms/wheel-bearing-hum/).

## Why bearings wear out early

The design life is around 100,000–150,000 km, but in practice the spread
is huge. The main accelerators:

- **Pothole and kerb strikes.** A brief shock load leaves micro-dents on
  the raceways, and the failure grows from there. One hard hit at speed
  can start a hum even on a fresh bearing.
- **Water and dirt.** Deep puddles and pressure-washing right at the hub
  force grease out and drive moisture past the seal. Corroded raceways are
  the second most common cause of an early death.
- **Non-standard wheels.** Larger wheels with low-profile tyres and
  spacers increase the leverage — the bearing works in a regime it was not
  designed for.
- **Incorrect tightening.** A hub nut over-tightened during a repair, or
  fitted without a torque wrench, kills a new bearing within a few
  thousand kilometres. So it matters that the workshop works to a torque
  figure rather than by feel.

The practical conclusion: if the car recently caught a pothole and a hum
appeared a week or two later, the connection is almost certainly direct.

## Front or rear: is there a difference

Front bearings are more heavily loaded — they carry the engine's weight
and the steering forces — so statistically the front ones hum more often.
But rear ones are trickier to diagnose: a hum at the back on rear- or
four-wheel-drive cars is easily confused with a differential whine or a
propshaft centre bearing. If the hum is clearly at the back, ask the
mechanic to check the driveline as well — the same few minutes on a lift.
By symptoms, front and rear bearings do not differ: the same hum, the same
reaction to weight transfer in curves.

## How the repair is decided

Two things govern the job: whether your car takes a separate bearing
pressed into the knuckle or a complete hub unit, and the hub nut torque.
The first decides the price and the tooling; the second decides how long
the new part lasts. There is no need to replace them in pairs — only the
worn one.

One thing workshops do not always mention: on many modern cars the ABS
sensor ring is part of the bearing, and it has a magnetic encoder that is
ruined by careless handling or by fitting the bearing the wrong way round.
An ABS light that appears right after a bearing replacement is almost
always that.

## What ignoring it leads to

A bearing fails progressively: hum, rumble, play, cage failure. In the
final stage the wheel goes out of true, wears the tyre, heats the hub and
can seize on the move. That is usually a long way off — but there is no
point reaching it: the longer a broken bearing runs, the higher the chance
its seat is damaged and the hub or the whole knuckle has to be replaced
instead.
