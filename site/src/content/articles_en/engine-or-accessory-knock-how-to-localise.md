Opening the bonnet on a knocking car, it is easy to be wrong about the
main thing: the engine bay is not only the engine but a dozen units hanging
on the accessory belt. The alternator, the water pump, the air
conditioning compressor, the idlers and tensioners all produce sounds that
are almost indistinguishable by ear from a knock deep in the block. The
cost of the two is wildly different, so they are worth separating before
anyone talks about dismantling.

## Why accessories imitate the engine so convincingly

Every accessory is bolted to the engine block itself. Metal conducts
vibration well, and a bearing making noise in the alternator sends the
sound through its bracket straight into the block — from where it emerges
as “a knock in the engine”. The second factor is speed: the accessories
turn in sync with the crankshaft, so the sound's frequency rises with the
revs exactly as the engine's own mechanics do.

Hence the conclusion: by ear and by frequency the source cannot be
separated. It is separated by its reaction to load, because accessories
can be loaded selectively while the engine's internal mechanics cannot.

## Four checks in increasing complexity

1. **Electrical load.** At warm idle, switch on the main beam, the heated
   screen and the blower at maximum. The alternator draws more current and
   its bearing is loaded harder — if the sound grows, that is where to
   look; see
   [signs of a worn alternator bearing](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-alternator-bearing/).
2. **Air conditioning.** Switch the compressor on and off a few times. A
   knock tied to the clutch engaging is the compressor or its clutch, not
   the engine.
3. **Steering to full lock.** On cars with hydraulic power steering, hold
   the wheel at full lock for a couple of seconds. The pump is loaded and
   its sound comes to the front.
4. **Belt removal.** The final check, done at a workshop: the belt is
   removed, the cold engine started for 10–15 seconds and listened to.
   Silence means an accessory. A knock that remains means the source is
   inside the engine.

## What each theory means

| Source | Character of the sound | Scale of the repair |
|---|---|---|
| Idler, tensioner | Even hum or measured tapping | A cheap part |
| Alternator bearing | Hum that grows with electrical load | An alternator rebuild |
| Water pump | Hum with a metallic edge, often with a leak | A moderate job, or a big one behind a timing belt |
| Timing chain | Rattle and rustle at the front, louder cold | Kit plus significant labour |
| Hydraulic lifters | Rapid ticking from above, usually gone when warm | Head-off labour |
| Crankshaft bearings | Dull low knock in time with the revs under load | An engine rebuild |

The lifter version is covered in

the chain in

and the bearings in

## Where the line of calm runs

The practical rule sounds like this: the higher the source and the more
easily it changes with external load, the calmer the situation.
Accessories almost always give you time: a failing idler or alternator
bearing hums for weeks first. Chain stretch also develops gradually.

The alarming zone is a low dull knock that grows under load and reacts to
none of the checks above, especially combined with falling oil pressure.
There, driving on turns the repair into a rebuild, and the right move is
not to make it “until Monday” but to stop.
