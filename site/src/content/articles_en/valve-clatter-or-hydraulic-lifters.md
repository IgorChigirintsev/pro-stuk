Ticking from the top of the engine is a sound almost every owner of a
high-mileage car has heard. Its frequency is exactly half the crankshaft
speed, its timbre is dry and sewing-machine-like, and its source is almost
always the same area: the valvetrain under the rocker cover. From there
the paths diverge. On some engines the clearance between the lobe and the
valve is adjusted by hand with shims or screws; on others hydraulic
lifters — small hydraulic cups that adjust the clearance themselves using
oil pressure — take care of it. The two cases sound alike and are cured
completely differently.

## What is knocking in each case

A mechanical clearance grows as the lobes, rockers and contact faces wear.
The lobe stops running smoothly onto the follower and starts striking
it — hence an even, monotonous ticking, the same cold and warm.

A hydraulic lifter ticks for a different reason: it cannot fill with oil.
The culprits are either contaminated passages and a coked plunger, or low
system pressure, or oil that is too thick or has lost its viscosity. That
knock is almost always louder on a cold engine and changes as it warms.

## Telling them apart by ear

| Sign | Mechanical clearance | Hydraulic lifter |
|---|---|---|
| After warm-up | Unchanged or slightly louder | Often fades or goes entirely |
| Consistency | Even, metronomic | Wanders; individual elements click out of step |
| Reaction to an oil change | None | Noticeable in some cases |
| Number of sources | Usually one or two cylinders | Can move between cylinders |
| Engine type | Adjustable valvetrain | Engine with hydraulic followers |

The first thing to do is find out which mechanism your engine has. There
may simply be no hydraulic lifters, and then the question is closed: it is
a clearance adjustment, covered in
[signs of valve clatter](/en/articles/signs-of-valve-clatter/). The second
is to warm the engine and listen for the change. The diagnostic value is
in the dynamics: a knock that is the same at any temperature supports the
clearance theory, one that disappears supports the lifters —
[how to check hydraulic lifters](/en/articles/how-to-check-hydraulic-lifters/)
and
[hydraulic lifters ticking when cold](/en/articles/hydraulic-lifters-ticking-when-cold/).
The neighbouring fork — a cold knock from the chain or the cam phasers —
is in
[cam phaser rattle when cold](/en/articles/cam-phaser-rattle-when-cold/).
The general portrait of this family of sounds is on the symptom page
[engine ticking](/en/symptoms/ticking-engine/).

## Where to start the check

The order that saves money:

1. **Oil and filter.** Check the level, the age and the viscosity. Oil
   that is too thick or past its life is the most common and the cheapest
   cause of lifter noise.
2. **Oil pressure.** If the pressure light flickers or the idle wanders,
   the problem may be not the lifters but the pump and the pick-up.
3. **Localisation.** With a stethoscope or a screwdriver, work along the
   rocker cover: a clearance knock usually has a clear point of maximum
   volume over one cylinder.
4. **Measuring the clearances.** On an adjustable valvetrain that is done
   with a feeler gauge on a cold engine and gives an unambiguous answer.

Being able to describe the dynamics of a sound to a mechanic speeds up the
diagnosis enormously — the same principle works elsewhere, for instance in
[brake squeal: pads or discs](/en/articles/brake-squeal-pads-or-discs/).

## How the repair is decided

The spread in cost comes from the design: on one engine access to the
valvetrain takes an hour, on another it means removing accessories and
drives. So it is worth asking a workshop not only for the total but for
what the job includes: the rocker cover gasket, adjustment shims and fresh
oil are often counted separately, and the difference between two quotes is
usually explained by those items rather than by margin.

Two more questions. Whether the clearances will be measured before
anything is ordered — on an adjustable engine that measurement is the
diagnosis, and shims are selected from it. And whether the oil passages
were assessed on a lifter engine: a new set of lifters fed through blocked
galleries ticks again within months.

If you hear ticking but are not sure which case it is, record the sound in
the Stuk app twice — right after a cold start and after ten minutes of
running. The difference between the two recordings is the key to the
answer, and the app shows which theory it points to.
