A rattle that appears the moment you accelerate and fades at a steady
speed is a sound with a double bottom. Most often it is harmless: under
drive the engine tilts, moves the exhaust, and somewhere underneath a
loose heat shield starts to buzz. But knocking — the destructive kind of
combustion that can kill pistons over thousands of kilometres — has
exactly the same timing. Here is how to tell one from the other and what
to check, in order.

## The mechanics: why the noise follows acceleration

The engine sits in the body on flexible mounts. When you press the
throttle, torque tilts the engine — noticeably on worn mounts, by a
couple of centimetres. The exhaust bolted to it moves as well: the front
pipe, the flexible section, the catalytic converter. From there, two
scenarios:

- the pipe or its shield **touches the body, the subframe or the steering
  linkage** — and you hear a rattle from below for exactly as long as you
  hold the throttle;
- the flexible section — the braided bellows that absorbs those
  movements — is **torn**, and it hisses and buzzes as it moves.

Lifting off returns the engine to its place and the noise disappears.
That clean link — drive on, noise on — is what separates a mechanical
rattle from most other sounds. A useful detail for observation: a
mechanical rattle is usually the same in any gear at the same pedal
effort, while noises that follow wheel speed or engine revs behave
differently and change with the gear.

## Rattle or knocking: the main question

Knocking is explosive combustion, heard as a sharp metallic ringing.
Confusing it with a heat shield is dangerous, so compare the signs:

| Sign | Shield or exhaust rattle | Knocking |
|---|---|---|
| Where the sound comes from | Below, under the floor | Ahead, from the engine bay |
| When you hear it | Under acceleration, sometimes at idle | Under load at low revs |
| Tone | Tinny buzzing, trembling | Sharp, rapid ringing |
| Reproducible while parked | Yes, by raising the revs | Usually not — it needs load |
| Link to fuel | None | Appears after refuelling, in heat, at altitude |

If the picture matches the right-hand column, start with the symptom page
[pinging when accelerating](/en/symptoms/pinging-when-accelerating/): it
covers knocking in detail, from octane rating to the knock sensor. A
rattle with a duller tone and jolts as you press the throttle is a reason
to check the engine mounts too — their knock is described on the page
[knocking when accelerating](/en/symptoms/knocking-when-accelerating/).

## What to check, in order

1. **The parked test.** Raise the revs to 2,000–3,000 in neutral. If the
   rattle appears, the source is in the exhaust and is then found by
   tapping and looking. If it does not, move to step 2.
2. **The load test.** On an empty road, accelerate in a high gear from
   low revs. Sharp ringing from the front is the knocking theory: check
   which fuel it started on and get it diagnosed.
3. **Look underneath.** On a lift or a ramp: signs of the pipe touching
   the body (shiny rub marks), a torn braid on the flexible section,
   loose shields, stretched hangers. Assess the engine mounts while you
   are there.
4. **Listen to the exhaust.** If it has become deeper or louder at the
   same time, corrosion has probably gone right through the system.

A separate case is a rattle under acceleration that appears only on a
cold engine and disappears after five minutes. That is the signature of
thermal clearance in the exhaust.

## Why knocking cannot wait

If the checks point at knocking, the attitude to the noise changes
completely. Explosive combustion creates shock waves that strike the
pistons, the rings and the ring lands — the metal breaks away gradually
and invisibly, and it ends in an engine rebuild.

The usual triggers are mundane: fuel with a lower octane rating than the
engine needs, spark plugs of the wrong heat range, a thick layer of
carbon in the combustion chambers, an engine overheating in traffic on a
hot day. Modern engines defend themselves with a knock sensor — it hears
the ringing and retards the ignition — but with a failed sensor or
consistently poor fuel that defence does not save you. The practical
conclusion: if ringing under load started after one particular fill-up,
begin with a full tank of fuel you trust; if the sound persists, book a
diagnosis and check the plugs rather than waiting for it to pass.

## How the repair is decided

The order of work follows the cost of being wrong. Cheap mechanical
causes come first: tightening a shield or clamping it takes minutes, and
the same visit shows whether the pipe is rubbing anywhere. A torn
flexible section is a defined job — but worth agreeing in advance whether
it is being replaced as a section or the whole front pipe, because on
many cars the flex is welded in and the choice affects both the price and
how long the repair lasts.

Only if the exhaust is sound does the engine come into the picture:
plugs, fuel, a scanner reading the knock sensor and ignition corrections.
In most cases the matter closes on the first line, and the point of the
inspection is to make sure this really is that case — and not knocking
wearing the mask of a harmless rattle.
