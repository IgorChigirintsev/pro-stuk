An alternator decoupler pulley is a pulley with a built-in one-way
mechanism, working like a bicycle freewheel: the belt can drive the
alternator rotor but cannot slow it down. It is an inconspicuous part, and
many owners first hear of it at a workshop — yet its wear is behind a good
half of the “whistles at startup” and “rattles under the bonnet” on cars
of the past twenty years.

## Why an alternator needs one

The crankshaft turns unevenly: each power stroke pushes it on, each
compression stroke holds it back. The belt passes those micro-pulses to
every accessory. Most of them tolerate it, but the alternator has a heavy
rotor spun to high speed which, by inertia, does not want to slow down
thousands of times a minute.

Without a decoupler the belt has to jerk the rotor back and forth: hence
vibration, squealing and accelerated wear of the whole drive. The
decoupler breaks that link — at every micro-deceleration of the crankshaft
the rotor freewheels past the belt and the pulse is absorbed. It matters
most on diesels and on engines with stop-start, where the unevenness is
greater.

## Signs of failure

A decoupler fails in two ways: either it stops gripping and slips, or it
seizes and becomes a rigid pulley. Both scenarios have recognisable
symptoms:

- **a whistle at the moment the engine starts and stops** — a worn
  decoupler slips exactly at those sharp changes of speed; the general
  breakdown of that sound is on the symptom page
  [squeal on startup](/en/symptoms/squeal-on-startup/);
- **rattling or chattering for a few seconds after switching off** — the
  spinning rotor carries on by inertia and a dying decoupler chatters like
  a bicycle freewheel;
- **visible belt vibration at idle** — the belt flaps between the pulleys
  because the pulses are no longer absorbed; against the general
  background that shows as engine shake too, see
  [vibration at idle](/en/symptoms/vibration-at-idle/);
- **undercharging** — a fully slipping decoupler stops turning the rotor
  under load: the charge light can come on at idle;
- **hum and rustle from the alternator pulley** — the decoupler's own
  bearings are worn.

## How to check it yourself

The check takes five minutes and does not need the alternator off. With
the engine stopped, remove the pulley's decorative cap (if fitted) and
hold the rotor shaft with a hex key through the central hole. Now turn the
pulley by hand:

- **one way** it should turn freely with a light rustle — that is the
  freewheel;
- **the other way** it should drive the shaft firmly — the working
  direction.

The verdicts: the pulley turns freely both ways — the decoupler is
slipping and not transmitting drive; it is rigid both ways — it has seized
and is working as a plain pulley. Either way the part needs replacing.
Also look for grease and rubber dust around the pulley and a chewed belt
edge.

## How the replacement is decided

A decoupler is not serviceable or greaseable — it is replaced. Removing it
needs a special multi-spline tool, so the work is usually combined with a
belt change or an alternator overhaul: the disassembly is shared and both
time and money are saved.

Two things worth asking. Whether the belt is being replaced at the same
time — a belt that has run on a seizing decoupler has taken the pulses the
decoupler was supposed to absorb, and often shows it in cracked ribs. And
whether the tensioner was checked against its wear indicator: a slipping
decoupler and a tired tensioner produce very similar belt noise, and
replacing one while ignoring the other leaves the sound in place.

Typical decoupler life is around 100,000 km, so its condition is always
worth checking at a scheduled belt change.
