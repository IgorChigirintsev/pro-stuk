A whine from the gearbox is a sound that rarely forces you to stop
immediately, but it almost always means the unit is already wearing
itself out. The question is usually not “drive or not drive” but “how much
is left and what happens at the end”. The answer depends on which part is
speaking up and how the sound behaves as you change gear and press the
clutch. Here it is in order.

## What actually whines inside a gearbox

In a manual gearbox there are not many sources of whine. Most often it is
the shaft bearings: the raceways wear, the clearance grows, and rotation
turns into an even high note tied to shaft speed. The second candidate is
gear teeth with a worn profile; that whine is heard in one particular gear
and disappears when you change to the next. The third is the release
bearing, but it only shows up with the clutch pedal down — see
[a whine with the clutch pressed](/en/articles/whine-with-the-clutch-pressed/).

An automatic whines differently: there the source is usually the valve
body pump or worn differential bearings. The giveaway is that the sound
depends on the operating mode and the oil temperature, not only on speed —
the early signs are in
[early signs of automatic transmission whine](/en/articles/automatic-transmission-whine-early-signs/).

The general logic: if the whine changes as you change gear, the problem is
inside the gearbox; if it is the same in every gear and in neutral while
rolling, check the wheel bearings and driveshafts. Telling those two apart
is covered in
[humming: gearbox or wheel bearing](/en/articles/humming-gearbox-or-wheel-bearing/),
and the sound portrait is on the symptom page
[humming while driving](/en/symptoms/humming-while-driving/).

## How urgent is it

| Sound picture | What is happening | Margin |
|---|---|---|
| Even whine in every gear, quieter in neutral | Early shaft bearing wear | Thousands of km, planned repair |
| Whine in one gear, quiet in the others | Wear on one gear pair | Thousands of km, but that gear goes first |
| Whine plus crunching on engagement | A synchro worn along with the bearing | Hundreds of km |
| Whine plus jolts and jerking | Valve body or clutch problems | Immediate diagnosis |
| Whine plus metallic grinding | The cage is breaking up, swarf in the oil | Do not drive |

The key divider is a second sound appearing on top of the first. While the
whine is alone and even, the gearbox is wearing predictably. As soon as a
thump, a crunch or grinding joins it, there is metal in the oil, and that
metal accelerates the wear of everything else — the process turns into an
avalanche. Crunching on engagement has its own breakdown in
[crunching when selecting a gear](/en/articles/crunching-when-selecting-a-gear/).

## Reducing the risk while you drive

Check the oil level and condition: it is the cheapest action with a real
effect. A low level, or oil of the wrong viscosity, turns moderate wear
into fast wear.

Then the driving regime. High revs and hard acceleration are bad for a
whining gearbox: the load on a worn bearing grows non-linearly. Calm
driving with early upshifts noticeably extends its life. It is also worth
avoiding towing a trailer and long loaded climbs. And keep the cost of a
tow in mind: if the sound changes sharply, calling one out is cheaper than
driving on to the point of destruction.

## How the repair is decided

The exact price is named after the box is opened, but the decision has a
structure worth knowing before you get a quote.

The first question is whether the noise is inside the gearbox at all. A
whining centre bearing on a propshaft, a worn CV joint or a wheel bearing
all get blamed on the gearbox, and each of them is a fraction of the cost.
Ask what was checked before the box was quoted.

The second is what comes out with it. If the gearbox has to come off, the
clutch, the release bearing and the rear main seal are reachable and the
labour is already paid for; refusing them and doing the same job again in
a year is the classic false economy here. On an automatic the equivalent
question is the oil and the filter, and whether the pan will be opened to
look for swarf — the answer to “is this a repair or a rebuild” is usually
lying in the pan.

The third is metal in the oil. Once it is there, replacing a single
bearing rarely ends the story, because the debris has been circulating
through everything. That is exactly why a whine caught early costs a
fraction of the same whine caught late.

A similar logic applies to other “tolerable” noises — for example a
whining water pump, covered in
[can you drive with a whining water pump](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-a-whining-water-pump/),
and ticking lifters, whose cost of delay is set out in
[what happens if you drive with noisy hydraulic lifters](/en/articles/driving-with-noisy-hydraulic-lifters/).
