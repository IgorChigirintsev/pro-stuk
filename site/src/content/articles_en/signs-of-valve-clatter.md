Valves are the cylinder's doors: the intake ones let air and fuel in, the
exhaust ones let the burnt gases out. Between the camshaft lobe and the
valve there is a small clearance — an allowance for the parts expanding as
they heat. While the clearance is correct the valvetrain runs almost
silently; when it drifts, a characteristic ticking appears. Here is how to
recognise valve clatter, why clearances change, and what an adjustment
involves.

## What valve clatter sounds like

The classic picture is **an even dry ticking from the top of the engine**,
like a fast sewing machine. The key markers:

- the sound comes **from under the rocker cover**, not from deep in the
  block;
- the frequency is **half crankshaft speed**: the camshaft turns half as
  fast, so the ticking sounds unhurried relative to the revs;
- the clatter is **audible at idle** and quickens with the revs;
- on a warm engine it does not disappear entirely and often becomes
  clearer.

Its temperature behaviour is a separate clue. The clearance is larger on a
cold engine, so light ticking after starting that fades with warm-up is
still within normal. A knock that persists or grows when hot, however, is
almost always a sign of genuinely enlarged clearances: the parts have
expanded and the sound remains, which means wear has used up the margin.

is a reason to check rather than a finished diagnosis. The lifter version

## Why the clearance drifts

On engines with manual adjustment the clearance changes through natural
wear, and the direction of the change matters more than the fact of it:

- **the clearance grows** when the camshaft lobe, the rocker or the valve
  stem tip wears — the valvetrain starts working with impact, hence the
  ticking;
- **the clearance shrinks** when the valve seat recesses and the valve
  sinks into the head — there is almost no sound, and therein lies the
  danger: the valve stops closing fully, loses the heat path through its
  seat and can burn, as covered in
  [signs of a burnt valve by ear](/en/articles/burnt-valve-signs-by-ear/).

LPG (hotter, drier combustion), overheating and long intervals without a
check all speed the process up. Engines with hydraulic lifters have no
manual adjustment — there the clearance is taken up automatically, and
ticking means problems with the oil or the lifters themselves; what
driving with that means is in
[what happens if you drive with noisy hydraulic lifters](/en/articles/driving-with-noisy-hydraulic-lifters/).

## The accompanying signs

Clatter rarely comes alone. Alongside it people usually notice:

| Sign | What it means |
|---|---|
| Even ticking from above that does not fade when warm | Enlarged clearances |
| Flat acceleration, misfiring at idle | A valve not closing, compression falling |
| Popping into the intake or exhaust | Valve closing timing disturbed |
| Fuel consumption up | The cylinders working inefficiently |
| Clatter appeared after an LPG conversion | Recessed seats, check the clearances urgently |

The reverse situation — signs with no clatter — also happens: the car has
become flat, consumption is up, there is a slight shake at idle, and there
is no ticking. On LPG engines that is the typical picture of shrunken
clearances, and it is more dangerous than the noisy one. So on gas you
cannot go by ear alone — a scheduled measurement is needed.

## How clearances are checked and adjusted

The check is mechanical: on a cold engine the rocker cover comes off and a
feeler gauge measures the clearance between the lobe and the follower for
each valve, against the manufacturer's table. What follows depends on the
design:

- **screw and locknut** — the clearance is set with a screwdriver and a
  spanner in a few minutes per valve; that is how many simple engines
  work;
- **shims or buckets** — a component of the right thickness is selected
  for each valve; the work takes longer and costs more, often with parts
  ordered and a second assembly.

The interval follows the specific engine's schedule, usually every
40,000–100,000 km. A practical rule: if an engine with manual adjustment
has started ticking and the service history is unknown (a recently bought
car), a clearance check is one of the first sensible steps.

Two things are worth clarifying before booking. First, which adjustment
method your engine uses — the price differs several-fold, and a mechanic
can tell you from the VIN or the engine code in advance. Second, whether
the specification calls for a cold adjustment: clearances are measured on
a cold engine, so the car is often left for several hours or brought in
first thing without warming up. While the cover is off, replacing the
rocker cover gasket makes sense — a second visit because of oil weeping
costs more than the gasket.

## What ignoring it leads to

The large-clearance scenario develops slowly: impact working peens the
valve tips and wears the lobes, and after tens of thousands of kilometres
the camshaft joins the bill. The small-clearance scenario is faster and
dearer: a valve that no longer seats overheats, burns, compression in that
cylinder falls, and the repair becomes a cylinder head job with a new
valve and seat work. So “quiet means fine” does not apply to valves: a
scheduled check is cheaper than either scenario.
