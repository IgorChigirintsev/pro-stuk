A whine that switches on exactly when your foot presses the clutch and
disappears the moment you let go is that rare case where the sound names
the culprit itself. That is how a release bearing behaves: a part that
spends most of its life doing nothing and works only while the pedal is
down. Here is why, how to tell it from other transmission noises, and what
the repair involves.

## What happens inside when you press the pedal

The clutch links the engine to the gearbox through a clamped disc. To
break that link, the pressure plate's diaphragm spring has to be pushed —
and that is the release bearing's job: it comes up against the rotating
diaphragm fingers and presses on them. While the pedal is up, the bearing
stands still and does not rotate. As soon as the pedal goes down, it is
pressed against the spinning pressure plate and starts turning at engine
speed.

Hence the character of the sound. Worn raceways only make themselves known
under load and rotation, that is, strictly with the pedal down. An even,
monotonous whine or rustle that grows with engine revs and vanishes
instantly when you release the pedal is this part's calling card.

## Checking it in a minute

The check is done with the car stationary, in neutral, with the handbrake
on:

1. Start the engine and let it reach operating temperature.
2. At idle, press the clutch smoothly to the floor and hold it for five to
   seven seconds. If a whine appears, the bearing is the suspect.
3. Release the pedal. The sound should disappear as sharply as it came.
4. Repeat at 1,500–2,000 rpm: release bearing whine changes pitch with the
   revs.

The mirror situation — a noise that instead disappears when you press the
pedal — points not at the release bearing but at parts behind the clutch.
If jerking when pulling away, a burnt smell and a loss of power on climbs
join the whine, the whole clutch assembly deserves a look rather than one
part.

## What else whines at that moment

| Source | How to tell it apart |
|---|---|
| Release bearing | Whine only with the pedal down, grows with revs |
| Input shaft bearing | Whines in neutral with the pedal up, fades when pressed |
| Release bearing guide sleeve | Not a whine but a creak or click as the pedal moves |
| Fork and cable linkage | Dry creak in time with pedal travel, audible with the engine off |
| Alternator, water pump, idlers | The whine ignores the pedal entirely |

The key divider here is a single thing: whether the sound is linked to the
pedal. If the whine lives its own life and does not react to the clutch,
look among the accessories instead.

## How the repair is decided

The deciding fact is that the gearbox has to come out, and that governs
everything else. Two consequences follow. First, the clutch is replaced as
a kit rather than one part: the disc, the pressure plate and the release
bearing have the same age and the same wear, and doing them separately
means paying the same labour twice.

Second, everything else that is only accessible with the box out belongs
in the same conversation: the rear main seal, the guide sleeve, and the
gearbox oil, which is drained anyway. A workshop that lists them is not
padding the estimate.

There is one more question worth asking in advance: whether your car has a
dual-mass flywheel and what condition it is in. It is inspected while the
clutch is out, and if it is worn, replacing the clutch alone brings the
vibration back within months. Finding that out after the gearbox is back
in is the expensive way.

By urgency, a release bearing is not an emergency part. Months usually
pass between the first whine and real trouble. But do not postpone
indefinitely: the whine turns into grinding, the pedal travel changes, and
a bearing that breaks up can damage the pressure plate fingers and turn an
ordinary repair into an expensive one.
