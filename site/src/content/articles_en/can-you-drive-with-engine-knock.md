There is no honest short answer to this question: everything depends on
what exactly is knocking. Some sounds let you drive calmly to a workshop
and even use the car for a week in a gentle regime; others turn every
kilometre into a lottery with a rebuild as the stake. Below is how to put
your knock into the “red” or “amber” zone in five minutes, the rules for
driving if you decide to drive, and why a tow truck is often the cheapest
line in the whole estimate.

## The red zone: switch off and call a tow truck

There are combinations of signs where driving on is not acceptable no
matter how far the workshop is:

- **a dull knock from deep in the block** that quickens with revs and grows
  under load — the signature of worn crankshaft bearings;
- **any knock together with the oil pressure light** — parts are knocking
  and there is nothing left to protect them;
- **a knock after losing oil** — a sump holed on a kerb or a track, a
  filter that has unseated, a fresh oil patch under the car;
- **metallic grinding, a sharp loss of power, heavy vibration** — signs
  that destruction is already under way.

The logic is simple: while the bearings are knocking, the repair is
limited to replacing parts; after a spun bearing or a seized crankshaft it
is a rebuild or a replacement engine. A tow across town is not comparable
with the difference between those two repairs.

Nobody can say how many kilometres are “left” with red signs: there are
cases of engines with knocking bearings covering a hundred kilometres, and
cases of seizure after five. Running the experiment on your own engine is
the most expensive way to find the answer. The wider list of sounds that
mean stopping is in
[noises that mean stop driving now](/en/articles/noises-that-mean-stop-driving-now/).

## The amber zone: you can drive to a workshop

Most engine knocks do not require abandoning the car on the hard shoulder:
in diagnosticians' experience a large share of “frightening” sounds turn
out to be the valvetrain, the accessories, or entirely normal operation.
You can usually drive calmly to a diagnosis with:

- **sharp ticking from the top** — hydraulic lifters; unpleasant but not
  critical, and the checks are in
  [how to check hydraulic lifters](/en/articles/how-to-check-hydraulic-lifters/);
- **even injector ticking** on direct injection — normal rather than a
  fault; the symptom page is
  [engine ticking](/en/symptoms/ticking-engine/);
- **pinging under acceleration after refuelling** — detonation on poor
  fuel; the first step is a different filling station and a look at the
  plugs, as set out in
  [metallic pinging under acceleration](/en/articles/metallic-pinging-under-acceleration/);
- **exhaust heat shield rattle** and knocking from accessories — the
  source is outside the engine itself;
- **a single thump when pulling away or changing gear** — engine mounts.

“You can drive there” does not mean “you can forget about it”: the amber
zone differs from the red one by having days and weeks for a diagnosis,
not months. A useful habit in the meantime is to check the oil level every
morning before starting and to note whether the sound is changing: a
stable knock and a progressing knock are different degrees of urgency.

## The traffic-light table

Both lists in one place. Use it like this: find the row that most resembles
your case, but whenever you are torn between two rows, take the more
cautious verdict — the cost of being wrong is not symmetrical here.

| Sound | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Dull knock from deep down, grows under load | Stop, tow truck | Bearings: a spun shell is close |
| Any knock plus the oil light | Stop, tow truck | Knocking without lubrication finishes the engine |
| Grinding, power loss, vibration | Stop, tow truck | Destruction is already under way |
| Sharp ticking from the top | Drive to the workshop | Hydraulic lifters, slow wear rate |
| Pinging under acceleration | Drive on, change filling station | Detonation on the fuel is likely |
| A single thump when pulling away | Planned diagnosis | Looks like engine mounts |

## If you decide to drive: the gentle regime

When the knock has been placed in the amber zone and the decision to drive
is made, there is one goal — not to let the problem move into the red zone
on the way. All the rules below come down to keeping the load on the parts
and the oil temperature minimal, and noticing any change at once:

1. **Check the oil level before starting** and top up if needed — the main
   insurance policy for the trip.
2. **Keep the revs below 2,000–2,500**, accelerate gently, no overtaking,
   towing or long climbs.
3. **Watch the oil pressure light and the temperature.** If the light
   flickers, stop right there, not “at the next filling station”.
4. **Listen to the sound.** A knock that grows noticeably as the engine
   warms or over the course of the drive is a reason to stop.
5. **Plan a short route** — to the nearest workshop, not across town and
   certainly not to another city.

A separate caveat for long distances: if the knock appeared on a road
where the nearest workshop is hundreds of kilometres away, the criteria
tighten. What in town would mean a fifteen-minute drive to a garage
becomes hours of loaded running in the heat. On a long trip any new knock
is a reason to stop at the next fuel station, let the engine cool, check
the oil level and judge soberly what is cheaper: waiting for a tow truck
or risking the engine.

## What not to do

Three typical mistakes that turn a cheap problem into an expensive one. Do
not pour in additives or thickeners before a diagnosis — they mask the
sound without removing the cause and stop the engine builder hearing the
real picture. Do not rev the engine to the limiter hoping the knock will
clear: with a lubrication problem that multiplies the wear. And do not put
the diagnosis off for months even if the knock is stable: the cost at an
early stage and at a late one differ by an order of magnitude. The general
catalogue of engine sounds is on the symptom page
[knocking in the engine](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-engine/).
