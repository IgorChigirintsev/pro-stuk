A Haldex coupling is the unit that engages the rear axle on all-wheel
drive cars with a transverse engine. In ordinary driving it barely
announces itself: it works in the background, engaging the rear axle as
needed. So owners usually describe the first signs of trouble not as a
breakdown but as an oddity — something crunched during a turn, something
jerked while parking. Here is how a struggling coupling sounds, what it is
confused with, and what is checked.

## How the unit works and why it makes noise

At the heart of a Haldex is a pack of friction plates compressed
hydraulically to transmit part of the torque to the rear axle. Electronics
control the process, a pump creates the pressure, and one small volume of
fluid serves as both the hydraulic medium and the lubricant. Hence the key
characteristic: the condition of the fluid directly determines how the
unit behaves.

Over time the fluid becomes saturated with wear products from the plates,
the filter blocks and the pump loses output. Compression of the pack
becomes imprecise: the plates alternately slip and grab with a jerk. It is
those jerks the driver hears as a crunch or a crackle during low-speed
manoeuvres, when the front and rear axles travel different distances and
the speed difference is at its greatest.

## The symptoms that identify the coupling

| Sign | When it shows itself |
|---|---|
| Crunching, jerking during a turn | At low speed, wheel on lock |
| Snatching while parking | During back-and-forth manoeuvres |
| All-wheel drive gone | Front axle spins, the rear does not help |
| An AWD warning on the dash | Constantly or intermittently |
| Hum and whine from the rear under power | At speed, rising with revs |

An important detail: the coupling almost never makes noise on a
stationary car. If the sound is heard at idle without moving, the
transmission is not where to look — the typical causes are covered in
[an engine knock at idle](/en/articles/engine-knock-at-idle/).

## What it is most often confused with

The main candidate for confusion is the outer CV joint. It also clicks
during low-speed manoeuvres, and by ear the sounds are similar. Three
signs separate them: the CV joint's sound comes from the front on one
particular wheel, it only appears with the wheel noticeably turned and
under power, and its clicking is strictly rhythmic with wheel speed. The
coupling sounds duller, comes from under the centre of the car and is more
often perceived as a jerk than as a chatter. The detailed portrait of the
joint is in
[signs of a worn outer CV joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-outer-cv-joint/),
with the general catalogue of manoeuvring sounds on the symptom page
[clicking when steering](/en/symptoms/clicking-when-steering/) and the
separation method in
[clicking: CV joint or brakes](/en/articles/clicking-cv-joint-or-brakes/).

The second candidate is the rear differential and the propshaft: the
centre bearing and universal joints give hum and vibration, but they are
tied to speed rather than to steering angle —
[signs of a worn propshaft centre bearing](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-propshaft-centre-bearing/).
The third is the brakes: a seized caliper can produce a grinding sound
during a turn that is easily blamed on the transmission. The fourth is a
difference in wear or size between the tyres on the two axles. All-wheel
drive with a coupling is sensitive to a permanent mismatch in wheel
speeds, and mismatched tyres can both provoke the jerking and accelerate
wear of the friction plates.

## Diagnosis and how the repair is decided

The check starts by reading fault codes: the coupling's electronics record
pump failures, sensor faults and entries into protection mode. Next the
fluid is inspected — its colour and smell say more about the state of the
friction plates than any external look. Then the unit's operation is
checked on a lift, watching whether the rear axle engages.

The criterion for the work follows from that order, and it is almost
always the fluid first. Scheduled servicing of the coupling — fluid and
filter — costs a fraction of restoring it, and this is the case where
saving on consumables almost guarantees a larger bill later. Only if fresh
fluid and a clean filter do not restore normal engagement does the pump or
the clutch pack come into the conversation.

Two things mechanics do not always say. The filter is a separate part from
the fluid and is sometimes quietly skipped — ask whether it is included,
because a new fluid pushed through a blocked filter is money wasted. And
before blaming the coupling, have the tyres checked for matching size and
tread depth across the axles: a mismatch there produces exactly these
symptoms on a healthy unit.
