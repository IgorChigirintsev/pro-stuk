Dampers die quietly. There is no moment when something breaks with a bang:
damping fades gradually, over tens of thousands of kilometres, and the
driver has time to get used to a car that wallows and holds the road less
well. That is why owners rarely complain about them — the diagnosis
usually turns up at a routine inspection. Here are the signs you can catch
without a test rig.

## How the car behaves

The first and most honest symptom is wallowing. After a speed hump or a
wave in the tarmac, the body should settle almost immediately. If it keeps
rocking two or three times, the hydraulics are no longer absorbing the
spring's energy. The same applies in corners: the lean is deeper, and
after a change of direction the car keeps leaning past where it should.

The second is bottoming out. A worn damper does not restrain suspension
travel, and over medium potholes the suspension reaches the bump stop —
you hear a dry impact where the same pothole used to be soft. The third is
braking: the nose dips noticeably more, the stopping distance grows, and
over bumps during braking the wheel starts to skip, so the ABS intervenes
earlier than it should.

## What an inspection shows

| Sign | How to judge it |
|---|---|
| Oil runs down the body | Clear runs with dust stuck to them — the part is finished |
| Scoring and corrosion on the rod | A damaged rod cuts the seal; its life is over |
| Destroyed bump stops and gaiters | Rubber turned to crumbs — the damper has been working at full travel |
| The body sitting differently left and right | A sagging spring or a strut that has lost its resistance |
| Uneven tyre wear | Patchy tread wear from the wheel bouncing |

A separate marker is temperature. After a brisk drive a healthy damper is
warm: it converts the energy of movement into heat. A damper that is cold
next to the others usually means it is no longer working. It is a rough
method, but on a lift it takes seconds.

## A five-minute check by hand

Start with the bounce test on each corner: press on the wing with your
weight and let go sharply. Count the movements — one or two is normal.
Then inspect the bodies and gaiters and run a finger along the rod. After
that, drive a familiar stretch with undulations at 60–80 km/h and see how
quickly the body settles.

Compare the feel with the car loaded, too. Worn dampers almost always show
themselves more with passengers and luggage: the suspension works closer
to the bump stops, and bottoming out becomes audible over bumps an empty
car took calmly.

If clicking or crunching appears when you turn the wheel while parked, the
cause may not be the damper itself but the top mount. And if you hear a
sharp single click over bumps and the steering behaves unpredictably, rule
out the more dangerous scenario first —
[how dangerous a ball joint failure really is](/en/articles/ball-joint-failure-danger/).
The full range of similar sounds is on the symptom page
[knocking in the suspension](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-suspension/).

## What it leads to, and how the repair is decided

Dead dampers are not only a comfort question. A wheel that bounces loses
grip, and the stopping distance on uneven surfaces grows noticeably. The
neighbours suffer as well: control arm bushes are destroyed, ball joints
and wheel bearings fail sooner, and tyres wear unevenly.

The economics here are simple: taking a strut apart twice costs more than
once. If the damper is coming off, assess the top mount, the bump stop and
the gaiter at the same time — replacing them at that moment adds almost
nothing to the bill, while doing them later means paying the same labour
again.

Two further points belong in any estimate. Dampers go on in pairs across
an axle. And wheel alignment afterwards is a required step rather than an
option, because replacing struts changes the geometry.
