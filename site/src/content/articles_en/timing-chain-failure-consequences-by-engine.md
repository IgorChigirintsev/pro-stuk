The timing chain synchronises the crankshaft and the camshafts — that is,
it keeps the pistons and the valves in agreement about who occupies the
cylinder volume and when. When the chain breaks or jumps the sprocket
teeth, that agreement vanishes instantly. What follows is decided by the
design of the particular engine, and the spread of consequences runs from
“we changed the chain” to “it is easier to look for a used engine”.

## What happens in the first second

The crankshaft keeps turning under its own inertia; the camshafts stop or
rotate to a random position. Some valves stay open while the pistons keep
travelling upwards. If the geometry of the combustion chamber allows them
to meet, the valves are bent — usually several at once, sometimes with
damage to the valve guides, the pistons and the cylinder head.

A separate scenario is a jump of one or two teeth without a break. It is
gentler: the timing shifts, the engine starts misfiring and losing power,
the check engine light comes on, but a piston does not always meet a
valve. Much depends on the driver's reaction — switching off at once, or
trying to “nurse it home”.

The difference between engines comes down to one parameter: whether the
piston crowns have deep enough reliefs for the valve heads. Engines where
those reliefs give sufficient clearance are called non-interference
designs — for them a break means a tow and a new timing drive. But the
pursuit of compression ratio and efficiency has made non-interference
designs a rarity: on the vast majority of modern petrol and diesel
engines the consequences of a break are mechanical.

## Three scenarios by severity

| Scenario | What happens | What the repair involves |
|---|---|---|
| A jump of one or two teeth | The engine misfires, loses power, camshaft faults; switched off in time | Chain replaced as a kit |
| Break on a non-interference engine | The engine stalls, the starter spins easily, no compression | New timing drive, sometimes head inspection |
| Break on an interference engine | Valves bend, pistons and head suffer | Cylinder head rebuild or a full engine rebuild |

The gap between prevention and consequences is roughly an order of
magnitude, whatever the local labour rates.

## Why a break almost always gives warning

A chain rarely snaps out of nowhere on a healthy engine. It is usually
preceded by weeks or months of symptoms: the lengthened chain sags, the
tensioner extends to its limit, the sprockets wear. To the ear that
appears as rattling or metallic rustling at the front of the engine,
loudest in the first seconds after a cold start while there is no oil
pressure in the tensioner. Then camshaft position faults, an uneven idle
and a loss of power join in.

The stage that is especially deceptive is when the cold-start noise lasts
a few seconds and goes away: it is easy to write off as “that is how the
engine wakes up”. The full cold-start sound picture is on the symptom page
[engine knock when cold](/en/symptoms/engine-knock-when-cold/), and the
sound portrait of a stretched chain in
[what a stretched timing chain sounds like](/en/articles/what-a-stretched-timing-chain-sounds-like/).

## What to do if it has already happened

The order of actions is short and strict. Switch off as soon as the engine
loses power and an unusual clattering appears. Do not try to restart:
every turn of the starter adds damage. Call a tow truck. Do not tow the
car on a rope in gear — that is the same as turning the engine on the
starter.

At the workshop the first steps are a compression test cylinder by
cylinder and an assessment of the timing drive. The result shows whether
you got away with a jump or the head has to come off. Either way it makes
sense to replace the whole drive rather than just the chain: worn
sprockets and a tired tensioner kill a new chain within a few thousand
kilometres.

One more thing workshops do not always volunteer: while the front of the
engine is open, the front crankshaft seal and, on many designs, the water
pump are reachable at no extra labour. Asking about them before the job
starts is cheaper than asking about them a year later.
