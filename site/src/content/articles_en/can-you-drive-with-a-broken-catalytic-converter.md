A catalytic converter is a block of ceramic honeycomb in the exhaust
system that burns off unburnt fuel. The ceramic is brittle: overheating,
an impact, poor fuel or a rich mixture crumble it over time. At first the
car simply rustles when you blip the throttle, then it starts choking
under acceleration. The question “can I still drive” is not rhetorical
here — the answer depends on which stage you are at.

## What is actually happening inside

The failure goes in two steps, and they are dangerous in different ways.
First the honeycomb cracks and part of the ceramic turns into debris that
moves freely inside the housing — the rustling stage. The exhaust is still
open, the engine runs almost normally, and what is lost is mainly the
emissions function.

The second step is sintering. The remaining fragments fuse into a plug and
the exhaust tract stops passing gas properly. The engine has to push
exhaust gases through a blockage: power drops, exhaust temperature rises,
the exhaust valves suffer, and on some engines the debris finds its way
back into the cylinders and scores them. It is the second stage that makes
driving genuinely risky.

The transition is not always gradual: sintering accelerates with
overheating, and the blockage itself causes the overheating. One long run
at high revs is enough to turn a rustling converter into a solid plug. So
“it still drives, so I still drive” works worse here than with most other
components.

## When you can drive on and when you cannot

| Sign | Drive to the workshop | Do not drive |
|---|---|---|
| Ceramic rustle | Present, power unchanged | — |
| Acceleration | Normal or slightly flat | The engine hits a ceiling in revs |
| Idle | Steady | Wanders, stalls |
| Smell and heat | None | Sharp smell, heat from the floor |
| Instruments | Check engine may be on | Coolant temperature rising |
| What to do | Drive calmly, book within days | Tow truck |

With a sintered converter a tow across town is cheaper than the
consequences of one long drive on a choked engine.

## Making sure it really is the converter

Three checks you can do. First, by ear: tap the converter housing with
your palm on a cold car. Distinct pouring inside means the honeycomb is
broken. Second, by feel at the wheel: if the car accelerates confidently
up to a certain speed and then hits what feels like an invisible wall,
that is the classic picture of a blocked exhaust. Third, a diagnostic
scan: converter efficiency and rear oxygen sensor codes give extra
confirmation.

Do not confuse the ceramic rustle with heat shield rattle or a loose
undertray — the sound is similar and the cost differs by an order of
magnitude. The full set of noises coming from underneath is on the symptom
page [rattling under the car](/en/symptoms/rattling-under-car/), and the
sound portrait in
[a broken catalytic converter: the sound](/en/articles/broken-catalytic-converter-rattle/).

## How the repair is decided

Two questions decide it, and neither is about the noise.

The first is why the converter died. Misfires, a leaking injector or a
rich mixture that cooked the ceramic will cook the replacement just the
same. A workshop that quotes a converter without reading the fuel trims
and misfire counters is selling you the same repair twice; ask what the
scan showed before agreeing to the part.

The second is what goes in its place and what your local rules require. An
original part, an aftermarket converter and a universal unit welded in
differ in price, in service life and in whether the rear oxygen sensor
will accept them — and emissions testing rules differ from country to
country, so the decision is not made on money alone. Find out what your
inspection requires before choosing.

The general logic of questions like this is always the same: while a part
makes noise but does not stop the car driving, you have weeks; as soon as
it starts affecting how the car behaves, the count is in days.
