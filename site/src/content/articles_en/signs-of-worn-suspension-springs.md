Suspension springs carry the whole weight of the car — engine, body,
passengers and load. Unlike dampers, which absorb oscillation, springs
take that oscillation: they compress on every bump, millions of times over
their life. The metal fatigues gradually, so worn springs are rarely
noticed in time — the car's behaviour changes so slowly that the driver
adapts. Below are the signs that catch spring fatigue early, simple checks
without a lift, and what decides the replacement.

## The main signs of wear

Tired or broken springs give themselves away with a set of symptoms, and
almost all of them are visible without tools:

- **The car has sagged.** Ground clearance is lower: the wheels look sunk
  into the arches and the car sits lower than in the photos from when you
  bought it.
- **A tilt.** One corner of the body is lower than the rest — a common
  case with a snapped coil or uneven fatigue.
- **Bottoming out in potholes.** The suspension bottoms out on a moderate
  defect with a hard metallic impact — a tired spring cannot hold the body
  up and the work falls to the bump stop.
- **Roll and float.** The car leans more noticeably in corners and rocks
  longer over undulations.
- **Knocking and creaking over bumps.** A snapped coil knocks against its
  seat or the next coil; dry coils creak over slow bumps.

A separate note about winter: in the cold the suspension rubber stiffens,
bottoming out happens more often, and a coil with a fatigue crack is more
willing to break after a cold night. If a knock appeared in the first
frosts, that is not a coincidence but a reason for an inspection.

Similar sounds come from other running gear parts — the lookalikes are on
the symptom page
[knocking in the suspension](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-suspension/).

## Signs and their likely causes

| Sign | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Even sag on both sides | Spring fatigue with mileage | Measure the ride height, compare with the spec |
| A tilt on one corner | A snapped or cracked coil | Inspect that wheel, replace the pair |
| Bottoming out with normal ride height | More likely the dampers than the springs | Suspension diagnosis |
| Metallic knock over bumps | A broken coil, less often an anti-roll bar link | Inspection on a lift |
| Creaking when rocked slowly | Dry contact between coils, worn isolators | Inspection, new isolators or springs |

## How to check the springs yourself

The first tool is a tape measure. Measure vertically from the centre of
the hub up to the edge of the arch on each side. A difference of more than
10–15 mm between left and right is a reason for a diagnosis; the figure
for your model is in the handbook.

The second step is inspection. Turn the wheels to full lock and shine a
torch on the spring: chipped paint, orange corrosion in the lower coils
and, above all, a fresh bright fracture are visible without dismantling.
The bottom coil breaks most often — it is closest to the road and the
first to suffer from water and salt.

Why look at the bottom coil first? It works in the most loaded zone while
being constantly blasted with sand and water from the wheel. The factory
coating is chipped through over time, moisture and salt get into the
micro-cracks, and corrosion undermines the metal from inside. A fatigue
crack grows unnoticed for months and the coil then breaks in an instant —
in a pothole or under a full load.

The third step is load. Bounce each corner of the body: the car should
compress springily and return with no knocks or creaks. If you hear a dull
thump specifically on compression, that resembles bottoming out — more on
that picture on
[knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/) and in
[signs of worn bump stops](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-bump-stops/).

## Springs or dampers: not confusing them

The two work as a pair and their symptoms overlap. The rough rule:
**springs are responsible for height, dampers for oscillation.** Sagging,
tilt and a permanently squatting rear are about springs. Long floating
after a bump, nose-diving under braking and losing contact with the road
on washboard surfaces are about dampers; their signs are in
[how to tell shock absorbers are dead](/en/articles/how-to-tell-shock-absorbers-are-dead/),
with the quick test in
[checking shock absorbers with the bounce test](/en/articles/checking-shock-absorbers-with-the-bounce-test/).

They are also linked: on sagged springs the damper works compressed,
bottoms into the bump stop more often and dies early. So when springs are
replaced it is worth assessing the dampers, and vice versa — what happens
if you do not is in
[driving with dead shock absorbers](/en/articles/driving-with-dead-shock-absorbers/).

## How the repair is decided

Springs are replaced with the strut removed and stripped, so the labour is
the same as for a damper. That drives every sensible decision here.

Springs on an axle are replaced in pairs — a single replacement leaves the
car uneven in height and stiffness. While the strut is apart it is worth
renewing the top mount bearing, the bump stop and the boot: the
disassembly is already paid for. And after the job the alignment should be
checked, because ride height changes the geometry.

On choosing parts, do not chase the lowest price: cheap springs made of
soft steel sag within a season or two, and the whole labour cost is paid
again. For cars that constantly carry loads or run on unpaved roads there
are heavy-duty versions — they hold their height longer, though they make
the ride slightly firmer over small stuff.
