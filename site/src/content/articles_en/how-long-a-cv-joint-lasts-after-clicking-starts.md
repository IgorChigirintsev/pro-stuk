The first clicks from a CV joint — the constant velocity joint that
carries torque to a steering wheel — are neither a verdict nor a reason to
abandon the car at the roadside. But nor should they be ignored for
months. The honest answer to “how much further will I get” is: from a few
hundred to tens of thousands of kilometres, and that whole spread is
decided by one thing — the state of the boot. Below are the guides by
stage, the factors that accelerate the failure, and the real risks.

## Why there is no exact figure

A clicking CV joint has two fundamentally different scripts:

- **The boot is intact, the wear is natural.** The grease is in place,
  there is no abrasive inside. The tracks wear slowly, and from the first
  single clicks to the need for replacement usually takes thousands, even
  tens of thousands, of kilometres.
- **The boot is torn.** The grease has been thrown out and there is water
  and sand inside. The joint works like a grindstone and is destroyed in
  thousands, or with hard use hundreds, of kilometres. In winter slush the
  process is faster still.

So the first action after the first clicks is not counting the remaining
life but inspecting the boot: turn the steering to full lock and shine a
torch into the pleats. An intact boot with no grease outside means there
is time. A split with grease flung about means the count is in weeks —
the detail is in
[a torn CV boot: how far can you drive](/en/articles/torn-cv-boot-how-far-can-you-drive/).

A third factor is how the car is used. The same joint in calm town driving
and in daily full-lock launches over broken yards wears at different
rates, so someone else's “I drove for a year with the clicking” transfers
poorly to your car.

## Guidance by stage

The table below is the typical picture, not a guarantee: a particular
joint can degrade faster, especially without grease.

| Stage | Sound | Typical margin left |
|---|---|---|
| First clicks | Single, only pulling away at full lock | Thousands of km with an intact boot |
| Regular rattling | Series of clicks in every turn under power | Hundreds to a few thousand km |
| Rattling in a straight line | Audible accelerating with barely any steering, vibration appears | Tens to hundreds of km, risky to drive |
| Play and thumps | Knocking on throttle/brake changes, vibration at any speed | Life used up, replace immediately |

How to recognise each stage by ear, and how to avoid confusing the outer
joint with the inner one, is set out in
[signs of a worn outer CV joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-outer-cv-joint/)
and on the symptom page
[clicking when steering](/en/symptoms/clicking-when-steering/). A useful
marker of the trend: if a week or two separated “occasional clicks” from
“rattling in every turn”, the boot is almost certainly torn and there is
no time to wait.

## What accelerates the failure

- **A torn or cracked boot** — factor number one, accelerating the wear
  tenfold.
- **Water and dirt**: deep puddles, fords, winter slush — all of it gets
  inside through the smallest crack in the pleats.
- **Sharp launches at full lock** and wheelspin: maximum angle plus
  maximum torque is the hardest regime for an outer CV joint.
- **Broken roads**: suspension impacts pass into the joint and batter
  already damaged tracks.
- **Towing and overloading** — more torque transmitted in every regime.

Remove from that list what is under your control and a clicking joint will
last noticeably longer.

## What the driver risks

It is useful to compare with another noisy component — the wheel bearing.
A bearing is dangerous because at its extreme stage it can skew or even
seize the wheel, so stretching it out is riskier — see
[can you drive with a humming wheel bearing](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-a-humming-wheel-bearing/).
A CV joint fails differently: it rarely seizes, more often loosening
gradually and finally breaking up — the cage disintegrates, the balls fall
out, and torque stops being transmitted. The car simply stops moving.

The main risks look like this:

- **being immobilised in an awkward place** — a motorway, a junction, a
  level crossing, with all the towing costs that follow;
- **failing under load mid-manoeuvre** — losing drive while overtaking or
  pulling onto a main road;
- **collateral damage** — fragments and a flailing shaft can catch boots,
  ABS sensors and neighbouring parts.

A separate trap is blaming the CV joint for someone else's sound. An even
hum that grows in a turn is a bearing's signature, not a joint's: check
against the symptom page
[humming when turning](/en/symptoms/humming-when-turning/) and
[signs of a worn wheel bearing](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-wheel-bearing/)
before ordering parts.

## Getting to the replacement without drama

- Drive gently: do not launch at full lock, do not spin the wheels, do not
  take corners with the throttle buried.
- Inspect the boot at once. If it tore recently and there is no clicking
  yet, or only the occasional click, a new boot with fresh grease can stop
  the process — cheaper than a new joint.
- Track the trend: note how often and in which regimes the clicks appear.
  A sharp increase means going to a workshop rather than waiting for next
  week's booking.
- Do not plan long motorway trips: a joint that fails far from home turns
  an inexpensive repair into expensive logistics.

## How the replacement is decided

The economics are simple: replacing at the first-clicks stage means one
joint and boots. Replacing after a failure usually means a complete shaft,
a tow truck and unplanned downtime. There is no need to replace joints in
pairs: if the other boot is intact and there are no sounds, it needs no
attention.

Two things worth confirming with the workshop: that the correct
molybdenum CV grease is used, and that the boot clamps are the proper
crimped type. Both are small details that decide whether the repair lasts
years or months.
