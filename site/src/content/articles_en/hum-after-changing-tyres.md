A familiar scenario: the car arrived for a seasonal wheel change quiet and
left with an even hum at speed. The first thought is that the fitters got
something wrong. Sometimes that is true, but more often the tyre change
merely altered the conditions in which a sound that already existed became
audible. The chronology helps: whether the hum was there from the workshop
gate or came out over a few days, and whether it changes in corners.

## What a tyre change actually alters

Changing a set of wheels changes several things at once. First, the tread
pattern itself: winter tyres with big blocks are louder than summer ones
with longitudinal grooves, and you hear it from the first kilometre.
Second, the position of specific tyres on the car: during rotation, a
sawtooth-worn wheel can move from the rear axle to the front, closer to
the cabin, and its hum becomes far more noticeable.

Third, the hubs are disturbed. A wheel tightened with uneven torque
distorts the brake disc and produces judder; a disc fitted onto a mounting
face that was not cleaned of rust does the same. And finally, removing the
wheel makes obvious a bearing that was already on the edge — until then
its hum was drowned by the noise of the old tyres.

## Working out whether the tyres are to blame

| Check | Tyres | Wheel bearing |
|---|---|---|
| Lane change left and right at 70 km/h | Hum unchanged | Volume changes |
| Moving from smooth to coarse tarmac | Noticeably different sound | The same sound |
| Running a hand across the tread | Steps can be felt | Tread is even |
| Spinning a raised wheel on a lift | Quiet | Rustling, hum, play |
| When the sound appeared | Right after fitting | Built up over weeks |
| Swapping wheels around | The hum travels | The hum stays |

The clearest check is the last one. Swap the front wheels with the rear
ones and drive the same route. If the hum has moved back with the wheels
and become quieter, the tyre is the cause. If it stays where it was, the
tyres are innocent and the hub needs checking. The second most useful
trick is to run your palm across the tread against the direction of
rotation: sawtooth wear feels like a row of small steps, and it is exactly
what produces that low hum.

## What to do with the answer

If the tread is the problem, the options are few. Uneven wear does not
smooth itself out again, but it can be slowed: get the alignment checked,
check the pressures and rotate the wheels to the manufacturer's pattern.
With severe sawtooth wear the tyre is replaced — the hum will keep
growing, and wet grip falls along the way.

If the hum is not about the tyres, the next step is a lift. The mechanic
raises the wheel, spins it by hand and listens, and checks for play by
rocking the wheel in two planes. The logic of assessing a speed-related
hum is covered in
[a hum on the motorway that vanishes in town](/en/articles/highway-hum-that-disappears-in-town/),
and the sound examples and check order are on the symptom page
[wheel bearing hum](/en/symptoms/wheel-bearing-hum/).

## How the repair is decided

The useful habit is to start with the cheapest step. Retightening the
wheel bolts and checking the pressures takes ten minutes and removes some
theories for nothing; only then does it make sense to pay for a diagnosis.

If a bearing is confirmed, two things shape the job: whether your car
takes a separate bearing pressed into the hub or a complete hub assembly,
and the hub nut torque, which decides how long the new bearing lasts. If
the tyres are confirmed, ask about the cause of the uneven wear rather
than only the tyre — alignment out of specification or tired dampers will
wear the new set the same way.

If you are not sure whether the sound changed after rotating the wheels,
do not rely on memory: a couple of decibels is almost impossible to judge
by ear. Record the hum in the Stuk app before and after the swap — it
compares the recordings with your answers and shows the likely causes with
percentages, so nobody replaces tyres where a bearing is at fault.
