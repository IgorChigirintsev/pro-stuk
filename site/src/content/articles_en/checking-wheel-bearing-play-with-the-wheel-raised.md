Checking a raised wheel for play is the basic procedure every chassis
diagnosis starts with. You can do it yourself: you need a jack, a solid
support and an understanding of which grip on the wheel tests which part.
That last point is what usually gets skipped, and the result is that the
play is found but the culprit is not. Here is the sequence that answers
not only “is there any” but “where”.

## Preparation: the boring but essential part

Park on level, hard ground — tarmac or concrete, not soil. Select a gear
or P, apply the handbrake, chock the wheels on the other axle. Lift at the
designated jacking point, and put the safety support in place immediately
after: an axle stand, or the removed wheel under the sill if you do not
have one. Rocking a raised wheel takes noticeable force, and a car on a
jack alone is dangerous at that moment.

A second detail is the handbrake. If you are checking a rear wheel on a
car with drum brakes, release the handbrake — otherwise the wheel is
locked and you will not feel part of the play. On the front axle, leave it
applied.

## Two grips, two different answers

| Grip on the wheel | What it tests | What play means |
|---|---|---|
| 12 and 6 o'clock | Wheel bearing, ball joint | Bearing or ball joint; separated by where the knock is felt |
| 3 and 9 o'clock | Track rod end, tie rod, rack | Play in the steering linkage |
| Vertically with the brake pedal held | Rules out play in the brake assembly | Play remaining means the chassis, not the brakes |
| Spinning the wheel by hand | Bearing and brake condition | Rustling, crunching, hum — worn raceways |

The brake pedal trick is worth remembering: ask a helper to press and hold
the brake and rock the wheel again. If the play disappears, the cause is
in the brake assembly or in the disc and hub; if it remains, the source is
higher up, in the suspension.

To separate the bearing from the ball joint, place a hand on the ball
joint while rocking: a click felt directly in your palm names the culprit
without doubt. The same trick works on a track rod end.

## What to listen for while the wheel is up

Since the wheel is in the air, spend another two minutes. Spin it by hand
and listen: an even rustle of the pads against the disc is normal, while
crunching, humming or an uneven noise that changes pitch is a sign of a
worn bearing. Note the resistance too: the wheel should manage at least a
couple of turns. If it stops almost immediately, check the caliper for
binding.

Inspect the CV joint boot while you are there: cracks and traces of grease
mean the joint will start clicking before long. If you hear metal grinding
on metal as the wheel turns, the pads are the subject — their sound
signature is on the symptom page
[knocking in the suspension](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-suspension/).

## What to do with the play you found

Play in the chassis is not something to observe for months: it grows
non-linearly and accelerates wear in the neighbouring parts.

Two things shape the repair. If it is the bearing, whether your car takes
a separate bearing pressed into the hub or a complete hub assembly decides
the price and the time — and the hub nut torque decides how long the new
one lasts. If it is a ball joint, ask whether it can be replaced on its
own or whether the whole control arm is required on this car: both answers
are legitimate, and knowing which applies explains the estimate.

Whatever is replaced, if it affects the geometry — arms, ball joints,
track rod ends — wheel alignment afterwards is part of the job, not an
optional extra.

A check of your own does not replace the workshop visit, but it changes
its character: you arrive with a specific theory and know what should be
confirmed on the lift.

If you found play but cannot tell which part produces it, record the sound
in the Pro-Stuk app on a rough stretch of road. The app matches the recording
with your answers and shows the likely causes with percentages — useful
insurance against extra items on the bill.
