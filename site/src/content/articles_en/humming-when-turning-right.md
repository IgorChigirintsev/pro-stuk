The car is quiet in a straight line, but in right-hand curves and lane
changes a low hum rises from under the wheels. That sound is rarely
accidental: most often it is a wheel bearing, and the direction of the
manoeuvre points straight at where to look for it. Here is the logic of
the diagnosis, a step-by-step check without a lift, the sound-alikes and
how the repair is decided.

## The side that hums points at the opposite wheel

A wheel bearing is the part the wheel turns on; it carries the weight of
the car at every moment of driving. In a right-hand curve, centrifugal
force leans the body left and most of the mass moves onto the left-hand
wheels. If the raceways of the left front bearing are already worn, the
added load makes them hum louder, while the right-hand side is unloaded
and stays silent. So a hum “to the right” usually means the left bearing,
and a hum “to the left” means the right one.

An important caveat: the rule works in gentle curves at 40–60 km/h and
above. In slow, tight turns — in a car park or a driveway — other
mechanisms and other suspects come into play, from CV joints to the power
steering.

## A step-by-step check in one outing

You need an empty, level road and ten minutes.

1. Accelerate to 60–80 km/h and note the baseline: is there a hum in a
   straight line?
2. Ease right, then left. Note in which curve the hum grows and in which
   it fades.
3. At the humming speed, select neutral and coast: if the hum stays, the
   engine and gearbox are out of suspicion.
4. Repeat the same stretch on a different surface: if the hum is
   unchanged, it is not the tyres.
5. After the drive, carefully bring a hand near the wheels: a hub that is
   noticeably warm on one side is extra evidence.

Write the result down in two lines: “hum from 60, grows to the right,
stays when coasting, ignores the surface.” For a mechanic that phrasing
cuts the diagnosis to ten minutes on a lift — all that is left is to raise
the wheels, spin them and rock them in two planes.

## The sound-alikes

| What you notice | Likely source |
|---|---|
| Hum with the car parked while steering | Power steering pump |
| The sound changes with the road surface | Tyres |
| Crunching or clicking in a tight turn under power | Outer CV joint |
| The hum follows the throttle, not the corners | Differential, transmission |
| A constant hum in a straight line with no reaction to manoeuvres | A late-stage bearing, or tyres |

The most common lookalike is the power steering: its pump howls when you
turn the wheel, but unlike a bearing it does so while parked as well, and
road speed has no effect — that case is covered in
[power steering whine when turning](/en/articles/power-steering-whine-when-turning/).
A constant hum with no clear link to manoeuvres is a separate problem with
its own list of causes, collected on the symptom page
[humming while driving](/en/symptoms/humming-while-driving/), and the
sound portrait of a worn hub is on
[wheel bearing hum](/en/symptoms/wheel-bearing-hum/).

## How the repair is decided

Two things keep the bill honest. First, do not agree to “a pair while we
are here” without play or noise on the other side: bearings are replaced
individually. Second, find out how your car is built before the work
starts — a separate bearing is cheaper as a part but needs a press, while
a complete hub assembly costs more and goes on faster, with no risk of a
pressing error.

One more detail that decides how long the repair lasts: the hub nut
torque. Over- or under-tightening destroys a new bearing within thousands
of kilometres, so a torque wrench rather than an impact gun is what you
want to see. And on many modern cars the ABS sensor sits inside the hub
assembly, which is why a failing bearing sometimes brings warning lights
along with the noise.
