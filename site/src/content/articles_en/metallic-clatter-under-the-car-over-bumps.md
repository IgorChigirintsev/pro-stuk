Clatter is not simply a knock but a ringing metallic rattle, as though a
piece of iron were loose under the floor. Most often that is exactly what
it is: what clatters is a part that has lost its mounting, not a worn
loaded component. That is good news — most causes of clatter are cured by
tightening fasteners for very little money. Here is what rattles under the
car, how to find the source in five minutes, and when clatter does mean
serious wear.

## How clatter differs from a knock

A dull knock is the sound of an impact through rubber: that is how control
arm bushes, dampers and mounts knock. Clatter is metal contacting metal
with no damper in between. In a suspension, metal should not strike metal
anywhere, so clatter immediately narrows the search to two scenarios:
either something has come loose and is hanging (undertray, heat shield,
exhaust), or a joint has worn dry — down to direct contact between metal
parts. The general breakdown of rattling from below is on the symptom page
[rattling under the car](/en/symptoms/rattling-under-car/).

## The simple causes: undertray, shields, exhaust

Top of the statistics is the **undertray**: the original bolts and clips
get lost over time and the steel sheet starts jumping on every bump.
Second are the **exhaust heat shields**: thin sheet metal corrodes around
the fixing points and rattles both over bumps and at particular engine
speeds — the search procedure is in
[finding a rattling heat shield](/en/articles/finding-a-rattling-heat-shield/).
Third are the **exhaust hanger rubbers**: stretched rubber rings let the
silencer swing and ring against the body.

All of this is checked by hand on a cold car: rock the undertray, tug the
exhaust, tap the shields with your palm. The rattle you find is audible
immediately.

## When it is the suspension that clatters

| Sign | Where it sounds | Likely cause |
|---|---|---|
| Rattle over fine ripples | Under the front | Undertray |
| Clatter with revs, even parked | Under the centre of the floor | Heat shield, exhaust |
| Ringing clatter over potholes with a knock | At a wheel | Anti-roll bar link worn dry |
| Clatter pulling away and over bumps | Under the rear | Exhaust hangers |
| Rattle from the boot | At the back | Jack, tools, luggage |

Of the suspension parts, the ones that most often survive to a genuine
metallic clatter are the **anti-roll bar links**: when the plastic insert
in the joint is completely worn away, the pin flops in its housing and
rings on every bump. That is the final stage of wear — earlier on it
sounds duller. What happens if you leave them is set out in
[what happens if you do not replace anti-roll bar links](/en/articles/not-replacing-anti-roll-bar-links/).
Anti-roll bar bushes end the same way, rubbed through to the bare bar.

## How to check in five minutes

1. **Cabin and boot.** Take out the jack, the tools and anything that can
   rattle — and drive the same stretch again.
2. **Blip the throttle while parked.** If the clatter is audible as the
   revs rise with the car standing still, the exhaust or a shield is to
   blame and the suspension has nothing to do with it.
3. **Hands.** With the car cold, rock the undertray, the exhaust and any
   visible shields.
4. **A helper.** Rock the car by a wing — a clatter at a wheel points to
   the anti-roll bar.

If five minutes do not find the source, the full self-search method is in
[finding a suspension knock without a lift](/en/articles/finding-a-suspension-knock-without-a-lift/),
and you can compare your sound with the typical ones on
[knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/). Clatter that
appears specifically over potholes at the front is covered in
[clattering under the car over bumps](/en/articles/clattering-under-the-car-over-bumps/).

## How the repair is decided

Most of the work here is fasteners rather than parts, and that changes
what to ask for. If the undertray fixings have pulled out, the honest fix
is new clips and, where the mounting point is torn, a repair washer or a
bracket — not one bolt through the remaining hole, which tears out again
within a month. If the heat shield has corroded around a fixing, a large
washer over the sound metal holds; a shield that has lost half its
perimeter is replaced rather than patched.

Ask which of the two you are getting. A workshop that says only “we
tightened it up” has not told you whether the fastener is now holding on
sound metal or on what was left of it.

There is not much danger in clatter itself, but two scenarios are worth
not leaving: a loose undertray can tear off at speed, and a swinging
silencer pulls off its remaining hangers and hangs on the pipe.
