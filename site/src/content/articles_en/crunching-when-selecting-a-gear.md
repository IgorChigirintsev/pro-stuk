Crunching at the moment the lever goes into gear is an unpleasant,
memorable sound: a short metallic rasp, as if something were slipping over
teeth. Mechanically that is exactly what happens. But the source varies,
and which one it is separates replacing an inexpensive part from opening
the gearbox. Here is what crunches, how to work out the cause from the way
the car behaves, and how the repair is decided.

## What a synchro does

Inside a manual gearbox the gears are permanently meshed, and a gear is
selected by a sleeve that locks the chosen gear to the shaft. The problem
is that the shaft and the gear are turning at different speeds at that
moment. The synchro — a small ring with a conical face — equalises their
speeds in a fraction of a second through friction, and only then does the
sleeve engage.

The synchro ring wears: the cone rubs away, the teeth round off, and there
is no longer enough time to equalise. The sleeve engages before the speeds
have matched, and you hear the crunch. The clear marker of synchro wear is
crunching in one particular gear, most often second, while the rest engage
cleanly. And a telling detail: shift slowly and there is no crunch; shift
quickly and there is.

## When the gearbox is not to blame

| Cause | How it shows | The check |
|---|---|---|
| Worn synchro | Crunch in one gear, usually second | Slow engagement is quiet |
| Clutch dragging (not disengaging) | Several gears crunch, first is hard to select while parked | Gear engages badly with the car stationary |
| Air or a leak in the hydraulic linkage | Soft pedal, sinking to the floor | Fluid level, hose condition |
| Worn shift linkage or cables | The lever moves too freely | Play in the lever in neutral |
| Low gearbox oil | Crunching worse when cold | Check the level |

A dragging clutch is arguably more common than worn synchros, and cheaper
if caught in time. The sign is simple: with the engine running and the
pedal to the floor, first gear engages with resistance and a crunch even
though the car is stationary. That means the input shaft is not stopping.

## Checking it yourself

1. **The first-gear test while parked.** Engine running, pedal to the
   floor, wait two seconds and select first. A crunch with the car
   standing still is a question for the clutch, not the synchros.
2. **Slow engagement.** Repeat the problem gear while driving, moving the
   lever at half your usual speed. Silence when slow and a crunch when
   quick is classic synchro wear.
3. **The cold test.** Check whether the crunch is there on a cold gearbox
   and whether it goes away after 10–15 minutes of driving. A difference
   points at the oil: thickened, or the wrong viscosity.
4. **The pedal.** Notice whether the pedal travel has changed and whether
   new sounds have appeared as you press it. The general guide to noises
   under acceleration is on the symptom page
   [knocking when accelerating](/en/symptoms/knocking-when-accelerating/).

## How the repair is decided

The order of steps follows cost. A gearbox oil change is the first move
when the crunch is worse cold — it is cheap, and the specification matters
more than the brand: many gearboxes need oil with a friction modifier
package designed for their synchros, and generic gear oil makes shifting
worse rather than better.

If the clutch turns out to be dragging, the cause may be as simple as air
in the hydraulic line or a worn master cylinder — both far cheaper than
the clutch itself, and worth ruling out before anyone quotes for a kit.

If it really is the synchros, the deciding fact is that the gearbox has to
come out. That means the clutch kit is replaced at the same time, along
with the rear main seal and the gearbox oil: the labour is already paid
for, and doing them separately later means paying it twice.

You can drive with a crunch, but every unsuccessful shift wears the ring
further, and one day the gear will refuse to engage at all. If one gear
crunches, plan the repair calmly. If the crunch has spread to several
gears and shifting has become hard, have the car looked at within days.
