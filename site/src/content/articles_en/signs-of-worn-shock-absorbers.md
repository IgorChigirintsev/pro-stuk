A damper is the part that absorbs the spring's oscillation and keeps the
wheel pressed to the road. Unlike anti-roll bar links or a wheel bearing,
it rarely announces itself with one vivid symptom: its effectiveness fades
gradually and the driver quietly gets used to a car that has started to
float. Below are the signs that catch the wear objectively, the checks you
can do without a test rig, and what decides the replacement.

## How a damper works and why the wear is invisible

The suspension spring compresses on a bump and wants to spring back —
without resistance the body would rock like a boat. The damper creates
that resistance: a piston on a rod forces oil through calibrated valves,
turning the energy of the oscillation into heat.

The valves, the rod seals and the oil itself all wear. The process is
spread over tens of thousands of kilometres, so “it was fine yesterday and
bad today” almost never happens with dampers — except after a hard pothole
strike or oil lost through the seal. The practical conclusion: trust
specific signs rather than a feeling that everything is as it always was.

A note on terms: a strut and a damper are not the same thing. The damper
is the damping element; a strut (a MacPherson type, for instance) is the
complete assembly where the damper is combined with the spring and the top
mount and forms part of the suspension.

## Eight signs of wear

1. **Floating after a bump.** A healthy suspension absorbs the oscillation
   in one or one and a half movements. If the body rocks two or three
   times after a speed hump, the dampers have done their time.
2. **Nose-diving under braking and squatting under acceleration.** The
   front dives deeply at every ordinary stop, the rear squats when you
   pull away.
3. **Bottoming out.** Dull hard impacts in potholes the car used to
   swallow: the spring compresses onto the bump stop because the damper is
   not resisting in time.
4. **Knocking.** A worn damper can knock itself (piston play, worn
   mounting bushes) — a duller sound over medium and large bumps. Other
   parts knock similarly; the lookalikes are on the symptom page
   [knocking in the suspension](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-suspension/).
5. **Oil weeping on the body.** A wet body and dirt trails on the boot
   mean the rod seal is passing oil. Light misting means watch; a clear
   leak means replace.
6. **Patchy tyre wear.** A wheel that skips over bumps wears the tread
   unevenly — in patches around the circumference.
7. **Uncertainty at speed.** The car floats in its lane, follows ruts,
   needs constant corrections and leans more than it used to in corners.
8. **A longer stopping distance on rough surfaces.** The wheel leaves the
   road more often and the ABS works earlier and longer, as covered in
   [driving with dead shock absorbers](/en/articles/driving-with-dead-shock-absorbers/).

No single sign is a verdict, but two or three from the list are a solid
reason for a diagnosis.

## What it is easily confused with

| Sign | What else it resembles | How to tell |
|---|---|---|
| Knock over bumps | Anti-roll bar links and bushes, control arm bushes | The links' knock is fine and frequent and quietens in corners; a damper's is duller and less frequent |
| Floating, roll | Sagged springs | Springs show as lost ride height; dampers show as floating |
| Bottoming out | Broken bump stops, sagged springs | Inspection on a lift: the state of the bump stops and spring height |
| Wandering in the lane | Alignment, worn tyres, steering play | Check the wheel angles and play before replacing dampers |

The most common neighbour by symptoms is the anti-roll bar links: they
also knock over bumps, but their sound is finer and more frequent. The
detailed breakdown is in
[signs of worn anti-roll bar links](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-anti-roll-bar-links/),
sagged springs in
[signs of worn suspension springs](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-suspension-springs/),
and bottoming out in
[signs of worn bump stops](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-bump-stops/).

## How to check them yourself

- **Inspection.** Turn the wheels and look at the damper bodies: oil
  weeping, a rusted or torn boot, impact marks. Look at the tread while
  you are there — patchy wear is visible and can be felt.
- **The bounce test.** Push firmly down on a corner of the body and
  release: a healthy suspension returns in one movement, a worn one keeps
  rocking. The method with its caveats is in
  [checking shock absorbers with the bounce test](/en/articles/checking-shock-absorbers-with-the-bounce-test/),
  and the wider set of signs in
  [how to tell shock absorbers are dead](/en/articles/how-to-tell-shock-absorbers-are-dead/).
- **The road test.** On an empty stretch, brake smoothly from 60 km/h:
  excessive dive and floating after the stop speak for themselves.
- **A workshop rig.** A shaker rig measures how well each wheel keeps
  contact and gives numbers per corner. Useful in disputed cases, but it
  does not replace an inspection.

Damper life on broken roads is roughly 60,000–100,000 km; past that, the
bounce test and an inspection are worth doing at every service. A separate
reason for an unscheduled check is a hard pothole or kerb strike: an
impact can bend the rod or damage the valves, and the damper fails in one
go with no gradual decline.

## How the repair is decided

Dampers are replaced in pairs on an axle — a car with differently worn
dampers holds the left and right sides unequally.

Two things worth settling before the job. First, whether the strut will be
stripped: on most front struts it will, which is the moment to renew the
top mount bearing, the bump stop and the boot for the price of the parts
alone. Second, whether an alignment follows — on most cars the front
geometry is disturbed when a strut comes off.

On parts, cheap dampers vary far more in valving than in appearance; a
mismatched pair, or a very soft aftermarket unit on a heavy car, floats
again within a season. Matching the damper to the car and to how it is
used matters more than the badge.
