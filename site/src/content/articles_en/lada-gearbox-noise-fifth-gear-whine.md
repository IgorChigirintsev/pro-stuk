An even whine that appears at cruising speed and is especially clear in
fifth gear is a familiar story for owners of Ladas with a manual gearbox.
The sound usually grows over months, which makes it easy to blame on the
tyres or the road. Here is why the top gear is usually the one that goes,
how to separate the gearbox from the running gear in a single drive, and
what a workshop does about it.

## Why the top gear suffers

Fifth gear in these gearboxes sits at the end of the casing, furthest from
the zone where the gears throw the oil around most. Lubrication reaches it
on a leftovers basis — especially if the oil level has dropped below
specification. And on a motorway it is exactly that pair that works for
hours under steady load at high shaft speed.

Add the typical local factors: infrequent gearbox oil changes, hard town
driving fully loaded, towing, the legacy of past impacts. Together they
mean the output shaft bearings and the teeth of the outermost pair wear
faster than the rest — first a rustle appears, then an even whine that
over time starts being audible in fourth as well.

## What actually makes the noise inside

| Source in the gearbox | How it shows |
|---|---|
| Shaft bearings | Even whine, grows with speed, changes when you select neutral |
| Worn gear teeth | Whine with a howl, different in different gears |
| Low oil level | The noise grows on a warm gearbox and on the motorway |
| Worn synchro | Not a whine but crunching and stiff engagement |
| Release bearing | The noise changes when the clutch pedal is pressed |

The simplest check works with the clutch and the gear. If the sound
changes when you press the clutch in neutral, the conversation is about
the input shaft or the release bearing —
[gearbox whine in neutral](/en/articles/gearbox-whine-in-neutral/) covers
that. If the whine depends on the gear selected and fades when coasting in
neutral, it is the gearbox's gears and bearings. If it reacts to neither
and follows only road speed and the direction of the curve, the gearbox is
not involved: look at the wheel bearings and tyres. Separating those is
covered in
[humming: gearbox or wheel bearing](/en/articles/humming-gearbox-or-wheel-bearing/)
and the full breakdown is on the symptom page
[humming while driving](/en/symptoms/humming-while-driving/).

## What not to confuse it with

A frequent mistake is taking engine bay sounds for the gearbox.
Transmission whine always follows road speed: as the car slows, the pitch
falls. Engine sounds follow revs and persist with the car standing still —
that is the first thing to separate them by. A wheel bearing has its own
portrait in
[signs of a worn wheel bearing](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-wheel-bearing/).

The practical order of checks: first the level and condition of the
gearbox oil — metallic suspension and a burnt smell say a lot by
themselves; then listening on a lift with the wheels raised; then a test
drive with a mechanic, catching the sound in each gear in turn. Only after
that does it make sense to talk about opening the gearbox.

## How the repair is decided

Always start with the cheapest step: the oil. Bringing the level up to
specification and refilling with fresh oil of the correct grade sometimes
reduces the whine noticeably, if the wear has not gone far. Two things are
worth knowing here. The specified oil grade matters more than the brand —
a gearbox filled with the wrong viscosity whines from the first week. And
if the drained oil comes out with a glitter of metal, that changes the
conversation: fresh oil buys time but the wear is already producing
debris.

If the sound stays the same afterwards and keeps growing, a gearbox repair
follows, and it is better planned in advance than met as a breakdown on
the road. Ask for a split quote — bearings and seals alone, or gears as
well — and for the old bearings to be shown afterwards. Where the line
runs between “drive on” and “stop” is set out in
[can you drive with a whining gearbox](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-a-whining-gearbox/).
