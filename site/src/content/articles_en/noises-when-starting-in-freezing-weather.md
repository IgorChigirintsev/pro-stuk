A hard winter is a stern test for any engine: in continental climates
morning temperatures of −25 to −30 °C can hold for weeks. After a night
like that even a healthy car starts with a concert: something squeals,
something ticks, and the engine growls more coarsely than usual for the
first seconds. Most of those sounds are the physics of cold rather than a
fault. But frost is also the best revealer of wear: a part that was silent
in summer is the first to speak up in winter. Here is the winter orchestra
by instrument.

## Squeal and yelp: the belt and its idlers

The commonest winter sound is a squealing yelp in the first seconds after
starting. The drive belt's rubber stiffens in the cold and the belt slips
on the pulleys until friction warms it. A short squeal is a signal that
the belt is ageing or the tension has slackened; a squeal lasting until
warm-up means there is nothing left in reserve. A separate participant is
the idler and tensioner bearings: thick grease in them makes a hum or
chatter in frost.

## Knocking and diesel clatter: thick oil and the piston group

The second most common story is an engine that knocks or clatters like a
diesel for the first minutes and then runs smoothly. In frost the oil
thickens several times over: the pump needs longer to force it through the
galleries, and while there is no oil the hydraulic lifters tick, the chain
rustles and the pistons slap in their wide cold clearances. The better the
viscosity is chosen — low-viscosity 0W and 5W grades suit hard winters —
the shorter that period.

The line between normal and not is duration and trend. A couple of minutes
of morning ticking at −30 °C is explicable; a knock that coarsens week by
week or does not go with warm-up is not. The full map of engine knocks by
type of sound is in
[engine knock: all the causes by sound](/en/articles/engine-knock-all-causes-by-sound/)
and
[an engine knock only when cold](/en/articles/engine-knock-only-when-cold/),
and you can compare your morning sound with typical ones on the page
[engine knock when cold](/en/symptoms/engine-knock-when-cold/).

## Grinding and clicking: the starter and the battery

The third group sounds before the engine catches. A single click under the
bonnet and then silence is the classic flat battery: the starter solenoid
clicks but there is no current left to crank. A series of rapid clicks is
the same thing, the solenoid chattering on what charge remains. Grinding
at start-up means the starter pinion is not engaging the flywheel ring
gear properly, or the ring gear itself is worn: in frost, with thickened
grease, that defect shows up sooner —
[the starter clicks but will not crank](/en/articles/starter-clicks-but-wont-crank/)
and
[a grinding noise when starting the engine](/en/articles/grinding-noise-when-starting-the-engine/).

Frost cuts a battery's capacity almost in half, so a battery that seemed
healthy in summer is the first to give up in winter. An age over four or
five years and dim headlights while cranking are reasons to test it before
the cold arrives.

## A winter cheat sheet: sound, cause, action

| Sound | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Squeal for 2–5 seconds | Stiffened belt | Check the belt and tension |
| Squeal until warm-up | Worn belt or idlers | Workshop this week |
| Ticking for 1–3 minutes | Thick oil, hydraulic lifters | Check the oil viscosity |
| Diesel clatter, quieter with warm-up | Piston skirts, clearances | Watch the trend, endoscope |
| Dull knock that does not go | Bearings, bore scoring | Do not drive, diagnose |
| Clicks without cranking | Battery | Charge or replace |
| Grinding while cranking | Starter pinion, ring gear | Diagnose the starter |

A separate winter rule: sounds in the first few hundred metres can also
come from frozen running gear — the suspension creaks on hardened rubber,
and brake discs rustle with overnight surface rust. Those go within the
first kilometres and have nothing to do with the engine, as covered in
[suspension knock in cold weather](/en/articles/suspension-knock-in-cold-weather/)
and
[brake squeal after standing](/en/articles/brake-squeal-after-standing/).

## What must not be blamed on the frost

Winter explains a great deal, but not everything. A heavy knock from the
bottom of the block that persists on a warm engine is the bearings or the
piston group, and frost is only the messenger here: the part is worn, and
dry winter starts finish it off faster. With that sound, the question of
driving or towing is settled in favour of a recovery truck — the arguments
are set out in
[can you drive with engine knock](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-engine-knock/).

## How the decision is made

The criterion through all of this is the same and it is free: duration and
trend. Write down how long the morning sound lasts, at roughly what
outside temperature, and compare a month later. A sound that lasts the
same number of seconds through the winter is physics; a sound that lasts
longer every few weeks is wear, whatever the thermometer says.

Two things worth doing before the cold rather than during it. Check the
oil viscosity against the manufacturer's specification with an allowance
for the climate — that single line closes a large share of morning ticking
for the price of a scheduled oil change. And have the battery tested if it
is past four years: a battery replaced in autumn costs the same as one
replaced in January, minus the tow.
