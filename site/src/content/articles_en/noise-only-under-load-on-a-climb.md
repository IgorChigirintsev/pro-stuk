There is a separate category of sounds that cannot be caught on a level
road: the car runs quietly, but start a climb or accelerate hard and a
squeal, a ringing or a crunch appears. On a long mountain pass or a
motorway drag they are especially clear. That is neither an accident nor a
caprice: load is a full diagnostic tool that narrows the list of suspect
parts by itself.

## Why load reveals a fault

When the car climbs, the engine delivers more torque, and the whole path
of that torque — from the crankshaft through the clutch and gearbox to the
wheels — comes under increased pressure. Worn parts are loaded to their
maximum at that moment: worn splines are pressed through, joints work at
the limit of their angle, the belt tries to slip on its pulley. Everything
that just about held on a level road breaks loose under load — and you
hear it.

The second mechanism is thermal and fuel-related. Under load the pressure
and temperature in the combustion chamber rise sharply, and if the petrol
does not match the engine's compression ratio the mixture starts to ignite
spontaneously. That is detonation — a bright metallic ringing, otherwise
known as pinking. On a level road it is absent, because there the engine
works at half effort.

## Reading the part from the character of the sound

| Sound under load | Likely source |
|---|---|
| Squeal, whine, squeak when pulling away uphill | The drive belt slipping |
| Bright ringing, like glass beads | Detonation: fuel, carbon, sensors |
| Crunching, chatter, clicks in a turn on a climb | Outer CV joint |
| Dull knock as the throttle is applied | Engine mounts, play in the driveline |
| A whine rising with revs rather than speed | Accessories: water pump, alternator, idler |

The key distinction is what the sound is tied to: engine revs or road
speed. It is easily checked. Accelerate to the speed at which the sound is
audible and change up a gear — the revs fall, the speed stays. The sound
went with the revs — look in the engine bay. It stayed — the source
rotates with the wheels and driveshafts. The full method is in
[the sound changes when you select neutral](/en/articles/the-sound-changes-when-you-select-neutral/).

## Three checks you can do yourself

1. **The cold start test.** Start the car in the morning and apply
   moderate throttle straight away. A belt that has lost its tension
   squeals specifically when cold and quietens after warm-up. The detailed
   breakdown is in
   [alternator belt squeal: the causes](/en/articles/alternator-belt-squeal-causes/).
2. **The turn-under-power test.** On an empty area, turn the wheel to full
   lock and drive slowly in a circle with light acceleration. Crunching and
   chatter mean the outer constant velocity joint — the one that transmits
   torque to a steered wheel,
   [checking a CV joint by driving circles](/en/articles/checking-a-cv-joint-by-driving-circles/).
3. **The fuel test.** If the sound is bright and only appears at full
   throttle, fill up with petrol one octane rating higher at a different
   station and repeat the climb. Gone — the question was the fuel, as set
   out in
   [pinking under acceleration](/en/articles/pinking-under-acceleration-causes/).

The catalogue of sounds tied to acceleration is on the symptom page
[knocking when accelerating](/en/symptoms/knocking-when-accelerating/),
which also covers cases where the knock is heard exactly as the throttle
is applied.

## How the repair is decided, and how urgent each case is

The criterion is which of the three tests answered, and the urgency
differs sharply between them. A squealing belt is a matter of the next few
weeks, and more because of the risk of it breaking than because of the
sound itself; the details are in
[can you drive with a squealing belt](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-a-squealing-belt/).
People drive with CV joint clicking for months, with the caveats covered
in
[can you drive with a clicking CV joint](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-a-clicking-cv-joint/).
Detonation is the only case on the list where the delay is measured not in
mileage but in the number of full-throttle climbs —
[signs of engine detonation](/en/articles/signs-of-engine-detonation/).

Two things mechanics do not always say. If a CV joint is clicking but its
boot is intact, ask whether the joint can be rebuilt with new grease and a
new boot rather than replaced — on many cars it can, for a fraction of the
cost. And if detonation is confirmed, ask what was actually checked
besides the fuel: the knock sensor and the cooling system are the two
things that let an engine detonate silently, and neither shows up on a
test drive.
