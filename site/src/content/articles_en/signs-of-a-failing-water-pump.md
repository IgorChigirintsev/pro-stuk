The water pump circulates the coolant: an impeller on a shaft turned by
the accessory belt or the timing belt. While the engine runs, the pump
continuously pushes coolant through the block, the head and the radiator.
The part is not serviceable and dies gradually — almost always with
warnings. Below: which signs give the wear away, how to check the pump
without dismantling, and what decides the replacement.

## What fails inside a pump

The design is simple: a shaft on a bearing, a seal separating the coolant
from the bearing, and an impeller. Hence three typical failures:

- **the bearing wears** — a whine and shaft play appear;
- **the seal wears** — coolant seeps out through the weep hole;
- **the impeller breaks up** — a rarer case, typical of plastic impellers
  and old aggressive coolant: the blades break off or spin on the shaft
  and circulation falls with no noise at all.

All three are accelerated by the same things: old coolant or coolant
diluted with water, an over-tightened belt, and simple age.

A note on modern cars: on some recent engines and on hybrids the pump is
electric — no belt, no pulley. Its failure signs are different: cooling
system faults on the dash and overheating with no warning noises.
Everything below concerns the classic belt-driven mechanical pump.

## The main signs of failure

- **Coolant traces at the pump pulley.** Drips, wet runs or dry
  orange-white deposits below the weep hole. That is the most honest sign:
  a weep hole does not simply sweat.
- **A whine or hum that grows with revs.** A worn bearing is louder when
  cold, in the first minutes after starting — that scenario is on the
  symptom page [squeal on startup](/en/symptoms/squeal-on-startup/).
- **Accessory belt squeal.** A binding pump slows the belt and it starts
  slipping — more on the symptom itself on
  [belt squeal](/en/symptoms/belt-squeal/).
- **Pulley play.** With the belt off, the pump pulley should not rock on
  its shaft even fractionally.
- **Unstable temperature.** The gauge creeps up in traffic, the heater
  blows cold at idle and revives with revs — the classic sign of weak
  circulation.
- **The coolant level falls** with no visible puddles under the car: the
  fluid evaporates off hot parts before it can drip.

## The stages of wear

| Stage | What you see and hear | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Early | A light whine when cold, dry traces at the weep hole | Watch the level, plan the replacement |
| Middle | Constant hum, a coolant smell, the level slowly dropping | Diagnosis and replacement within weeks |
| Late | Pulley play, a fresh leak, belt squeal | Workshop without delay |
| Critical | Overheating, steam from under the bonnet, a seized shaft | Stop and do not continue |

How fast the stages follow one another is hard to predict: a seal can
sweat for months or open up during one long trip. So the middle stage is
already a reason to book rather than to keep watching.

## How to check it yourself

1. **The expansion tank level** on a cold engine. A slow drop week by week
   is a reason to look for where the coolant is going.
2. **Inspection with a torch.** Find the pump pulley and the weep hole
   next to it and check for runs and stains. On engines where the pump
   hides behind the timing cover little is visible from outside — there a
   leak often gives itself away as drips on the undertray.
3. **Pulley play.** With the engine off and the belt removed or slackened,
   rock the pulley by hand and turn it: play, roughness and rustling are
   signs of a dying bearing.
4. **The heater test.** If the vents blow cool at idle and warm up at
   2,000 rpm, circulation has weakened.

## How the replacement is decided

The pump's own price matters far less than where it sits. If it is driven
by the accessory belt and reachable from outside, the job is modest. If it
is behind the timing belt, the labour is several times greater — which is
why it is combined with the scheduled timing kit, as set out in
[signs of a worn timing belt](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-timing-belt/).

Two things to settle before the work. The coolant: it should go back as
fresh fluid of the correct specification, not strained back in, and the
system must be bled properly — an air lock after the job shows up as a
rising gauge a week later. And the thermostat, which is usually reachable
at the same time and costs little: replacing it now avoids a repeat visit
if it is the reason the temperature was unstable in the first place.

Old coolant is one of the reasons a pump does not reach its design life,
so renewing it with the pump is not an upsell but the point.

## What ignoring it leads to

The main risk is overheating. Weak circulation goes unnoticed in winter
and on the motorway, but in summer traffic the temperature climbs
quickly — and overheating means a blown head gasket and a warped head. The
second scenario applies to engines where the timing belt drives the pump:
a seized shaft snaps the belt or shears its teeth, and on most engines
that means the valves meeting the pistons. Against those figures a planned
pump replacement is one of the cheapest insurances on a car. What driving
with a whining pump risks is set out in
[can you drive with a whining water pump](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-a-whining-water-pump/).
