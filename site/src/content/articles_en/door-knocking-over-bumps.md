A knock arriving from the side — as if someone were tapping lightly from
inside the door — is one of the most deceptive faults. The owner usually
drives off to have the suspension checked, hears "everything is fine" from
the mechanic, and is left with the sound. Yet the source is almost always
in the door itself: the window regulator mechanism, the check strap and
the latch all live in there, and each of them is capable of hitting metal
on every bump.

## The three main sources of a door knock

**The window regulator.** The commonest cause. The mechanism hangs inside
the door on its runs and holds the glass by a frame or clamps. Over time
the cable stretches, the rollers wear, the glass clamps loosen — and the
glass together with the mechanism gains a few millimetres of freedom. Over
bumps that mass hits the inner door panel.

**The door check strap.** The metal arm that holds the door at its
intermediate positions. Its pin and bush wear, the door gains fore-and-aft
play and taps at the leading edge over bumps. That knock comes with a
characteristic companion sign: the door now closes with a different sound,
and no longer holds firmly when open.

**The latch and its striker.** The striker on the body pillar wears or
shifts, and the door no longer sits tightly. The knock here is dull,
closer to the trailing edge of the door, often heard together with a light
rattle from the glass.

## Localising it in one drive

| Check | How to do it | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Lower the window 5–10 cm | Drive the same stretch | Sound changed — window regulator |
| Press the door from inside with your palm | A helper presses while driving | Knock gone — door play |
| Rock the closed door by the handle | Parked, up and down | Movement present — latch or striker |
| Open and move the door through its stops | Slowly, to the clicks | Crunching — check strap |
| Bounce the car by the wing | Parked, with all your weight | Knock present — suspension, not the door |

The last item is the most important. If the sound is reproduced by
bouncing the stationary car, the door has nothing to do with it: most
likely an anti-roll bar link or a damper is knocking and the body is
simply conducting the sound into the cabin. That fork is covered in detail
in
[suspension creaking over bumps](/en/articles/suspension-creaking-over-bumps/)
and

the general catalogue of sounds over bumps is on the symptom page
[knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/).

## What you can do yourself

Taking the door card off most mass-market models is a twenty-minute job:
a few screws under caps and plastic clips around the edge. Inside, three
things are worth checking: the tightness of the bolts holding the glass to
the carrier, the state of the mechanism's rollers and cables, and whether
the factory rubber dampers are present on the inner panel. Often it is
enough to tighten the glass fixing and add a piece of felt where the
mechanism touches metal.

The check strap is cured by greasing the pin and bush; if the play is
noticeable the part is replaced complete, and it is not expensive. The
latch striker is adjusted by moving it: slacken the screws, shift it
slightly, tighten and check how the door sits.

Interior rattles that are not the door at all have their own breakdown in
[a rattling dashboard](/en/articles/rattling-dashboard/) and
[interior creaking over bumps](/en/articles/interior-creaking-over-bumps/).

## How the repair is decided

There is rarely a price list for door mechanisms — the work is charged by
time, so the decision turns on what the checks found rather than on parts.
The order is the cheap and reversible first: tighten the glass fixing,
grease the check strap, adjust the striker. Only if the play stays does
the regulator get replaced, and on many cars it comes as a module with the
motor.

Two things mechanics do not always say. If several doors knock at once,
check the suspension first — that coincidence is unlikely, and the body is
probably just conducting the sound. And if a knock returns a few weeks
after the door card has been off, the usual culprit is a clip or a missing
foam pad rather than the mechanism: ask for the factory dampers to be put
back rather than left in the bin.

If a hum or a whine unrelated to bumps has joined the knock, the source is
worth looking for in the transmission —
[can you drive with a whining gearbox](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-a-whining-gearbox/).

The easiest way to settle this kind of knock is by comparing two
recordings. Start recording in the Stuk app and drive a rough stretch you
know, first with the window closed, then with it slightly down. The app
matches the recordings with your answers to a few questions and shows
which is more likely — the door, the body or the suspension.
