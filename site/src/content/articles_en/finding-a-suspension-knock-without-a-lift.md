A suspension knock rarely calls for an immediate tow, but it always calls
for an answer to the question “what exactly is knocking”. A lift is not
essential for a first approximation: most chassis parts can be checked on
level ground with your hands, a bounce test and a short controlled drive.
Below is a four-step sequence that narrows the list of suspects from “the
whole suspension” to one or two specific parts. You will need a torch,
gloves and — for some tests — a jack and a helper.

## Step 1. Build a portrait of the sound

Before crawling under the car, spend a week simply listening. Answers to a
few questions rule out half the options:

- **Where it knocks** — front or rear, left or right. An open window and a
  slow pass along a wall or parapet help: the sound reflects and becomes
  clearer.
- **What it knocks on** — washboard tarmac and expansion joints, or single
  potholes and speed humps. Frequent sharp knocking on small stuff and
  single dull impacts on big bumps give different lists of causes.
- **What changes the sound** — corners, braking, pulling away, a loaded
  boot, warming up. A knock that disappears in a corner as the body leans
  one way is the classic signature of an anti-roll bar link.

The general picture is on the symptom page
[knocking in the suspension](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-suspension/), and
the specific case of small bumps on
[knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/).

It is also worth ruling out false sources: an unsecured jack and tools in
the boot, a loose spare wheel, objects in the door pockets and on the
parcel shelf. A noticeable share of “suspension knocks” turn out to be
luggage tapping against the body — five minutes of clearing the boot is
cheaper than any diagnosis.

## Step 2. The bounce test

Over each wheel in turn, rock the car vigorously by a wing or bumper and
listen. A creak, knock or click while rocking localises the problem
corner, and the way the body behaves after you let go says something about
the dampers: a healthy strut settles the movement in one motion.

The bounce test catches strut top mounts (a creak from above), ball joints
(a knock from below) and tired dampers well. Silence during the test
disproves nothing: many parts need an impact, not a gentle push.

## Step 3. Checks by hand

Next, the parts you can reach without a lift. Gloves, and for some tests a
helper.

- **Anti-roll bar links.** The bar ties the left and right wheels together
  to limit body roll; the links are its jointed rods and the main supplier
  of knocking on washboard surfaces. On many cars you can reach a link
  behind the wheel with the steering on full lock: rock it by hand — play
  and knocking are felt straight away.
- **Track rod ends.** A helper rocks the steering wheel in short movements
  with the engine running while you hold a palm on the track rod end
  behind the wheel. Thumps and clicks into your palm mean play in the
  joint.
- **Ball joints.** A full test needs a jack — rocking the raised wheel by
  the top and bottom.
- **Anti-roll bar bushes and control arm bushes.** Inspect with a torch:
  cracked rubber, squeezed-out or torn bushes, rusty streaks from the
  joints.
- **Fasteners and the undertray.** Loose engine undertray bolts are a
  banal and very common cause of clatter over potholes. While your gloves
  are on, check the visible suspension fasteners and the arch liner clips.

## Step 4. The controlled drive

The final step is a drive along a familiar route with changing surfaces.
The aim is to test the hypothesis, not to gather new impressions: if you
suspect an anti-roll bar link, the knock should disappear as the body
leans; a damper, grow over a series of undulations; the steering, respond
to small corrections. A helper is useful: the driver watches the road, the
passenger listens and records the sound on a phone by the relevant arch.

A few standard manoeuvres on an empty road complete the picture: gentle
braking (a knock as the nose dips — bushes or track rod ends), pulling
away (a single thud — a ball joint or an engine mount), a slow slalom
(noises as the body leans — the anti-roll bar and its links). All of them
at speeds that are safe for everyone else: the goal is to hear the sound,
not to hit a pothole flat out.

## Summary table

| How the knock shows | First suspects | Quick check |
|---|---|---|
| Frequent and sharp, on washboard and joints | Anti-roll bar links | Rock the link by hand |
| Dry and metallic, on small potholes | Ball joint, track rod ends | Vertical play, rocking the steering |
| Dull and single, in big potholes | Control arm bushes, bump stops | Inspect the joint rubber |
| Knock from above, felt through the body | Strut top mounts | Creak during the bounce test, crunch when steering |
| Metallic clatter after potholes | Loose fasteners, undertray | Inspect and tighten |
| Knock plus body wallowing over undulations | Dampers | The bounce test |

The table is about probabilities, not verdicts: suspension parts work
together, and a worn damper, for instance, accelerates the wear of
everything else. If the knock comes from the rear, the candidates shift:
there it is more often beam or arm bushes, anti-roll bar bushes and
dampers, and on a multi-link rear suspension the list of joints is even
longer than at the front.

## When a lift is unavoidable

Home tests have a limit. Rear beam and arm bushes often need loading with
a pry bar from below; early wheel bearing play is caught on a spinning
raised wheel; hidden cracks in springs are only visible on a clean
inspection. If the list has not narrowed after four steps, or the
suspicion concerns the rear suspension, a lift is the sensible next stop —
and against the price of even one part replaced for nothing, that
inspection is cheap insurance.

A special case is a knock that appeared right after a repair or a tyre
change: then the places recently touched come first, from wheel bolt
torque to how the removed parts were refitted.

If the knock still refuses to be localised, there is one more tool: record
the sound while driving in the Stuk app. It matches the recording with
your answers about when and on which surfaces the knock appears and shows
the likely causes with an urgency level — a convenient starting point both
for the garage check and for the conversation with a mechanic.
