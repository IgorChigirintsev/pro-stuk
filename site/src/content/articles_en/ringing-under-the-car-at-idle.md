A ringing under the car at idle is a sound with character: thin, metallic,
often trembling in time with the engine. The suspension is almost never to
blame here — on a stationary car it has nothing to knock with. What rings
is what belongs to the exhaust system: heat shields, a disintegrating
catalytic converter, loose fasteners, and less often parts of the engine's
accessories. Here are the causes from harmless to expensive, and how to
narrow the field in ten minutes.

## Common sources of ringing at idle

- **A heat shield.** The thin steel sheet protecting the floor and the
  lines from the heat of the pipe. A rotted-through mounting point turns
  it into a plate trembling with the engine's vibration — the commonest
  and cheapest case.
- **The catalytic converter.** Inside it is a ceramic block with a
  honeycomb structure. When the honeycomb cracks, the fragments ring and
  rustle inside the casing, especially at idle and when the box is tapped
  lightly —
  [broken catalytic converter rattle](/en/articles/broken-catalytic-converter-rattle/).
- **The pipe touching the body.** Sagged rubber hangers or tired engine
  mounts shift the system, and at idle it rings against the floor or the
  subframe in time with the vibration.
- **The internals of the silencer.** Detached baffles and tubes inside the
  box give a ringing and rattle that change with the revs.
- **The accessories.** A loose belt cover, bracket or air conditioning
  line rings from above, but the sound reflects off the floor and seems to
  come from below.

If the ringing comes with general shaking of the car, look first at the
symptom page
[vibration at idle](/en/symptoms/vibration-at-idle/): increased engine
vibration makes even healthy fasteners ring.

## Narrowing the field: a table of checks

| Check | Result | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Warm the engine | The ringing went with warm-up | Shield or a gap in the system: the metal expanded |
| Select D with the brake held | The ringing changed | The engine tilted — exhaust contact or mounts |
| A light tap by hand on the boxes (cold car) | Rustling and ringing inside | Catalytic converter or silencer internals |
| Raise the revs to 1500–2000 | The ringing disappeared | A narrow-band resonance — typical of shields |
| Inspect the pipe hangers | The pipe hangs askew, rubbers torn | The system touching the body |

Ten minutes with that table usually gives a working theory before the
workshop. Two points on technique: only tap the system on a cold car — the
pipe reaches hundreds of degrees after a drive; and do the gear selection
test on level ground with the parking brake on. The general breakdown of
clattering and ringing from below is on the symptom page
[rattling under the car](/en/symptoms/rattling-under-car/).

## Ringing and revs: what the link tells you

Ringing strictly at idle that disappears on a throttle blip is almost
always resonance: the engine's vibration frequency at 700–900 rpm has
coincided with the natural frequency of a loose part. That is the typical
signature of a shield. It is also why ringing often switches on after some
repair or a mount change: the idle speed shifted slightly, and a part that
was silent for years fell into resonance.

Ringing that does not disappear with revs but grows and changes pitch is a
reason to think about the catalytic converter or the silencer internals:
the fragments tumble more actively. If the exhaust has also become
noticeably louder than usual, corrosion right through the system is
likely — that picture is covered in
[the exhaust has become louder](/en/articles/exhaust-has-become-louder-causes/).

A third variant is ringing that only appears under load: a hint at idle, a
distinct rattle under acceleration. There the pipe touching because the
engine is rocking on worn mounts is more often to blame — the mechanics
are described in detail in
[a rattle under the car when accelerating](/en/articles/rattle-under-car-when-accelerating/)
and
[engine mount failure symptoms](/en/articles/engine-mount-failure-symptoms/).

## What ignoring it leads to

The answer depends on the source, and the spread is wide. A loose shield is
pure acoustics: it can ring for years, and at worst it falls off and has
to be refitted. The pipe touching the body is already less pleasant: the
metal wears through on both sides, and within six months the penny hangers
turn into welding on the estimate.

The most expensive scenario is the catalytic converter. Once the honeycomb
starts ringing it does not recover: the ceramic crumbles further, the
fragments restrict the gas flow, the engine loses pull and starts using
more fuel. On some engines the ceramic dust is drawn back into the
cylinders under certain conditions and works there as an abrasive — that
is a conversation about engine repair on a completely different scale from
replacing the converter in time,
[signs of a blocked catalytic converter](/en/articles/signs-of-a-blocked-catalytic-converter/).
So ringing with rustling from the middle of the system is a reason for a
diagnosis this week rather than someday.

## How the repair is decided

The criterion is which of the three groups the checks put you in. A shield
or a hanger is a fastening job with no parts to speak of, and it can be
done at any convenient visit. The pipe touching the body is fixed by
restoring the hangers or the mounts — and the mount is worth checking
before the pipe, because it is the cause rather than the symptom. Only the
converter is a genuine parts decision, and it is quoted after a diagnosis
rather than over the phone: the choice between an original unit and a
universal one changes the figure severalfold.

Two things mechanics do not always say. Before condemning a converter, ask
for back pressure to be measured or the fault codes read — a rattling
casing and a blocked converter are not the same finding, and only one of
them costs real money. And if the shield is simply removed rather than
refastened, the heat it was shielding now reaches the floor and the fuel
lines; a clamp costs less than that trade.
