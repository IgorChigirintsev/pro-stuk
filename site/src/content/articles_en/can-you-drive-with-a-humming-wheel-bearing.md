A hum from one side of the car that grows with speed is the typical voice
of a worn wheel bearing — the unit the wheel turns on. The good news: it
almost never fails suddenly and usually leaves the driver thousands of
kilometres of margin. The bad news: that margin is unpredictable and
shrinks after every pothole. Below is an honest breakdown of when you can
drive normally with a hum, when to book the workshop this week, and when
it is better not to drive at all.

## The short answer: it depends on the stage

Reduced to three situations:

- **a faint hum at 60–100 km/h, no vibration, no play** — you can drive
  with no restrictions, but book the inspection rather than putting it off
  until winter;
- **a constant loud hum from 40–50 km/h that changes in gentle curves** —
  drive briefly and carefully, replace within days;
- **rumbling, vibration in the wheel and floor, wheel play, heat from the
  hub after a drive** — stop making journeys; at most drive slowly to the
  nearest workshop.

Before counting the kilometres left, make sure it really is the bearing:
worn tyres sound very similar and the two are constantly confused. Quick
tests to separate them are in
[humming: wheel bearing or tyres](/en/articles/hum-wheel-bearing-or-tyres/).
And if the sound is not a hum at all but clicking in a turn, that is a CV
joint, with a logic of its own.

## What is left: guidance by stage

| Stage | What you hear and feel | Rough margin | Driving regime |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early | Light hum on the motorway, not always noticeable | Usually thousands of km | No restrictions, book a check |
| Middle | Constant hum, louder or quieter in curves | Hundreds to thousands of km | Town, moderate speed, replace this week |
| Late | Rumbling, vibration, hum at any speed | Tens to hundreds of km | Only as far as the workshop |
| Critical | Play, grinding, a hot hub | No margin left | Tow truck, or minimal speed |

An important caveat: these are guides from experience, not a guarantee. A
bearing fails non-linearly — spalling of the raceways accelerates as it
goes, and one hard pothole strike can move the part from the early stage
straight to the late one. Overloading, larger wheels with low-profile
tyres and driving through deep puddles also speed it up: a hot hub cools
abruptly and water is drawn in past the seals.

You can identify the side without a lift: on an empty road at 60–80 km/h
ease smoothly in both directions. In a curve the weight transfers to the
outer wheels, so if the hum grows when you turn left, the right side is
loaded and humming, and vice versa. That marker is covered in more detail
on the symptom page
[humming when turning](/en/symptoms/humming-when-turning/).

## What driving with a hum actually risks

The real danger appears not at the first hum but as the wear builds:

- **play grows.** The wheel starts to wander, the alignment drifts, the
  tyre wears in patches, the car holds a straight line less well;
- **neighbouring parts suffer.** On many modern cars the ABS sensor is
  built into the hub assembly: a ruined bearing produces ABS and stability
  control faults. A skewed hub presses the brake disc unevenly, and judder
  appears under braking;
- **the hub gets hot.** A broken cage and degraded grease turn the unit
  into a brake: after a drive the disc and hub are noticeably hotter than
  on the other side;
- **the seat is damaged.** This is the main financial risk: if the bearing
  has spun in the knuckle or on the hub, what needs replacing is no longer
  the bearing but the hub or the steering knuckle, and the bill multiplies;
- **the wheel seizes.** The extreme and rare scenario, but it is what makes
  the late stage unacceptable for driving: the wheel abruptly stops
  turning and the car leaves its lane.

## If the repair is not today: reducing the risk

When the part is on order but you still have to drive, switch to a gentle
regime:

1. **Lower speed, especially on broken surfaces.** A pothole strike at
   speed is the main accelerator of failure.
2. **No overloading.** A full boot, five passengers and a trailer all load
   the raceways directly.
3. **No long motorway runs.** An hour at 110–120 km/h heats the unit more
   than a day of town driving.
4. **Listen to the trend every day.** A louder hum, a new rumble,
   vibration or heat from the wheel is a signal to stop using the car. How
   the sound develops is described on the symptom page
   [wheel bearing hum](/en/symptoms/wheel-bearing-hum/) and stage by stage
   in
   [what a failing wheel bearing sounds like](/en/articles/what-a-failing-wheel-bearing-sounds-like/).
5. **Check for play every few days.** Jack the wheel up, take it at the top
   and bottom and rock it vertically. A palpable knock means the limit has
   been reached — the procedure is in
   [checking hub play with the wheel raised](/en/articles/checking-wheel-bearing-play-with-the-wheel-raised/).

## How the repair is decided

Two things govern the job. First, whether your car takes a separate
bearing pressed into the existing hub or a complete hub assembly replaced
as a unit — that decides the tooling, the time and whether the ABS sensor
comes with it. Second, the hub nut: it has a specified torque, and both
over- and under-tightening kill a new bearing within a few thousand
kilometres, so a torque wrench rather than an impact gun is what you want
to see.

There is no need to replace bearings in pairs — the worn one is enough.
But if the car has covered 150,000 km and one side has started humming,
the other is usually not far behind: ask the mechanic to check it on the
same lift, which takes a minute.

The arithmetic of delay is simple: while only the bearing is worn, the
repair is one part and a couple of hours. As soon as the seat is damaged
or the built-in ABS sensor has died, the hub, the knuckle or the whole
assembly join the bill. At the late stage the difference between
“replace this week” and “replace in two months” easily reaches double.

[how to check a wheel bearing yourself](/en/articles/how-to-check-a-wheel-bearing-yourself/).
