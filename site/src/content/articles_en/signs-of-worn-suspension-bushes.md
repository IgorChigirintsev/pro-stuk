A suspension bush is a rubber-to-metal joint: two steel sleeves with
rubber vulcanised between them. Suspension arms attach to the body
through these joints, and it is the rubber that damps vibration and small
impacts from the road. Rubber ages, cracks and tears — so bushes are
consumables, and their wear is almost always audible and visible in
advance. Below are the signs by stage, simple checks, and what decides the
repair.

## The main signs of wear

Worn bushes rarely show up as one thing alone — it is usually a
combination:

- **A dull knock over small bumps.** Single, rubbery, with no metallic
  ring. Most often heard over expansion joints, tram rails and speed
  humps. The general picture of this symptom is on
  [knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/), and the
  front-end version in
  [a dull knock from the front over potholes](/en/articles/dull-knock-from-the-front-over-potholes/).
- **Creaking over bumps and when the car is rocked.** Cracked rubber rubs
  against its sleeve — a sound like an old door. Especially noticeable in
  the cold, when the rubber stiffens.
- **The car pulling to one side.** An arm with a ruined bush shifts under
  load, the wheel angles drift, and the car pulls — especially under
  braking.
- **Uneven tyre wear.** The classic is an eaten inner or outer edge with
  correct tyre pressures.
- **The alignment does not hold.** The rack shows correct figures and a
  couple of thousand kilometres later the angles have drifted again — a
  sure hint at tired bushes.
- **Delays and shoves when pulling away and braking.** Worn rear arm or
  rear beam bushes give the feeling that the back of the car steers
  itself.

## The stages of wear

| Stage | What happens to the rubber | What you hear and feel | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early | Surface cracks | Nothing, or a light creak in frost | Watch, inspect at the next service |
| Middle | Deep cracks, the rubber has tired | Creaking over bumps, the car feels less composed | Diagnosis within a month |
| Late | Tears, rubber separating from the sleeve | Dull knock over small bumps, pulling, chewing tyres | Replace within weeks |
| Critical | The rubber is destroyed, metal on metal | Loud knock, the car wanders in its lane | Replace without delay |

How fast the stages follow one another depends on the roads and the
climate: sharp temperature swings and road salt age rubber faster. The
average life is roughly 60,000–120,000 km, and noticeably shorter on
broken roads.

## How to check them yourself

A full check is done over a pit or on a lift, but some things are visible
without either:

1. **Inspection with a torch.** With the wheel at full lock, look at the
   front arm joints. Cracks more than a couple of millimetres deep, tears
   and rubber squeezed outwards mean replacement. A light surface network
   is acceptable.
2. **A pry bar.** Over a pit the arm is levered next to the joint: it
   should resist springily and return. Free movement, a knock or a
   displaced sleeve means wear.
3. **Wet tarmac.** The tracks left by the wheels in a straight line should
   line up. If the rear wheel runs offset from the front one, the rear
   beam or arm bushes may be worn.

It is important not to confuse bushes with a ball joint — they knock in
similar situations, but the consequences of wear differ. The ball joint's
own signs are in
[signs of a worn ball joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-ball-joint/) and
the self-check in
[how to check a ball joint yourself](/en/articles/how-to-check-a-ball-joint-yourself/).

## How the repair is decided

The first fork is bush or whole arm. On many modern cars the bushes are
not supplied separately and the complete arm is replaced; on others the
bush is pressed out. Ask which applies to your car before comparing
prices, because the two answers are not comparable.

The second is the alignment, which is compulsory after the job rather than
optional.

The third is the detail not every workshop mentions: the bush bolts must
be tightened with the car standing on its wheels, at normal ride height.
Tightened with the suspension hanging, the new rubber works permanently
twisted and dies within 10,000–20,000 km. If a knock appears shortly after
suspension work, that is the first thing to check.

## Why bushes die early

The rubber's design life is measured in years, but several factors cut it
several times over:

- **Road salt and temperature swings.** Winter chemicals attack rubber and
  freeze-thaw cycles accelerate cracking.
- **Oil and other fluids.** A weeping engine or steering rack seal drips
  onto the front arms — rubber soaked in oil swells and tears within a
  season or two. So the leak should be fixed along with the bushes.
- **Pothole strikes.** A sharp impact tears the rubber from the inside
  while the joint still looks decent from outside.
- **Repair mistakes.** Tightening the bolts with the suspension hanging is
  the most common reason new bushes knock after 10,000–20,000 km.

## What ignoring it leads to

A bush is not the part that drops a wheel on the move, and that is its
treachery: you can drive with the knock for a long time, and many do. The
price accrues quietly. A wandering arm accelerates wear in ball joints,
track rod ends and wheel bearings, eats expensive tyres and makes the car
nervous under braking, especially in ruts and on rough tarmac. In advanced
cases the ruined sleeve damages its seat in the arm or beam, and instead
of a cheap bush the whole assembly has to be replaced. The general context
is on the symptom page
[knocking in the suspension](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-suspension/).
