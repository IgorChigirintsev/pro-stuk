The timing chain drives the valvetrain: it links the crankshaft to the
camshafts and makes the valves open exactly on time. Unlike a belt, a
chain is metal and runs in oil, so the sounds of its wear are its own —
not a squeal but a characteristic rustle. Recognising it by ear is doubly
useful: a chain drive wears slowly, and the sound gives you time for a
calm repair rather than an emergency one.

## What a chain rustle sounds like

The main sound is a metallic rustle, as though small coins were being
poured inside the engine or an old sewing machine were running. It comes
from the front of the engine (where the chain is), follows the revs —
quickening on a throttle blip — and is heard best at idle with the bonnet
open.

As the wear progresses the rustle gains detail: individual ticks appear,
the sound becomes uneven and ragged, and on lift-off there is a short
metallic slap as the slack run of chain hits the guide. At an advanced
stage the rustle becomes a distinct chatter and clatter when cold.

Listen in a quiet place, crouching by the front wheel on the timing drive
side or leaning under an open bonnet. A useful reference is a temperature
comparison: record the cold start on your phone in the morning and the
warm idle in the evening. On a healthy chain drive the recordings are
almost identical; on a worn one the difference is immediately audible.

Do not confuse the rustle with valvetrain ticking — a rhythmic, drier
sound whose breakdown is on the symptom page
[engine ticking](/en/symptoms/ticking-engine/).

## When a rustle is normal

A brief rustle in the first one or two seconds after a cold start is
acceptable on many engines. The reason is the hydraulic tensioner: the
device that keeps the chain taut using engine oil pressure. Overnight some
oil drains out of it, and until pressure builds the chain runs with a
little slack.

Three questions define the boundary. The sound goes within a couple of
seconds — acceptable. It lasts five seconds or more and repeats at every
start — tensioner or chain wear, covered in
[signs of a failing timing chain tensioner](/en/articles/timing-chain-tensioner-failure-signs/).
It is heard constantly, including on a warm engine — a workshop, without
delay. The cold-start picture as a whole is on the symptom page
[engine knock when cold](/en/symptoms/engine-knock-when-cold/).

## Why a chain starts rustling

A chain does not stretch like an elastic band — it lengthens through wear
in dozens of joints: each pin and bush wears and the total length grows by
millimetres. That is enough for the tensioner to reach the end of its
travel and for the chain to start oscillating and rubbing the guides.

Several factors speed it up:

- **old or infrequently changed oil** — the joints are lubricated by the
  same oil as the engine, and abrasive in it grinds the chain fastest;
- **a worn hydraulic tensioner** — it does not hold pressure and the chain
  sags;
- **worn guides and shoe** — the plastic wears and the free movement
  grows;
- **frequent cold starts and short trips** — the drive spends longer
  running before oil pressure builds.

Typical chain life is 150,000–250,000 km, but the spread is enormous: on
engines sensitive to oil, stretched chains turn up at 90,000, while with
careful servicing a drive reaches 300,000. The practical rule is simple:
the shorter the oil change intervals (7,000–10,000 km in hot and dusty
conditions), the longer the chain stays quiet.

On engines with a belt-driven camshaft the wear picture is entirely
different — see
[signs of a worn timing belt](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-timing-belt/).

## The stages: from rustle to a jumped tooth

| Stage | What you hear | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Early | A rustle for 1–2 seconds at a cold start | Watch, change the oil on time |
| Middle | A rustle for 5–10 seconds, episodes at idle | Diagnosis within weeks |
| Marked | Constant rustle, ticking, slaps on lift-off | Measure the stretch, prepare to replace |
| Critical | Clatter when cold, camshaft codes, lost power | Workshop at once, preferably not under your own power |

At the later stages other signs join in: the check engine light with
correlation codes (P0016, P0017 — the ECU sees the crankshaft and camshaft
out of step), harder starting and lost power. The full breakdown is in
[signs of a stretched timing chain](/en/articles/signs-of-a-stretched-timing-chain/).

## How the repair is decided

The main risk is the chain jumping a sprocket tooth. The valve timing
shifts, and on most modern engines the valves meet the pistons: the repair
turns from “change the chain” into rebuilding half the engine. The second
scenario is worn guides breaking up, with the fragments going under the
chain.

Before replacement the wear can be confirmed objectively. Many workshops
assess the stretch without dismantling — with a scan tool from the
camshaft correlation angles, or by the tensioner plunger position through
an inspection hole. That measurement costs no more than an ordinary
diagnosis and saves you from the main mistake: replacing a chain “just in
case” when what was rustling was, say, an accessory idler.

It is worth replacing the complete kit — chain, tensioner, guides and
sprockets — because a new chain on worn guides rustles again. While the
front of the engine is open, the water pump is often right there, and its
signs of wear are in
[signs of a failing water pump](/en/articles/signs-of-a-failing-water-pump/);
asking whether it is included avoids doing the same disassembly twice.
