Anti-roll bar links are the first thing a mechanic checks when an owner
complains of a knock in the suspension. They are small rods with joints
connecting the anti-roll bar — the springy bar that reduces body roll — to
the wheel's suspension. The part is cheap and quick to replace, and on
broken roads it wears more often than almost anything else in the running
gear. Below: the signs of wear, how to check the links without a lift, and
what decides the replacement.

## How a link is built and why it knocks

An anti-roll bar link is a rod 10–30 cm long with ball joints or bushes at
each end. As the body rolls, the anti-roll bar twists and pulls the
unloaded side of the suspension up through the links. The joints work at
small angles constantly — thousands of cycles per kilometre of rough road.

What wears is usually not the rod but the joint: the plastic liner is worn
away, play appears, and the pin starts to flop in its housing. Every small
bump turns that play into an impact — hence the characteristic frequent
knock. A torn boot speeds it up: dust and water grind the joint away
within a few thousand kilometres.

## The main signs of wear

- **A frequent dull knock over small bumps.** Expansion joints, washboard
  surfaces, tram rails, speed humps at low speed. On large potholes the
  knock can even be less noticeable than on small stuff.
- **The knock goes quiet in a turn.** In a corner the anti-roll bar is
  loaded and the play in the joint is taken up, so there is nothing left
  to knock. That is the links' main distinguishing feature.
- **The knock is more often at the front**, on the side of the worn link,
  and does not depend on braking or acceleration.
- **Increased roll and a vague feel in corners.** Noticeable at a late
  stage, when the play is large or the link has broken: the car leans more
  and needs correcting.
- **Creaking** over bumps — heard when the joint has dried out but is not
  yet broken.

## The stages of wear

| Stage | What you hear and feel | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Early | Occasional single knocks on small stuff, boot intact | Check at the next service |
| Middle | Constant frequent knock, quiet in corners | Diagnosis and replacement within a couple of weeks |
| Late | Knock over any bump, roll noticeably increased | Replace without delay |
| Broken | The knock has gone, but the car leans in corners | Workshop: the anti-roll bar is not working |

Note the last row: a broken link can stop knocking altogether. Silence
after a long period of knocking is not recovery but a reason to look under
the car. What happens next is set out in
[what happens if you do not replace anti-roll bar links](/en/articles/not-replacing-anti-roll-bar-links/).

## How to check them yourself

No lift is needed for the basic check:

1. **Rocking by hand.** With the car parked, find the link behind the
   front wheel (turn the steering for easier access) and rock it firmly.
   Perceptible play or clicks in the joint mean wear. A healthy link moves
   stiffly and silently.
2. **Inspecting the boots.** A torn or cracked joint boot is a reason to
   prepare for replacement even before a knock appears.
3. **The helper test.** A helper rocks the car by the wing while you
   listen at the wheel. The links' knock sounds like frequent metallic
   clicks around the anti-roll bar.
4. **Listening on the move.** Drive slowly over a washboard surface or
   gravel: links answer with a fine, frequent chatter rather than single
   thumps.

At a workshop the diagnosis is confirmed on a lift: the mechanic rocks the
link with a pry bar or by hand with the wheel raised, and the play is
immediately visible and audible. The check takes a few minutes and is
usually free when you book the replacement. Do not ask for a replacement
“because it knocks” without an inspection: several other parts make a
similar sound, and it is better for the mechanic to show you the play.

Anti-roll bar bushes, track rod ends and worn dampers knock similarly —
their signs are in

and on the symptom page
[knocking in the suspension](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-suspension/).

## How the replacement is decided

This is one of the cheapest suspension jobs, and no alignment is needed
afterwards — the part does not affect the wheel angles. The work takes
20–40 minutes a side on most models; it takes longer when the fasteners
have seized and have to be worked with penetrating oil or cut off, which
is what the upper end of any quote reflects.

Two practical points. First, a link's ball stud usually has to be held
with a hex key or a spanner on a flat while the nut is undone — a joint
spun round with an impact gun is destroyed as it is removed, which does
not matter for the old one but does if the mechanic reuses hardware.
Second, if the fastener was cut off, the new nut and bolt should be
proper replacements rather than whatever fits.

## Is it worth putting off

Anti-roll bar links are not brakes: their wear does not usually create a
sudden emergency. But ruined joints finish themselves off with every
kilometre, the knock masks other suspension faults, and with a broken link
the car holds its line noticeably worse in a sharp manoeuvre. Given how
cheap the part is, saving on the replacement is rarely justified.
