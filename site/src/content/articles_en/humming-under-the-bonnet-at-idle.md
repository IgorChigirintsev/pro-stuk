A hum at idle is awkward because it has no link to speed or manoeuvres:
the car is standing still and the sound is there. But idle offers another
lever — it is easy to interrogate: switch consumers on and off, listen with
the bonnet up, change the revs. So finding the source on a stationary car
is simpler than it seems, as long as you work in order.

## First separate hum from vibration

Step one is to work out what you are actually hearing. Low-frequency
shaking that reaches the steering wheel, the seat and the body is usually
not the sound of a component but engine vibration passed into the body
through worn mounts. It has its own set of causes and is covered
separately on the symptom page
[vibration at idle](/en/symptoms/vibration-at-idle/).

A hum in the narrow sense is a sound: even, mid or low pitched, coming
from the engine bay. Its source is almost always something that rotates.
Put a hand on a wing: if there is no vibration but there is a sound, you
are dealing with sound.

It is worth checking the revs straight away as well. A normal idle on most
cars is 650–850 rpm; if the tachometer sits noticeably higher and the
engine hums simply because it is running fast, the cause is not a bearing
but the engine management — from a cold engine to an air leak. A hum is
worth investigating at normal idle speed.

## The cooling fan: the most common answer

With the car standing, no air passes through the radiator, so the cooling
fan runs often and for a long time — especially in hot weather and with
the air conditioning on. A hum that comes in waves, appearing and
disappearing by itself every few minutes, is almost certainly it.

Two things make the sound abnormal. A worn electric fan bearing hums
louder than usual and howls. Some cars with longitudinal engines use a
viscous coupling that engages the fan as the engine heats up; when it
fails, the fan runs at full speed constantly and the engine roars even
when cold. The check is easy: wait for the fan to switch off — if the hum
goes with it, you have found the source.

A separate question is why the fan runs so much at all. A radiator blocked
with fluff and dust, a dead thermostat or an air lock in the cooling
system keep it turning almost without pause. In that case the hum is not a
fan fault but a symptom of overheating, and the place to start is the
radiator and the coolant level.

## The belt and the accessories

If the hum is constant and does not follow the fan cycles, move on to what
the accessory belt drives. The convenient part is that many of them can be
interrogated with switches:

- **the air conditioning compressor** — switch the A/C on and off: if the
  hum follows the button, you have your candidate;
- **the alternator** — switch on main beam and the screen heaters: worn
  bearings hum more noticeably under the extra load;
- **the power steering pump** — turn the wheel while parked: if the sound
  grows, check the fluid level and the pump;
- **idlers, the tensioner and the water pump** — these ignore switches;
  they are listened to with a stethoscope or identified with a brief run
  without the belt.

If the switches show nothing, the belt itself is the remaining tool: a
workshop removes it briefly and runs the engine for half a minute. Silence
means the noise was in the accessories, and the units are then listened to
one by one. Over time bearing hum turns into squeal and howl.

## The timing drive and the engine itself

Less often the hum at idle comes from under the valve cover — from the
timing drive. A stretched timing chain at low revs produces a sound on the
border between a hum and a metallic rustle. On engines with a timing belt,
its idlers can hum too — quietly, because a plastic cover muffles them.

## Summary table

| Check | How the hum reacts | Likely source |
|---|---|---|
| Wait 2–3 minutes | Comes and goes in waves | Cooling fan |
| A/C button | Follows the switch | Air conditioning compressor |
| Main beam + heaters | Grows | Alternator bearings |
| Turning the wheel while parked | Changes | Power steering pump |
| Reacts to nothing | Constant | Idlers, water pump, timing chain |

For urgency, use this order: fan and compressor hum can wait for a
scheduled appointment; alternator and idler hum is a question of weeks; and
water pump hum with coolant traces cannot be postponed, because that unit
belongs to the cooling system and its failure on a long drive means an
overheated engine.

One practical note about the repair: when an idler or tensioner is
replaced, the belt goes with it. It is a small part next to the labour
already being paid for, and a new idler running on an old, stretched belt
is a short-lived fix.
