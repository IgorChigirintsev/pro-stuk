A hum that appears or grows during left-hand curves and gentle lane
changes is one of the most talkative sounds in a car: it does not merely
report a problem, it tells you which side to look at. Most often this is a
wheel bearing announcing itself. Here is why it hums specifically in a
corner, how to confirm the diagnosis in a single drive, and how urgent it
is.

## Why left means right

In a corner the body leans outwards and the car's weight transfers to the
outer wheels. Turning or changing lanes to the left loads the right-hand
side: a noticeably larger share of the mass presses on the right wheel
bearing, the worn raceways are squeezed harder — and the hum grows. The
inner, left wheel is unloaded at that moment, so even a worn left bearing
goes quiet in a left-hand curve.

Hence a rule worth remembering and telling the mechanic: the hum grows
when you go left, suspect the right bearing; grows when you go right,
suspect the left. The rule works from 40–60 km/h in gentle curves; in
tight parking manoeuvres other parts get involved and the picture blurs.

## Confirming it in one drive

1. **The lane-change test.** On an empty straight road at 60–80 km/h, ease
   left, then right. The hum fading in the right-hand curve and growing in
   the left is the picture of a right-hand bearing.
2. **The coasting test.** Accelerate to the speed where the hum is audible
   and coast in neutral. If the hum stays, the source rotates with the
   wheels and the engine and gearbox are out of it.
3. **The surface test.** Drive a stretch with different tarmac. A bearing
   hums the same everywhere; tyre noise changes on a smooth surface.

If all three agree, a wheel bearing is highly likely. The sound portrait
is on the symptom page
[wheel bearing hum](/en/symptoms/wheel-bearing-hum/).

## What else hums in corners

| Source | How it differs from a bearing |
|---|---|
| Power steering pump | Hums when steering while parked, ignores speed |
| Tyre noise | Changes with the surface, the same in both curves |
| Outer CV joint | Not a hum but clicking in a tight turn under power |
| Differential, transmission | The hum follows drive: different under power and coasting |
| Rear bearing | Same weight-transfer logic, but the sound comes from behind |

The main divider is what the sound follows. A hum tied to speed and to the
direction of the manoeuvre is the chassis. A hum tied to turning the wheel
regardless of movement is the power steering. Crunching and clicking are
joints. If you are unsure which group your case belongs to, the full
catalogue is on the symptom page
[humming when turning](/en/symptoms/humming-when-turning/).

## How the repair is decided

Bearings are not replaced in pairs — only the worn one. But it is worth
asking for both sides to be checked during the diagnosis: if the other one
is close behind, at least you will know what to expect.

Two details decide the job itself. Whether your car uses a bearing pressed
into the existing hub or a complete hub assembly changes both the price
and the time — and on many modern cars the ABS sensor lives inside that
assembly, which is why a failed bearing sometimes brings warning lights
with it. And the hub nut torque matters more here than on most work: an
over- or under-tightened nut destroys a new bearing within thousands of
kilometres.

## How urgent it is

A bearing almost never fails suddenly: from the first hum to critical play
is usually thousands of kilometres. The dangerous signals, after which
nothing should be postponed, are a rumble instead of an even hum,
vibration in the body and steering, play in the wheel, and a hub that is
hot after a drive. At that stage the cage is breaking up, and the count is
no longer in thousands of kilometres. Closing the question within the
first weeks is cheaper and calmer.
