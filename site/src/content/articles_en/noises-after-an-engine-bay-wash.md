Washing the engine bay looks like a harmless procedure, yet it is often
right afterwards that a car starts squealing at start-up, misfiring at
idle or answering with a strange rattle. There is nothing mysterious about
it: water gets into places the designers never intended it to reach, and
changes the behaviour of several parts at once. Most of those noises clear
by themselves, but some mean real damage.

## Why water changes anything at all

An engine bay is not sealed. Connectors, spark plug wells, the air filter
housing, the accessory pulleys — all of it is designed for splashes and
rain, but not for a directed jet. A pressure washer forces moisture past
seals, and the cleaning product additionally washes factory grease off
parts and reduces friction where friction is needed.

Hence three typical scenarios. First, water in the pulley grooves: the
drive belt loses grip and starts slipping with a squeal. Second, moisture
in the plug wells and on the ignition coils: the spark tracks away, the
engine misfires and shakes. Third, water in sensor connectors: the
readings drift, the electronics act on wrong data, and the idle speed
wanders.

## Which noises will clear by themselves

| Noise | Cause | When it clears |
|---|---|---|
| Squeal at start-up, fading with warm-up | Water on the belt and pulleys | 1–2 drives |
| Misfire, shaking at idle | Moisture on coils, in plug wells | 1–3 days |
| Hissing, steam from the manifold | Water evaporating off hot metal | Immediately |
| Wandering idle for the first minutes | Moisture in sensor connectors | 1–2 days |
| Squeal lasting a week or more | Worn belt, tensioner | Will not clear |
| Grinding, whine from the alternator area | Bearing grease washed out | Will not clear |

The separator is simple: a noise caused by water weakens with every drive.
If the second day is quieter than the first and the third is barely
noticeable, that is drying out and no intervention is needed. If the noise
holds steady or grows, the water has merely revealed a problem that was
there already — before the wash the part was running on the last of its
grease or on dry friction.

## What to do in the first few days

First, do not rush to a workshop the same evening. Give the car two or
three drive-and-cool cycles, preferably with a long run at speed: warm air
under the bonnet dries moisture faster than standing does. Second, open
the bonnet and inspect the connectors and plug wells; water in the wells
can be blown out or soaked up with a dry cloth. Third, listen to the
engine cold each morning: moisture-related sounds show themselves most
clearly at the first start.

## How the repair is decided

If the water only accelerated existing wear, the bill is small: a belt, an
idler, a tensioner. The criterion is the same trend test — a part that was
already at the end of its life keeps making noise after everything has
dried, and that is what it is worth paying to replace. A part that goes
quiet needs nothing at all.

Two things worth knowing. If the engine keeps misfiring after drying,
ask for the fault codes to be read rather than the coils replaced by
guesswork: a misfire code names the cylinder, and corroded connector
contacts are cleaned rather than bought. And an alternator whose bearing
grease was washed out will not recover — the whine is permanent, and
catching it early means a bearing rather than a whole unit.

A separate recommendation for the future: if you wash the engine before a
sale or after a repair, ask for the alternator, the fuse box and the air
intake to be covered with film, and for the wash to be done by hand at low
pressure. That removes most of the scenarios described here.

The hardest part is telling whether the noise is really weakening. Hearing
remembers loudness poorly but catches differences well in direct
comparison. Record the morning start in the Pro-Stuk app on the first day and
repeat it on the third: the app compares the recordings with your answers
and shows whether things are heading back to normal or it is time to book
a diagnosis.
