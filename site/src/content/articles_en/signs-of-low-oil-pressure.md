Oil pressure is literally what an engine's life hangs on: while the pump
pushes oil through the clearances, the crankshaft and camshafts turn on a
thin film and barely wear. An engine tolerates a pressure drop silently
for a while, and by the time it says so out loud the count is in hours of
running. Below: the signs that catch the problem before the light, the
causes of a pressure drop, and how it is checked.

## How the lubrication system works

The oil pump is driven from the crankshaft and draws oil from the sump
through a pick-up with a coarse screen. The oil then passes the
full-flow filter and travels through galleries to the crankshaft
bearings, the camshafts, the hydraulic lifters and other consumers.

The pressure in that system is not constant: it rises with revs (the pump
is mechanical) and falls with temperature (hot oil is thinner). For most
cars the norm is roughly 1–2 bar at hot idle and 3–5 under load, but every
model has its own figures. The warning light is set to a low threshold —
usually a fraction of a bar — so it comes on not when the pressure
declines but when it has already collapsed.

That is the main trap: most modern cars have no pressure gauge, only a
light. Between “pressure normal” and “pressure critical” the dashboard
says nothing, and the whole range of gradual decline stays invisible. The
only available indicators of that middle ground are the engine's sounds.

## The signs, from early to late

The lubrication system degrades gradually, and the sounds appear before
the light.

| Sign | What it means | Urgency |
|---|---|---|
| Lifters ticking longer than usual | Pressure marginal in some regimes | Check the oil, watch |
| The engine is noisier when hot | The film is thinning in the friction pairs | Diagnosis within days |
| The light flickers at hot idle, in curves | Pressure dropping below the threshold | Measure the pressure at once |
| The light stays on | Emergency pressure | Switch off, tow truck |
| A dull knock from deep in the block | The bearings are running without a wedge | Repair; do not drive |

**Hydraulic lifters** are the first and most sensitive indicator: those
small plungers take up the valvetrain clearance using oil pressure and
start ticking when there is not enough. A prolonged morning tick is
covered in
[hydraulic lifters ticking when cold](/en/articles/hydraulic-lifters-ticking-when-cold/),
the checks in
[how to check hydraulic lifters](/en/articles/how-to-check-hydraulic-lifters/),
and the sound itself on the symptom page
[engine ticking](/en/symptoms/ticking-engine/).

**Bearing knock** is a late sign: a dull sound from deep in the block
means the crankshaft bearings are already running metal to metal. Where
the line runs is set out in
[can you drive with engine knock](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-engine-knock/).

## Why the pressure falls

Work through the causes from cheap to expensive:

- **a low oil level** — consumption between changes, leaks past seals and
  gaskets; the pick-up gulps air, especially in corners;
- **oil of the wrong viscosity or past its life** — too thin when hot to
  build resistance in the clearances;
- **a clogged pick-up screen** — deposits from old oil choke the pump at
  its inlet; typical of engines with infrequent changes;
- **a worn oil pump or a stuck pressure relief valve** — the pump delivers
  less than specified or bleeds the pressure away;
- **enlarged bearing clearances** — worn bearings drain the pressure and
  the distant consumers such as the lifters suffer;
- **a faulty sensor or wiring** — the light is lying; a pleasant theory,
  but only acceptable after a gauge reading.

Conditions contribute too. Town driving with warm-ups and traffic ages oil
faster than motorway use, and summer heat adds temperature: oil that would
honestly see out its interval in a mild climate gives up earlier in a
yard–traffic–car park cycle. A shortened change interval is the cheapest
prevention for every pressure problem.

## What to do when the light comes on

The algorithm is short. Stop somewhere safe and switch the engine off —
not “drive home”, but immediately. Check the level on the dipstick: if it
is below minimum, top up and see whether the light goes out after
restarting. If it does, drive carefully to a workshop and find where the
oil is going. If it does not, switch off and call a tow truck: a few
minutes of running without pressure costs more than any transport. A light
that flickers only in corners or under braking almost always means a low
level: the oil is sloshing away from the pick-up.

A separate warning about topping up on the road: if the light comes on far
from home and all you have is oil of a different viscosity, adding it is
acceptable — it is the lesser evil compared with running low. But change
that mixture in full at the first opportunity and find out where the oil
went.

If a dull knock has joined the light, the situation is already in the red
zone, described in
[noises that mean stop driving now](/en/articles/noises-that-mean-stop-driving-now/).

## How it is checked and what to insist on

The main diagnostic tool is a mechanical gauge fitted in place of the
sensor: it answers immediately whether the pressure is genuinely low or
the electrics are lying. Two things are worth asking for. That the reading
is taken both at idle and at raised revs on a fully warm engine — a
pressure that is fine cold and collapses hot is exactly the case a single
cold measurement misses. And that the oil filter and the oil itself are
inspected: metallic glitter in the old oil changes the conversation from
“which part” to “how much of the engine”.

The economics are typical for an engine: the level and fresh oil cost
almost nothing, a pump costs a great deal more, and the consequences of
running without pressure cost more again. So at the first signs a
measurement is better value than waiting. And the most underrated habit on
this list is free: check the dipstick every week or two and before every
long trip. Most engines killed by oil starvation signalled the problem
long before the light — nobody pulled the dipstick.
