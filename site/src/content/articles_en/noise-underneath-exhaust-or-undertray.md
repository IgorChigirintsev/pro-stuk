Metallic noise from below is a genre with almost always two suspects: the
exhaust system and the undertray — the steel or composite panel under the
engine. They sound similar — rattling, clattering, a dull knock over
bumps — but their origins differ, and they can be separated without a
lift, in one drive and a couple of minutes in a car park. Here is what
each source reacts to and what to do next.

## Why they sound alike

Both the undertray and the exhaust are large metal parts hung under the
body at a few mounting points. While the fasteners are sound, both are
rigidly fixed and silent. As soon as free movement appears — a stripped
thread, a split exhaust hanger rubber, a broken undertray screw — the part
starts touching the body or its neighbours. Hence the shared character of
the sound: not a ringing metal-on-metal note but a dull clatter with a
“something is loose” quality.

The difference is in what makes the part move. The undertray is shaken by
road bumps — pure suspension and body mechanics. The exhaust is shaken not
only by bumps but by engine vibration: the manifold is rigidly attached to
the cylinder head, and any change of revs travels down the whole system to
the silencer.

## The checks that settle it

1. **The idle test.** Start the engine and, from neutral, raise the revs
   smoothly to 2,500–3,000. If the rattle appears or changes tone, it is
   the exhaust or a heat shield. Silence means almost certainly the
   undertray.
2. **The rocking test.** With the car parked, rock the body by a wing,
   then slam a door. A clatter means a loose panel or a component hanging
   on a broken fixing.
3. **The hands test.** On a fully cold car, get underneath and tug in turn
   at the silencer, the flex pipe, the shields and the edge of the
   undertray. More than a centimetre of movement anywhere is already a
   cause.
4. **The surface test.** Fine washboard surfaces bring out the undertray;
   smooth tarmac under load brings out the exhaust.

## The differences

| Sign | Undertray | Exhaust system |
|---|---|---|
| Reaction to revs while parked | None | Yes, the sound follows the revs |
| Reaction to bumps | Strong, the main trigger | Moderate |
| Character of the sound | Dull clatter, panel rattle | Ringing, rattling, sometimes a roar or pops |
| Reaction to a slammed door | Often responds | Usually not |
| Accompanying signs | Impact marks on the panel, torn-out fixings | Exhaust smell in the cabin, a louder exhaust |

Heat shields deserve their own row. Formally part of the exhaust system,
they behave by its rules but cost almost nothing to fix: a shield is
secured with a clamp or a large washer. Before agreeing to a new silencer,
ask for them to be checked —
[finding a rattling heat shield](/en/articles/finding-a-rattling-heat-shield/)
covers how. And a roar that appeared suddenly with vibration at idle
points at
[a torn exhaust flex pipe](/en/articles/signs-of-a-torn-exhaust-flex-pipe/).

## How the repair is decided

Undertray fixings are consumables: clips and screws cost very little, and
almost any workshop supplies them as part of a tightening job. The
question worth asking is whether the mounting point itself is intact — a
fixing that has torn out of a bracket needs a repair washer or a new
bracket, not one more screw through the remaining hole.

On the exhaust side, ask whether the hangers were checked: a system that
sags on perished rubber rattles against the body no matter how well the
clamps are tightened, and it also shortens the life of the next silencer.

If the rattle turns out to be neither the undertray nor the exhaust, the
circle of suspects moves to the accessories — where to start there is in
[whine under the bonnet: alternator or water pump](/en/articles/alternator-or-water-pump-whine/).
