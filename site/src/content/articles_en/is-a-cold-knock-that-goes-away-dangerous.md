A knock at a cold start that disappears after a few seconds or minutes is
the most common source of doubt: it knocks, but then everything is fine,
so surely it can be left alone. Sometimes that is genuinely true and the
sound is explained by the physics of warming up. Sometimes wear is behind
it that only shows up cold for now, and in six months will stop going away
at all. The difference between the two is read from the timing and the
character of the sound.

## Why an engine knocks when cold at all

Three mechanisms account for most cases. First, thermal clearances: parts
made of different metals expand at different rates, and in a cold engine
the clearances are larger than designed. Second, oil: while it is thick,
the pump takes longer to fill the galleries, and the hydraulic lifters and
the chain tensioner run for a while without the pressure they need. Third,
condensation and fuel residue on the cylinder walls, which change the
combustion conditions in the first seconds.

All three stop applying as the engine warms, so the mere fact that the
sound disappears proves nothing: both a healthy engine and an early stage
of wear behave that way. The evidence is in the details.

The logic is simple: a worn component runs with an enlarged clearance when
cold and knocks; once warm, thermal expansion takes the clearance up and
it goes quiet. The engine really does drive normally, but the wear has not
gone anywhere — it is only temporarily compensated. The greater the wear,
the longer the sound lasts, and once the clearance is large enough,
warming up no longer covers it.

## What to read: duration, character, trend

| Sign | More likely normal | More likely wear |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | 2–5 seconds after starting | Tens of seconds or minutes |
| Character | Light ticking, rustling from the top | Rattle, clatter, dull thumps |
| Seasonality | Only in hard frost | All year round |
| Trend over six months | Unchanged | Louder and longer |
| Reaction to an oil change | Noticeably quieter | No change |
| Accompanying signs | None | Camshaft faults, wandering idle |

The most important row here is the trend. A stable sound that has been the
same for years is rarely a problem. A sound that slowly takes up more time
and grows coarser is wear giving you advance notice.

## Three common sources

Hydraulic lifters are the most harmless: ticking from the top of the
engine that fades as oil pressure builds. It is often solved with fresh
oil of the correct viscosity — the cold-start version is covered in
[hydraulic lifters ticking when cold](/en/articles/hydraulic-lifters-ticking-when-cold/).
If that does not help, the lifters themselves are replaced.

The timing drive needs attention: rattling or metallic rustling at the
front of the engine in the first seconds says the tensioner is not holding
a slack chain. How to listen for it is in
[checking timing chain tension by ear](/en/articles/checking-timing-chain-tension-by-ear/),
and why it should not be postponed in
[a broken timing chain: consequences by engine](/en/articles/timing-chain-failure-consequences-by-engine/) —
because from here the scenario does not develop gradually.

The pistons and bearings are the case where a dull cold knock means worn
clearances. The quiet stage can last a long time, but it ends in a rebuild.
The cold-start sound picture as a whole is on the symptom page
[engine knock when cold](/en/symptoms/engine-knock-when-cold/).

## A sensible plan

If the sound fits into a few seconds, has not changed for months and comes
with no fault codes — watch it and check the oil viscosity at the next
change. If it has stretched to tens of seconds, become coarser or appeared
for the first time recently — a diagnostic scan and an inspection by an
engine specialist. A separate urgency marker: if the knock has stopped
going away after warm-up, the “warning” stage is over.

One thing worth asking when you do go in: what oil is in the engine and
whether it matches the manufacturer's specification, not just the
viscosity grade. Modern lifters and chain tensioners are sensitive to the
oil's specification, and a knock that appeared right after a cheap oil
change is a story workshops see regularly.

A useful habit at any stage is to check the car systematically rather than
only by the loud symptom: wheel bearings and play, for instance, can be
diagnosed yourself in ten minutes, as described in
[how to check a wheel bearing yourself](/en/articles/how-to-check-a-wheel-bearing-yourself/)
and
[checking hub play with the wheel raised](/en/articles/checking-wheel-bearing-play-with-the-wheel-raised/).
