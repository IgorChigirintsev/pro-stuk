The alternator and the water pump are neighbours on the accessory belt,
and both whine in the same dull way: an even low sound that grows with
revs and barely cares whether the car is moving. Getting the diagnosis
wrong costs differently in each direction: mistaking the pump for the
alternator is the expensive mistake, because a seized pump can take the
timing belt with it. Here is how to separate the two with simple checks.

## Why the sound comes out the same

The same thing whines in both units — the bearing the shaft turns on. Worn
raceways produce a monotonous low hum whose frequency follows crankshaft
speed through the pulley ratio. Both units are bolted to the engine block
and pass vibration into the metal, so by ear the sound seems to come from
everywhere at once.

The key difference is in the physics of the load. An alternator is loaded
electrically: the more current the consumers ask for, the greater the
magnetic resistance on the shaft and the harder the bearing works. A water
pump moves coolant and does not react to electrics at all — but its
bearing sits next to a seal, and the two usually die together, leaving
damp traces behind.

## The checks that separate them

1. **Electrical load.** At a warm idle, switch on main beam, the screen
   heater and the blower at maximum, and listen for a change. If the whine
   grows and drops in pitch, it is the alternator.
2. **Look for leaks.** With the engine cold, inspect the pump housing and
   the area below it. Streaks, coolant residue, damp dust — practically a
   verdict on the pump even without the other signs.
3. **Pulley play.** With the belt off, rock each pulley across its axis by
   hand and spin it. Crunching, noticeable movement or catching points at
   a specific unit.
4. **A stethoscope.** A mechanic's stethoscope, or a long screwdriver held
   against a housing, localises the source more reliably than anything —
   it is where a workshop starts.

The belt itself and the idlers are worth ruling out separately: their
sound is more of a squeal than a hum. The neighbouring cases are covered
on the symptom page [belt squeal](/en/symptoms/belt-squeal/).

## Side by side

| Sign | Alternator | Water pump |
|---|---|---|
| Reaction to lights and heaters | The whine grows | No reaction |
| Fluid traces | None | Coolant drips, residue |
| What comes with it | Battery light, dim lights | Overheating, temperature swings |
| How it develops | The whine turns into a howl | The whine turns into grinding, play appears |
| Risk if it fails | A flat battery | A thrown belt, overheating, engine damage |

The risk row is worth reading twice. It is what decides whether the repair
can wait: an alternator gives you time and warns you with a light, while a
pump fails without a prelude.

## How the repair is decided

The deciding question is what drives the pump on your engine. If the
timing belt does, and the mileage is anywhere near the scheduled belt
change, the pump is replaced along with it as a matter of course: taking
the same assembly apart a second time costs more than the part. If the
accessory belt drives it, the job stands on its own.

For the alternator there is a similar fork worth asking about: whether the
bearings can be replaced or the whole unit is being sold to you. On many
alternators a rebuild is a legitimate option, and so is replacing the
one-way pulley alone — that pulley is a common source of noise by itself
and is much cheaper than the alternator.

Whichever it turns out to be, the accessory belt is replaced whenever it
comes off after long service. It is a small part next to the labour
already being paid for.
