A knock that comes straight into your palms on a broken road is one of the
commonest reasons to book a diagnosis. The good news is that the circle of
suspects is short: there are only a few parts between the driver's hands
and the wheel, and the source is almost always among them. The key is to
separate genuine feedback into the hands from a knock that is merely heard
at the front: those are two different lists of causes. Here are the
culprits by frequency and the checks that need no lift.

## Why the knock reaches your hands

The steering wheel is connected to the road wheels almost rigidly. The
steering shaft turns the rack pinion, the rack moves the tie rods, and the
rods turn the hubs through the track rod ends. There is a minimum of
rubber in that chain to absorb impacts, so any play — an enlarged
clearance in a joint or in the rack's mesh — turns into an impact on
potholes that reaches your palms almost undiminished.

Suspension parts are arranged differently: anti-roll bar links and bushes,
ball joints and arm bushes are mounted through rubber and knock into the
body. That knock is heard under the car or felt through the floor, while
the wheel stays calm. Hence the first question of self-diagnosis: do your
hands feel the impact, or does the sound simply arrive from the front? If
the latter, start the search on the symptom page
[knocking in the suspension](/en/symptoms/knocking-in-suspension/), and
the general breakdown of road knocks is on
[knocking over bumps](/en/symptoms/knocking-over-bumps/).

## The main causes by frequency

| Part | Character of the knock | Companion signs |
|---|---|---|
| Track rod ends | Frequent fine chatter on small bumps | Steering play, feedback in the palms |
| Steering rack | Single dull impact on potholes and joints | Repeats when the wheel is rocked while parked |
| Tie rods | Similar to the ends, slightly "deeper" | The car pulls, uneven tyre wear |
| Anti-roll bar links | Chattering knock on washboard and speed humps | Barely reaches the wheel |
| Strut top mount bearings | Knock and crunch when the wheel turns | Heard from above, at the strut tower |

Statistically the joints are most often to blame — the ends and the rods:
they take the impacts from the wheels first and work in the harshest
environment, next to dirt and water. The rack is second by frequency but
first by the cost of getting it wrong: repairing it costs several times
more than replacing an end, so it matters not to miss the diagnosis. How
to tell the rack from the joints specifically is covered in
[a steering rack knock: how to identify it](/en/articles/how-to-identify-a-steering-rack-knock/).

The weather offers a useful observation too. Rubber bushes stiffen in
frost, so in winter the anti-roll bar knocks sound louder and drier, while
play in the steering joints barely depends on temperature. A sound that
grew sharply with the cold argues for the suspension rubber rather than
the rack or the ends —
[suspension knock in cold weather](/en/articles/suspension-knock-in-cold-weather/).

## Narrowing it down in ten minutes

1. **Rocking the wheel while parked.** Start the engine and rock the wheel
   left and right through 10–15 degrees in short movements. A helper puts
   a hand first on the track rod end, then on the tie rod, then on the rack
   housing: the impact is felt through the palm in exactly the part that
   has play.
2. **Locating the sound.** A knock "in the wheel itself" and in the column
   means the rack or the steering shaft universal joint; a knock from
   below, at the wheel, means an end or a rod; a crunch from above, under
   the bonnet at the strut tower, means the top mount bearing.
3. **Checking the play.** With the car parked, free movement at the rim of
   more than two or three centimetres is a sign of worn joints or
   clearance in the rack.
4. **Checking with the wheel raised.** If you have a jack, raise a front
   wheel and rock it by hand at 9 and 3 o'clock. Play and knocking in that
   plane means the steering joints; play at 6 and 12 points at the hub or
   the ball joint —
   [checking track rod ends for play](/en/articles/checking-track-rod-ends-for-play/).

A separate case is a knock that appears not on potholes but as the wheel
is turned on the move: that has a different list of causes, from the CV
joint to the ball joint. It is covered in
[a knock when steering on the move](/en/articles/knock-when-steering-on-the-move/).

## How the repair is decided

The cheapest part on the list is the anti-roll bar link, the most
expensive is the rack — so locating the source precisely is worth more
than replacing parts in turn and hoping. If a workshop proposes replacing
several parts at once "as prevention", ask to be shown the play in each of
them on the lift; conscientious mechanics do that without objection.

Two things worth planning for. An alignment after replacing ends or rods
is compulsory, not optional: without it a new part eats a set of tyres
quickly. And on the rack, ask whether the adjuster still has travel before
agreeing to an adjustment — one already at its limit means the answer is a
rebuild, and paying for the adjustment first is money spent twice.

## The cost of postponing

A joint with play wears in an avalanche: every impact enlarges the
clearance, and the enlarged clearance makes the impacts harder. The
extreme scenario for an end or a rod is the joint separating, after which
the wheel stops obeying the steering. In practice that is rare, but it is
exactly why noticeable play in the steering is one of the faults that make
a car unroadworthy.

With the rack the scenario is less dramatic but expensive: the clearance
in the mesh will not shrink by itself, worn bushes gradually finish off the
bar and the housing, and yesterday's cheap adjustment turns into a rebuild
or a replacement. There is a domestic argument too — tyres: a car with
play in the steering wears its tread noticeably faster than it should, and
a set of tyres can cost more than the repair itself.
