The clutch transmits torque from the engine to the gearbox and breaks that
link for gear changes. It wears slowly and linearly, so the driver gets
used to the changes and notices the problem when the disc is nearly gone.
The good news: a dying clutch gives clear signals thousands of kilometres
before it fails completely, and almost all of them can be checked without
a workshop. Below are the signs by stage, a one-minute test, and how the
replacement is decided.

## The first signs

Three early signals that appear long before serious trouble:

- **The bite point has crept up.** The car starts moving at the very end
  of the pedal's travel. The friction material on the disc has thinned —
  the main indicator of remaining life.
- **A burning smell after pulling away uphill** or with some slip. The
  specific smell of scorched friction material is unmistakable — the
  clutch has overheated.
- **Revs rising faster than speed.** Press the throttle hard in a high
  gear and the tachometer jumps while the acceleration does not match —
  the disc is slipping under load.

## The stages of wear

| Stage | What you notice | How much further |
|---|---|---|
| Early | Bite point above mid-travel | Thousands of km, watch |
| Middle | Smell under load, slipping when overtaking | Plan the replacement |
| Late | Slips in any gear, vibration when pulling away | Replace without delay |
| Critical | Revs are there, the car barely moves | Tow truck, or straight to a workshop |

How fast the stages follow one another depends on driving style: in town
traffic the late stage arrives quickly, because an overheated disc wears
in an avalanche.

## The one-minute slip test

The classic check needs nothing but a level surface: apply the handbrake,
start the engine, select third or fourth gear and release the clutch pedal
gently while holding a little throttle. A healthy clutch locks the engine
to the braked wheels and the engine stalls almost at once. If the engine
keeps running and the car does not even try to lurch, the disc is
slipping.

The second version is on the move: on a motorway in top gear at 60–70
km/h, press the throttle sharply. Revs rising without matching
acceleration is the same slip.

## Other signs: jerking, vibration, sounds

**Jerking when pulling away.** The car shudders as you release the
pedal — that is an oil-contaminated disc (a leaking seal), worn damper
springs or warping from overheating.

**Vibration.** Shaking when pulling away under load is typical of the
clutch. Shaking with the car stationary in neutral, however, is usually
not clutch-related — more often engine mounts or ignition; see the symptom
page [vibration at idle](/en/symptoms/vibration-at-idle/).

**Sounds.** The clutch is convenient to diagnose with the pedal. A rustle
or hum that appears with the pedal pressed is almost always the release
bearing, covered in
[signs of a worn clutch release bearing](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-clutch-release-bearing/)
and
[a whine with the clutch pressed](/en/articles/whine-with-the-clutch-pressed/).
Noise in neutral that disappears when you press is the gearbox input shaft
bearing. A sound that ignores the pedal entirely comes from the engine.
Clatter at startup and shutdown belongs to the flywheel —
[signs of a failing dual-mass flywheel](/en/articles/signs-of-a-failing-dual-mass-flywheel/).
A metallic knock on sharp throttle is usually not the clutch either — see
[knocking when accelerating](/en/symptoms/knocking-when-accelerating/).

## What the pedal itself tells you

Pedal effort is a separate source of information worth tracking:

- **The pedal has become noticeably heavier.** The pressure plate's
  diaphragm fingers are worn or deformed — the unit is close to
  replacement.
- **The pedal has become suspiciously light or sinks.** On cars with
  hydraulic clutch actuation that is more often the master or slave
  cylinder, or a fluid leak — the clutch itself may be fine and the repair
  noticeably cheaper.
- **The pedal does not return fully, or creaks.** Wear in the fork, the
  cable or the pedal box bushes.

Telling these apart before the workshop visit matters: “slipping plus a
heavy pedal” and “the pedal sank” lead a mechanic to different parts and
different bills, though both sound like “something with the clutch”.

## What kills a clutch early

Its life is decided not by mileage but by the number and quality of
pull-aways. The main accelerators: launches at high revs with wheelspin,
resting a foot on the pedal while driving (the release bearing and disc
work constantly), holding the car on a hill with the clutch instead of the
handbrake, towing trailers and other cars, and labouring at low revs. One
long, overheating hill start can take more life out of it than a month of
calm driving.

## How the replacement is decided

The labour of removing the gearbox is the bulk of the cost, and that
single fact drives everything.

It is replaced as a kit: pressure plate, disc and release bearing.
Economising by keeping the old release bearing or cover means paying the
same labour again. While the box is out, the rear main seal and the
gearbox input shaft seal are reachable — ask whether they are in the
quote, because an oil leak onto a fresh disc is the classic reason a good
job fails within a year.

Two more questions. Whether the flywheel will be measured or resurfaced:
on a single-mass flywheel the surface can often be machined, on a
dual-mass one the free play is measured and the number decides. And
whether the release mechanism — fork, pivot, and on hydraulic systems the
slave cylinder — is being replaced or reused; a worn fork pivot makes a
new clutch feel exactly like the old one.

That is exactly why delay is expensive: while only the kit is involved,
the bill is one thing; once overheating has reached the flywheel, it is
another.
