A whine is a tonal sound whose pitch rises with the revs. It differs from
a squeal (sharper, tied to belt slipping) and from a hum (lower and
steadier). Appearing under acceleration, a whine says that some rotating
part is struggling under load. The task of the diagnosis is to work out
which one: there are about a dozen candidates, but simple tests narrow the
list to one or two in a few minutes.

## The first test: engine or transmission

Before working through the parts under the bonnet, it is worth ruling out
the gearbox. Park on level ground, apply the brake and raise the revs
smoothly in neutral to 2500–3000.

The whine is audible while parked — the source rotates with the engine:
the accessories, the timing drive or the engine's internals. The whine
only appears on the move and changes with gear or on lift-off — the
gearbox, the final drive or transmission bearings are more likely.

That test takes a minute but saves hours: half of all "whining engines"
turn out to be the transmission. A special case is a CVT: an even whine
under acceleration is partly standard for one, but volume that grows from
drive to drive is a reason to check the level and condition of the
gearbox fluid. If the whine accompanies the car at a steady speed too, it
is worth studying the symptom page
[humming while driving](/en/symptoms/humming-while-driving/) — it covers
wheel bearings and the final drive, which drivers also often take for
engine whine. The full method is in
[the neutral throttle test: what it shows](/en/articles/the-neutral-throttle-blip-test/).

## The accessories: the main suspects

If the whine reproduces while parked, start with the parts the drive belt
turns.

- **The alternator.** Its rotor turns two or three times faster than the
  crankshaft, so worn alternator bearings whine first. The sound is thin
  and grows noticeably with the headlights and heaters on —
  [signs of a worn alternator bearing](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-alternator-bearing/).
- **The power steering pump.** It whines when short of fluid or worn: the
  sound changes sharply as the wheel is turned to full lock. Cars with
  electric assistance do not have this part —
  [power steering whine when turning](/en/articles/power-steering-whine-when-turning/).
- **Idlers and the belt tensioner.** A dried-out idler bearing gives a
  whine that turns into grinding; the sound depends on neither the
  steering nor the electrical load —
  [signs of a worn idler pulley](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-idler-pulley/).
- **The air conditioning compressor.** Checked with a button: the whine
  appears or grows when the a/c comes on and the candidate is found —
  [a/c compressor clutch failure sounds](/en/articles/ac-compressor-clutch-failure-sounds/).

Accessory whine has a typical progression: at first it only appears under
load during acceleration, a few weeks later it is audible at any raised
revs, then constantly. The earlier the sound is caught, the cheaper the
repair: at the first stage one bearing or idler is usually enough, at the
last the belt and neighbouring parts often suffer too. At idle those same
parts give a lower sound, covered separately in
[humming under the bonnet at idle](/en/articles/humming-under-the-bonnet-at-idle/).

## The water pump and the timing drive

Two sources are more serious. **The water pump** — the coolant pump: its
worn bearing whines under load, and coolant weeps near the pulley. Do not
drag it out — a water pump breaking up leads to engine overheating,
[signs of a failing water pump](/en/articles/signs-of-a-failing-water-pump/).

**The timing chain** rarely whines, but a stretched chain with worn guides
gives a sound between a rustle and a whine under acceleration, coming from
the front of the engine. The distinguishing detail is a metallic rustle at
idle accompanying it. How it sounds and what it is confused with is in
[timing chain rustle: what it sounds like](/en/articles/timing-chain-rustle-what-it-sounds-like/).
If what you hear under acceleration is not a whine but a ringing metallic
note, that is a different symptom —
[pinging when accelerating](/en/symptoms/pinging-when-accelerating/),
usually connected with detonation.

## Narrowing the field

| Behaviour of the whine | Likely source |
|---|---|
| Present while parked, grows with lights and heaters | Alternator bearings |
| Changes as the wheel is turned | Power steering pump |
| Appears when the a/c is switched on | Air conditioning compressor |
| Independent of steering and load, present while parked | Idler, tensioner or water pump |
| Only on the move, depends on the gear | Gearbox or transmission |
| Whine with a metallic rustle from the front of the engine | Timing chain, guides |

## How the diagnosis is done and what decides the repair

A whine is localised with a stethoscope, holding the probe against bearing
housings with the engine running. The second technique is a brief run with
the drive belt removed: if the whine has gone, the accessories are to
blame; if it remains, look in the timing drive or inside the engine.
Removing the belt yourself is only sensible if you understand the routing:
on many engines it drives the water pump, and running without it for long
is not acceptable.

Before the workshop visit it helps to prepare three facts: at what revs
the whine appears, whether it reacts to the steering, the air conditioning
and electrical load, and whether it is audible while parked. Those answers
cut the search from an hour to ten minutes — the mechanic knows
immediately which parts to listen to. If the whine only arises under load
on the move, the workshop will run the car on a lift or ask for a short
drive together.

Two things worth knowing before agreeing to parts. Whichever bearing is
found, the belt and the idlers have comparable service lives, so replacing
them together costs almost nothing extra in labour — the belt is already
off. And if the water pump is the suspect and it sits in the timing drive,
its replacement is scheduled with the timing kit rather than on its own,
because reaching it twice means paying the same labour twice.
