On a winter morning an automatic behaves differently: the gearbox hums at
idle, shifts are felt as jolts, a dull thump is heard when pulling away,
and a few kilometres later all of it quietly goes. For an owner that is an
annual cause for concern — and in most cases the concern is excessive.
Here is which cold sounds are explained by the physics of the fluid and
which remain a symptom after warm-up.

## Why a cold gearbox sounds louder

An automatic transmission works on fluid that simultaneously transmits
pressure, lubricates and cools. Below freezing that fluid becomes
noticeably thicker. The pump has to force a viscous liquid through the
narrow channels of the valve body — hence the higher pitch of the hum and
the increased noise at idle. Pressure builds more slowly, the clutches
engage less smoothly, and shifts feel harsher.

The second factor is thermal clearances. Metal parts are contracted more
in the cold than in their working range, and some joints have slightly
more play. So a single dull thump when moving the selector from P to D is
more noticeable in winter than in summer. As things warm the fluid thins,
the clearances reach their design values, and the unit goes quiet.

## What counts as normal and what does not

| Sound | Normal if | Worrying if |
|---|---|---|
| Hum at idle | Gone within 5–10 minutes of driving | Persists when warm, grows with speed |
| Thump on P → D | Single, quiet | A hard thump with a delay before engagement |
| Harsh shifts | Only the first kilometres | Persist on a hot gearbox |
| Whine on acceleration | No — there should be no whine | Any steady whine under load |
| Revs slipping | No | Revs rise but the car does not accelerate |

The key criterion is warm-up. Everything that goes with temperature
belongs to fluid viscosity. Everything that remains on a hot gearbox
belongs to the condition of the unit and calls for a diagnosis. The second
criterion is the trend: a sound that has become noticeably louder this
winter than last is worth checking even if it still goes after warm-up —
the full symptom list is in
[signs of a failing automatic transmission](/en/articles/signs-of-a-failing-automatic-transmission/),
and the jolting case in
[automatic gearbox jolts and clunks when shifting](/en/articles/automatic-gearbox-jolts-and-clunks-when-shifting/).

## Not confusing the gearbox with its neighbours

In winter a great deal gets louder, and some sounds are wrongly attributed
to the automatic. The suspension is stiffer in frost: the rubber bushes
have hardened, and a rattle over the first kilometres is usually the
anti-roll bar rather than the gearbox; how to check that is covered in
[checking anti-roll bar links for knocking](/en/articles/checking-anti-roll-bar-links-for-knocking/)
and
[suspension knock in cold weather](/en/articles/suspension-knock-in-cold-weather/).
A hum that depends on speed rather than gear is most often a bearing — its
portrait is in
[what a failing wheel bearing sounds like](/en/articles/what-a-failing-wheel-bearing-sounds-like/).
And shaking with a low hum at idle in gear at a traffic light is covered
on the symptom page
[vibration at idle](/en/symptoms/vibration-at-idle/).

A simple check takes one drive: if the noise is the same at any speed and
changes only with engine revs, look towards the power unit; if it grows
with speed regardless of gear, look towards the running gear — the method
is set out in
[the sound changes when you select neutral](/en/articles/the-sound-changes-when-you-select-neutral/).

## What to do and how the decision is made

The practical winter programme is short. Do not push the first few
kilometres: keep the revs calm and avoid hard starts until the fluid has
warmed. Do not leave the gearbox in D with the brake held for long periods
in frost. And once a season check the level and condition of the fluid —
darkened fluid with a burnt smell explains a good half of the "winter"
complaints that in fact stopped being seasonal long ago.

A fluid change does not cure a worn unit, but it often removes some of the
noise and jolting if the fluid has not been changed for a long time. What
decides whether to do it is mileage and the fluid's condition rather than
the calendar — and at high mileage with clutches already worn, a change
can make things worse, which is why the decision follows a diagnosis
rather than preceding it.

Two things mechanics do not always say. The fluid specification matters
more than the brand: the wrong friction modifier produces exactly the
harsh shifts you were trying to cure. And adaptive gearboxes change their
behaviour after a fluid change or an adaptation reset not immediately but
over several hundred kilometres — so a request to come back for a second
check is normal practice, not an attempt to stretch the bill.

If you are unsure whether your sound falls within normal, record two
