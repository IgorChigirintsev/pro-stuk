An exhaust does not get louder suddenly and for no reason: if the car has
started sounding deeper, a leak has appeared somewhere in the system —
the gases are escaping before the silencer, bypassing its baffles.
Sometimes that is a micro-crack at a joint, sometimes a hole burnt right
through a box. The good news is that the source is nearly always found in
ten minutes on a lift, and some of the causes are cheap to cure. Here are
the typical leak points, their sound signatures and what decides the
repair.

## Where leaks usually appear

- **The silencer and resonator.** The boxes rot from the inside through
  condensation and from the outside through road salt. A burn-through
  along a seam or the floor of the box gives a deep rumble, noticeable at
  idle and rising with the revs —
  [signs of a rotten silencer](/en/articles/signs-of-a-rotten-silencer/).
- **The flex pipe.** The flexible corrugated section between the engine
  and the pipe. A torn flex hisses and roars, the sound growing under
  load, often audible from under the bonnet —
  [signs of a torn exhaust flex pipe](/en/articles/signs-of-a-torn-exhaust-flex-pipe/).
- **Joints and gaskets.** Flange connections after the catalytic converter
  and between boxes lose their seal through burnt gaskets and seized
  studs — the sound is sharp and cutting.
- **The exhaust manifold gasket.** A leak right at the engine: chattering
  and roaring from above, louder when cold — that case is covered in
  detail in
  [the exhaust roars when cold](/en/articles/exhaust-roars-when-cold/) and
  [burnt exhaust manifold gasket signs](/en/articles/burnt-exhaust-manifold-gasket-signs/).
- **A cracked downpipe.** Often near the flex or a mounting: the metal
  tires from vibration, especially with tired engine mounts.

A separate signal is popping and banging in the system on lift-off. That
is a different mechanism — fuel burning in the pipe — and it is covered on
the symptom page
[exhaust backfire](/en/symptoms/exhaust-backfire/).

## The sound of a leak and its location

| How it sounds | When it is louder | Likely location |
|---|---|---|
| Deep rumble, a "sports exhaust" note | In all states, rising with revs | Burnt-through silencer or resonator |
| Hissing with a roar | Under load, on acceleration | Flex pipe, cracked downpipe |
| Sharp cutting hiss | Audible at the floor at idle | Flange, joint gasket |
| Chattering from above, louder when cold | First minutes after starting | Exhaust manifold gasket |
| Metallic rattle on top of the rumble | Over bumps and on acceleration | Broken hanger, pipe touching |

If the volume grew at the same time as a rattle over bumps, do not blame
it all on the exhaust: a pipe sagging on torn hangers knocks against the
body, and the running gear gives similar sounds — compare the picture on
the page
[rattling under the car](/en/symptoms/rattling-under-car/) and in
[noise underneath: exhaust or undertray](/en/articles/noise-underneath-exhaust-or-undertray/).
Front suspension knocks have their own causes, from anti-roll bar links to
the arm joint covered in
[signs of a worn ball joint](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-ball-joint/).

## Judging the scale yourself

First, an inspection on a cold car. Go along the system with a torch:
rusty crumbs on the boxes, black sooty marks at the joints (gases always
leave soot where they escape), damp patches of condensation at a seam.
Soot is an almost guaranteed marker of a leak, and it can often be found
without a pit, just by looking underneath with a light.

Second, the palm test. With the engine running and cold, briefly block the
tailpipe with a thick glove for a couple of seconds: with a sealed system
the pressure pushes noticeably against your palm, with a large leak it
barely does, while the hiss from the hole gets stronger.

Third, listen to the pattern. A leak ahead of the silencer makes the sound
louder under load; a leak in the box itself gives an even rumble in all
states. Those observations are worth writing down and telling the
mechanic — they save time on the lift.

## Extending the life of the exhaust system

Every exhaust rots, but the speed of the process is controllable. The main
killer of the system is condensation: water collects in the boxes on every
journey, and if the route is short it does not have time to evaporate. A
car living on ten-minute commutes benefits from a regular half-hour run at
speed — the system heats right through and dries out.

The second factor is road chemicals. Salt eats the boxes from outside, so
washing the underside after winter is prevention rather than cosmetics.
The third is mechanical: intact rubber hangers stop the pipe vibrating and
cracking at the welds, and care over kerbs and deep ruts saves the boxes
from dents, which is where corrosion often starts. None of that costs
anything comparable to a new silencer.

## How the repair is decided

The criterion is the state of the metal around the hole, not the hole
itself. A single burn-through in a sound box is welded; a box thinning
along its seams is replaced, because a patch on tired metal lasts months.
Ask to be shown the surrounding wall rather than the hole — that is the
one thing that decides between a cheap fix and a repeat visit.

Two things mechanics do not always say. If a joint is opened, the gasket
and the studs go back new: seized studs snap on removal, and a joint
reassembled on old hardware leaks again within weeks. And a burn-through
close to the catalytic converter also upsets the oxygen sensor readings,
so fuel consumption rises — which means the sooner the leak is closed, the
cheaper the whole thing, including the fuel.
