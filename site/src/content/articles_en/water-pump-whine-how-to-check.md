A water pump is the cooling system's pump: an impeller on a shaft, turned
by a belt, pushing coolant through the engine and the radiator. When the
shaft bearing wears, a hum or whine appears from the front of the engine
and grows with revs. The difficulty is that the alternator, the idlers and
the air conditioning compressor all turn on the same belt right next to it
— and they sound very similar. Below is how to work out that it really is
the pump, and which checks you can do without taking anything apart.

## Why a pump starts to whine

There are two vulnerable places inside: the shaft bearing and the seal
that separates coolant from that bearing. The typical failure chain looks
like this: ageing coolant loses its anti-corrosion properties, the seal
wears and starts letting fluid into the housing. Coolant washes the grease
out of the bearing — and the bearing speaks up: first a light rustle, then
a clear whine, and in the late stage a howl and shaft play, with the
impeller able to touch the housing.

Three things speed the process up: water or cheap coolant instead of fluid
to specification, an over-tightened drive belt loading the shaft, and
simply age — a pump's nominal life is 60,000–120,000 kilometres depending
on design and servicing.

## Whose whine is it: the pump or its neighbours

The first thing to establish: the whine follows engine revs, not road
speed. It is audible with the car parked, changes when you blip the
throttle in neutral, and does not care whether the car is moving. That is
what separates it from wheel bearing hum, which exists only on the move.

Then the accessory components have to be separated from each other:

| Component | How it usually sounds | Quick check |
|---|---|---|
| Water pump | Even hum or whine, grows with revs | Coolant traces at the weep hole, pulley play |
| Alternator | Hum or whine, often louder with lights and heaters on | Switch loads on: if the noise grows, suspect the alternator |
| Tensioner or idler pulley | Rustle, hum, sometimes a clatter | Listen with a stethoscope, spin it with the belt off |
| Air conditioning compressor | Hum appears or changes when the A/C is switched on | Press the A/C button and compare |
| The belt itself | Squeal, especially in damp weather | The character: a squeal, not a hum |

Squealing is a separate story with its own causes, from wear to a weak
tensioner — it is covered on the symptom page
[belt squeal](/en/symptoms/belt-squeal/).

## Checking the pump yourself

1. **Localise the sound.** A mechanic's stethoscope, or a long screwdriver
   with the handle at your ear and the tip on a housing (carefully, away
   from anything rotating), lets you compare the volume at the pump, the
   alternator and the idlers. Where it is loudest and roughest is the
   source.
2. **Look at the weep hole.** There is a small hole at the bottom of the
   pump housing: coolant comes out of it when the seal starts to give.
   Drips, whitish or rusty streaks, dried coolant traces around it are a
   sure sign the pump is on its way out, even if the whine is still faint.
3. **Check the pulley for play.** With the engine off and cold, rock the
   pump pulley by hand across the shaft. Noticeable play condemns the
   bearing outright.
4. **Remove the belt and spin the pulleys.** The most informative test:
   without the belt each pulley turns on its own. A healthy one is quiet
   and smooth, a worn one rustles, crunches or catches. If the pump is
   driven by the timing belt, leave this test to a workshop: getting the
   timing drive back together wrongly is expensive.
5. **Watch the indirect signs.** Coolant slowly disappearing, a sweetish
   burnt smell under the bonnet, temperature higher than usual in traffic,
   a heater blowing cooler than it should — all are signs of circulation
   problems.

If the whine is audible both moving and parked but refuses to be
localised, compare the picture with the symptom page
[humming while driving](/en/symptoms/humming-while-driving/), which
collects other sources of a similar sound.

## How the repair is decided

The key question is what drives the pump on your engine. If it runs off
the accessory belt, replacement is a self-contained job. If it sits in the
timing drive, the pump is replaced together with the belt and the idlers —
and the reverse is true as well: whenever the timing belt is changed, the
pump goes with it even without symptoms, because taking the same assembly
apart again in twenty thousand kilometres costs more than the part.

Two more things belong in any estimate. Coolant is drained during the job,
so fresh fluid to the manufacturer's specification is part of the work,
not an extra. And the thermostat is often replaced at the same time for
the same reason — it lives in the same circuit and the labour is already
paid for.

## Can you drive with a whining pump

A short trip to a workshop, yes; postponing for months is a bad idea. A
worn bearing degrades at an accelerating pace: the play grows, the
impeller starts touching the housing, the seal gives up entirely. The
worst outcomes are the pump seizing and snapping the belt — on
timing-belt-driven pumps that often means bent valves and a rebuild — and
rapid coolant loss with overheating. Both cost incomparably more than a
planned replacement. On the way to the workshop, keep an eye on the
temperature gauge and do not work the engine hard.
