A dual-mass flywheel is a part most drivers first hear of at the moment it
needs replacing. Its purpose is a noble one: to absorb the pulses with
which a piston engine turns the crankshaft, so that a smooth flow of
torque reaches the gearbox. For that the flywheel is split into two
rotating parts joined by springs and a friction damper. Those springs sag
over time, the internal grease is used up, and the part starts producing a
set of quite recognisable sounds. Here they are in order and by stage.

## Rattling at idle and when switching off

The first sign is a metallic rattle or light ringing from where the engine
meets the gearbox, at idle. It grows on a warm engine and often disappears
if you press the clutch: disconnected from the gearbox, the flywheel stops
being shaken.

The second early marker is a thump or short knock at the moment you switch
the ignition off. As it stops, the crankshaft passes through a resonance,
and tired damper springs collapse with a perceptible clack. On a healthy
unit shutdown is soft and almost silent. The general breakdown of noises
that show up at idle is on the symptom page
[vibration at idle](/en/symptoms/vibration-at-idle/), and the full sound
portrait in
[what a worn dual-mass flywheel sounds like](/en/articles/what-a-worn-dual-mass-flywheel-sounds-like/).

## Vibration when pulling away and at low revs

Next comes the tactile part. Vibration appears when pulling away,
especially releasing the clutch gently on light throttle: the car shakes
as though it is about to stall, although the revs hold. At low revs in a
high gear, hum and shake through the body join in — a healthy damper ate
those pulses, a worn one passes them straight through.

A characteristic marker is that the vibration grows specifically under
load: uphill, with a full car, overtaking in too high a gear.

## Telling the flywheel from its neighbours

| Sign | Dual-mass flywheel | Release bearing | Engine mounts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rattle at idle | Present, fades with the clutch pressed | Appears with the clutch pressed | No |
| Reaction to the clutch pedal | The sound goes | The sound arrives | Unchanged |
| Thump when switching off | Characteristic, repeatable | No | A dull nudge is possible |
| Vibration when pulling away | Pronounced | No | Present, but with a body lurch |
| Dependence on road speed | None, only on revs | None | None |

The key divider is the clutch pedal. If the sound appears when you press
it, the question is the release bearing —
[signs of a worn clutch release bearing](/en/articles/signs-of-a-worn-clutch-release-bearing/).
If it fades instead, the flywheel is the suspect. If the pedal changes
nothing while the engine visibly nods as you select a gear, start with the
mounts —
[signs of worn engine mounts](/en/articles/signs-of-worn-engine-mounts/).

Remember too that some “engine area” noises do not come from inside at
all: the accessory belt and its idlers have their own repertoire, covered
in
[can you drive with a squealing belt](/en/articles/can-you-drive-with-a-squealing-belt/).

## How the repair is decided

An honest flywheel diagnosis is only possible with the gearbox off: the
free rotation angle between the two masses is measured, along with run-out
and any signs of overheating. Indirect evidence of wear is damper grease
thrown onto the bellhousing. Because of this the flywheel is almost always
replaced together with the clutch: removing the gearbox is the bulk of the
labour, and there is no sense in doing it twice.

Two questions worth asking. Whether the flywheel was measured or judged by
eye — the free angle is a number, and a workshop that gives it is
answering the question. And what else is included while the box is out:
the clutch kit, the release bearing and the rear main seal are all
reachable only now.

The figures involved are serious, which makes it all the more painful to
replace a flywheel when cheap mounts were to blame. So the sensible order
is to rule out the simple things first: mounts, misfires, the release
bearing.
