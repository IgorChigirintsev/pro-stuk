A blocked catalytic converter rarely announces itself with one vivid
symptom. Far more often it chokes the engine bit by bit over months: the
car pulls more lazily, climbs get harder, consumption rises — and the
driver gets used to the new character, blaming it on age. Here is how to
recognise a clogged converter, how it differs from other causes of lost
power, and how the repair is decided.

## What happens inside

A catalytic converter is a ceramic block with thousands of thin channels
coated with precious metals. All the exhaust gases pass through that
honeycomb, and on the hot surface the remaining fuel and harmful compounds
are burnt off. While the channels are clean, the resistance to flow is
small and the engine does not notice it.

Problems begin when extra fuel or oil keeps reaching the exhaust: a rich
mixture from faulty injectors, misfires, oil consumption past worn valve
stem seals. The unburnt residue settles on the channel walls and cokes,
and under severe overheating the ceramic partly melts. The passage
narrows, the exhaust gases are squeezed — and the engine starts working
against a cushion, pushing spent gases into a blocked system instead of
delivering power to the wheels.

## How it feels from the driver's seat

The typical picture combines several signs at once:

- **A flat spot at high revs.** Up to 2,500–3,000 rpm the car drives
  almost normally, but above that it hits an invisible wall: the revs rise
  lazily and the acceleration dies. The more blocked the converter, the
  lower the ceiling.
- **Heavy acceleration under load.** A hill, a full car, an overtake — and
  the engine clearly will not pull, though on a flat road alone it behaves
  reasonably.
- **Overheating around the exhaust.** A burning smell, a darkened or
  glowing pipe in the section ahead of the converter.
- **Hard hot starting** and a tendency to stall at idle after a long
  drive: pressure in the exhaust stops the cylinders scavenging properly.
- **Rising fuel consumption** with no change in driving style.
- **Popping and banging in the exhaust** — unburnt mixture igniting in the
  system. That sound and its relatives are on the symptom page
  [exhaust backfire](/en/symptoms/exhaust-backfire/).

A separate scenario is a metallic rustle or pouring sound under the
floor: that is ceramic which has already started breaking up, described in
[a broken catalytic converter: the sound](/en/articles/broken-catalytic-converter-rattle/).

## What it gets confused with

| Similar symptom | How it differs from a converter |
|---|---|
| A blocked air filter | Power fades more gently, checked in 15 minutes |
| A weak fuel pump | The flat spots are ragged, the car jerks rather than hitting a smooth ceiling |
| A stretched timing chain | Power falls along with chain noise and camshaft codes — [signs of a stretched timing chain](/en/articles/signs-of-a-stretched-timing-chain/) |
| A rotten silencer | Power is unaffected, only loudness changes — [signs of a rotten silencer](/en/articles/signs-of-a-rotten-silencer/) |
| A faulty turbocharger | Whistling, smoke, lost boost with a sound exhaust |

An indirect but telling sign is how the car behaves with the converter
temporarily bypassed during a diagnosis: if power returns at once, little
doubt remains. A more precise assessment comes from measuring exhaust back
pressure: a gauge is fitted in place of an oxygen sensor and the pressure
is watched as the revs rise.

## How the repair is decided

The first step is a diagnostic scan. It shows converter efficiency and
mixture codes and, more importantly, hints at the cause — because a
converter almost never dies by itself but because of a faulty engine. If
oil consumption or misfires are not cured, the new unit repeats the fate
of the old one. That is the question to settle before any part is ordered:
what killed this one.

The second is what goes in its place. An original converter, an
aftermarket one and a universal unit differ in price, in service life and
in whether the rear oxygen sensor will accept them, and emissions testing
rules differ from country to country — so the decision is not made on
money alone. Find out what your inspection requires first.

The third is the oxygen sensors themselves: they live in the exhaust
stream that cooked the converter, and a workshop replacing the converter
without checking them often ends up back at the same fault code.
