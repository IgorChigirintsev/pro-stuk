A caliper is the brake component that presses the pads against the disc
when you press the pedal and should release them when you let go.
“Seized” means it has lost that movement: the piston or the carrier's
slide pins have been gripped by corrosion and dirt. The problem is
insidious because it develops over months and gives no obvious failure for
a long time — the brakes “work, on the whole”. Below are the signs that
identify a seized caliper without a lift, and what servicing involves.

## Why calipers seize

A caliper lives in the worst conditions on the car: water, sand, road salt
and heating to hundreds of degrees. All that protects it are the rubber
boots on the piston and the slide pins. The chain is simple: a boot
cracks, moisture gets in, corrosion follows, the part stops sliding.

Infrequent servicing speeds it up (the slide pins should be cleaned and
greased at every pad change), as does old brake fluid, which has absorbed
water and provokes rust inside the bore, and long periods standing —
after a month parked outside, calipers seize especially readily.

Seasonality plays its part: the typical time to discover the problem is
spring, after a winter on salted roads. Over the cold months corrosion
does its work invisibly, and with the first warm, active drives the seized
part shows itself as a hot wheel and pulling. So a spring brake check at
the seasonal tyre change is not a formality but the cheapest way to catch
the problem at the start.

## The main signs

| Sign | What is happening | What has seized |
|---|---|---|
| Pulls to one side under braking | One caliper is not clamping fully | Piston or slide pins |
| Pulls to one side without braking | A pad is permanently pressed | The piston is not returning |
| One wheel hotter than the rest | A pad rubbing the disc | Piston or slide pins |
| A burning smell from a wheel | An overheated pad | Piston |
| Pads worn tapered or on one side | The carrier is skewed | Slide pins |
| The car coasts worse, consumption up | Constant light braking | Piston |

No sign alone is a verdict, but two or three together are an almost
unambiguous picture. A sticking pad often gives a constant
[squealing brake](/en/symptoms/squealing-brakes/) noise or a rustle
audible even without the pedal, changing as it heats. A metallic sound
under braking is a reason to check pad thickness: a seized caliper eats
them unevenly and fast — see
[grinding when braking](/en/symptoms/grinding-when-braking/) and
[telling brake pad wear by ear](/en/articles/brake-pad-wear-by-ear/).

## How to check it yourself

1. **The temperature test.** After 15–20 minutes of calm driving without
   heavy braking, hold a hand near each wheel (without touching the brake
   disc — it can burn). All the wheels should be similarly warm. One
   noticeably hotter puts that caliper under suspicion.
2. **The coasting test.** On an empty level road, accelerate to 40–50 km/h
   and coast in neutral. If the car slows noticeably faster than it used
   to, or pulls to one side, something is dragging.
3. **Inspecting the pads.** At a seasonal wheel change, compare the pads:
   on a healthy caliper the inner and outer wear at roughly the same rate.
   Tapered wear or a difference of one and a half to two times means
   seized slide pins — the detail is in
   [signs of seized caliper slide pins](/en/articles/seized-caliper-slide-pins-signs/).
4. **Listen after rain.** A light film of rust on the discs after a damp
   night is normal and clears within a couple of stops. But a squeal from
   one wheel that lasts the whole journey is no longer about moisture.

## What ignoring it leads to

A permanently pressed pad overheats the disc, and an overheated disc
warps — judder appears under braking, felt through the wheel and the
pedal, as covered in
[brake judder when braking](/en/articles/brake-judder-when-braking/). The
pads wear out in a few thousand kilometres instead of tens of thousands.

The most dangerous scenario is a long mountain descent: a brake that is
constantly heating boils the fluid, vapour forms in the circuit and the
pedal sinks. That is not a theoretical risk on hilly routes, so a sticking
caliper is one of the faults to fix before a long trip, not after it.

## Front or rear: which seizes more often

Workshop statistics say the rear calipers suffer more often, for two
reasons. First, the rear brakes work less than the front ones — they take
a small share of the braking effort, so they clean themselves less well
and warm up more slowly, and moisture lives in them longer. Second, on
many cars the parking brake mechanism is built into the rear caliper: an
extra assembly with its own mechanics, which infrequent use of the
handbrake only harms. Owners of automatics who go years without touching
the handbrake know the scenario: once applied in frost, it will not let
the wheel go.

The practical conclusion: use the handbrake regularly rather than on high
days and holidays, and ask for the rear calipers to be inspected at every
pad change — even if only the front ones are bothering you.

## How the repair is decided

Restoring costs several times less than replacing, so a decent workshop
first assesses the state of the piston and bore rather than offering a new
unit by default. Ask what the bore looked like: pitted corrosion means
replacement, a sound surface means a rebuild kit.

Two more things. Whether the correct rubber-safe, high-temperature brake
grease is being used on the pins and the piston seal — ordinary chassis
grease destroys the boots and the fault returns within a season. And
whether the brake fluid is due: fluid that has absorbed moisture is what
corrodes the bore from inside, so a caliper rebuild with the old fluid
left in is half a job.

The cheap prevention is asking for the slide pins to be greased at every
pad change: it adds little to the work and postpones seizure by years.
