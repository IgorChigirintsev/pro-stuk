All-wheel drive on the CR-V works on demand: in normal driving the car is
front-wheel drive, and the rear axle is engaged by a coupling when the
front wheels start to slip or the load changes. The unit runs in fluid and
is designed for regular servicing — and it is precisely skipped servicing
that most often stands behind a complaint of whining from the rear. Here
is how to tell this sound from the others and what is done about it.

## What a troubled coupling sounds like

The characteristic picture is a whine or hum that appears during a tight
turn at low speed: turning around in a car park, entering a driveway,
going round a roundabout. Sometimes the sound is joined by a shuddering
feeling, as though something at the back of the car grabs and lets go. In
a straight line and at speed the noise can be almost inaudible.

The logic is simple: in a tight turn the front and rear wheels travel
different distances, and the coupling is forced to slip continuously. If
the fluid has reached the end of its life, the clutch pack works in jerks
— and that is what you hear. The sound portrait of the whole family of
cornering noises is on the symptom page
[humming when turning](/en/symptoms/humming-when-turning/).

## How it differs from other causes

| Source | When you hear it | The distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| Rear coupling | Tight turn at low speed | Whine with shuddering, quieter in a straight line |
| Wheel bearing | Straight line from 40–60 km/h | Changes with the side in gentle curves |
| Rear differential | Constantly at speed | The pitch rises smoothly with speed |
| Outer CV joint | Tight turn under power | Not a hum but clicking |
| Tyres | Everywhere | Changes with the road surface |

The most common confusion is with a wheel bearing. Speed separates them:
a bearing prefers a straight line and becomes noticeable from 40–60 km/h,
while the coupling makes its noise at parking speeds and in tight curves.
If the sound is present in both, there are probably two causes.

## What a workshop does

The first step is almost always to check the condition and level of the
fluid in the rear of the transmission. For couplings of this type the
exact fluid specification is critical — a universal gear oil is not
suitable, and filling with “whatever was on the shelf” is not an option:
the properties are matched to the friction plates, and they decide whether
the unit works smoothly or in jerks. The correct specification is looked
up by model and year, and this is a case where asking twice is
worthwhile.

Then comes an inspection on a lift (play, driveshaft condition, leaks), a
test drive with turns on an open area, and only after that any discussion
of opening the unit. Often it never gets that far: a coupling serviced in
time returns to normal operation.

While the car is on the lift, it is worth assessing the dampers as well —
body movement changes weight distribution and adds work for the
all-wheel-drive system.

## How the repair is decided

Two questions decide the scope. The first is whether the fluid has ever
been changed: on many cars this service is not in the owner's mental
model at all, and a first change at high mileage frequently cures the
noise on its own. The second is what the fluid looks like when it comes
out — dark fluid with a burnt smell and metal particles means the friction
plates have already suffered, and a second change will not save them.

There is one honest caveat worth hearing from a workshop: if the noise
returns within a few thousand kilometres of a correct fluid change, more
fluid will not help. At that point the choice is between opening the unit
and living with the noise, and a shop that says so plainly is more
trustworthy than one that sells a third change.
